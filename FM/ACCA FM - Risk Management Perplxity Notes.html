1. Introduction to Risk Management

Risk management is a crucial area in ACCA Financial Management (FM). It involves identifying, assessing, and prioritizing risks followed by coordinated application of resources to minimize, monitor, and control the probability or impact of unfortunate events.

🎯 What is Financial Risk?

Financial risk refers to the possibility of losing money on an investment or business venture. In the context of ACCA FM, we focus on three main types of financial risk:

  • Foreign Exchange (FX) Risk - Risk from changes in currency exchange rates
  • Interest Rate Risk - Risk from changes in interest rates
  • Credit Risk - Risk that customers won't pay what they owe

Why Manage Risk?

Companies manage risk for several important reasons:

  • To reduce uncertainty - Makes planning and budgeting more reliable
  • To protect cash flows - Ensures the company can meet its obligations
  • To avoid financial distress - Prevents situations where the company might go bankrupt
  • To satisfy stakeholders - Shareholders, lenders, and employees prefer stable returns
  • To comply with regulations - Some industries require risk management

⚠️ Important Exam Point

In the exam, you may be asked to discuss the arguments FOR and AGAINST hedging. Remember:

  • FOR hedging: Reduces uncertainty, protects cash flows, helps with planning, satisfies shareholders
  • AGAINST hedging: Costs money (transaction costs), eliminates upside potential, shareholders can diversify themselves, management might use it for speculation

The Risk Management Process

5 Steps in Risk Management

Step 1: Identify the Risk
What risks does the company face? (e.g., will the dollar weaken against the euro?)
Step 2: Measure the Risk
How big is the risk? What's the potential financial impact?
Step 3: Decide on Strategy
Should we hedge? If yes, what method should we use?
Step 4: Implement the Hedge
Put the hedging strategy into action
Step 5: Monitor and Review
Check if the hedge is working and adjust if needed

2. Foreign Exchange (FX) Risk

Foreign exchange risk arises when a company has cash flows in a foreign currency. If exchange rates change, the value of these cash flows in the home currency will also change.

Types of Foreign Exchange Risk

📊 Three Types of FX Risk

  1. Transaction Risk - Risk on specific future transactions (e.g., you'll receive $100,000 in 3 months)
  2. Translation Risk - Risk when consolidating foreign subsidiary financial statements
  3. Economic Risk - Long-term risk from exchange rate changes affecting competitiveness

In ACCA FM, we focus mainly on transaction risk - the risk on specific, known future transactions.

Understanding Exchange Rates

💡 How to Read Exchange Rates

Exchange rates are quoted in pairs, e.g., USD/EUR 1.2000

  • The first currency (USD) is the base currency
  • The second currency (EUR) is the quote currency
  • This means: 1 USD = 1.2000 EUR

Alternative quote: EUR/USD 0.8333 means 1 EUR = 0.8333 USD

📝 Example 1: Converting Currencies

A UK company expects to receive $500,000 from a US customer in 3 months. The current spot rate is £1 = $1.6000.

Solution:

To convert dollars to pounds, we DIVIDE by the exchange rate:

Amount in £ = $500,000 ÷ 1.6000 = £312,500

Key Rule:
• If you have the quote currency and want the base currency → DIVIDE
• If you have the base currency and want the quote currency → MULTIPLY

Bid and Offer Rates

Banks quote TWO rates - a bid rate and an offer rate. This is crucial for exam calculations!

🏦 Understanding Bid and Offer

  • Bid rate: The rate at which the bank BUYS the base currency (you SELL to the bank)
  • Offer rate: The rate at which the bank SELLS the base currency (you BUY from the bank)
  • The bank always wins! They buy low and sell high

Example quote: USD/EUR 1.1950 - 1.2050

  • Bid: 1.1950 (bank buys USD at this rate)
  • Offer: 1.2050 (bank sells USD at this rate)

📝 Example 2: Using Bid and Offer Rates

A UK company needs to pay €200,000 to a German supplier. The bank quotes: £1 = €1.1500 - 1.1600

Which rate should the company use?

Solution:

Step 1: The company needs to BUY euros (and sell pounds)
Step 2: From the bank's perspective, the bank is SELLING euros (buying pounds)
Step 3: The bank will use the rate that's WORST for the company
Step 4: Calculate both ways:
Using 1.1500: £ = €200,000 ÷ 1.1500 = £173,913 Using 1.1600: £ = €200,000 ÷ 1.1600 = £172,414

Answer: The company will pay £173,913 (the bank uses 1.1500 - the rate that costs the company MORE)

Easy Rule: When converting, calculate both ways and pick the one that's WORST for you (the bank always wins!)

Forward Exchange Rates

A forward exchange rate is an exchange rate agreed TODAY for a transaction that will occur at a specified future date.

Forward Rate = Spot Rate ± Forward Points

Forward points are quoted in "points" where 1 point = 0.0001 of the exchange rate.

📝 Example 3: Calculating Forward Rates

Spot rate: £1 = $1.6000
3-month forward points: 50-70 (premium)
Calculate the 3-month forward rate.

Solution:

Step 1: Convert points to decimal:
50 points = 0.0050
70 points = 0.0070

Step 2: Since points are INCREASING (50 to 70), ADD to spot rate:
Bid forward rate = 1.6000 + 0.0050 = 1.6050 Offer forward rate = 1.6000 + 0.0070 = 1.6070

Forward rate: £1 = $1.6050 - 1.6070

Memory Rule:
• Points going UP (small to big) → ADD to spot
• Points going DOWN (big to small) → SUBTRACT from spot

Money Market Hedge

A money market hedge uses borrowing and lending in different currencies to lock in an exchange rate. This is a popular exam topic!

How to Create a Money Market Hedge

For a Foreign Currency RECEIPT (you'll receive foreign currency):
1. Borrow in foreign currency NOW (present value of the receipt)
2. Convert to home currency at spot rate
3. Deposit home currency until the receipt date
4. When foreign currency receipt arrives, use it to repay the foreign currency loan
For a Foreign Currency PAYMENT (you'll pay foreign currency):
1. Borrow in home currency NOW
2. Convert to foreign currency at spot rate
3. Deposit foreign currency until the payment date
4. When payment is due, use the foreign currency deposit to pay
5. Repay the home currency loan

📝 Example 4: Money Market Hedge for a Receipt

A UK company will receive $500,000 in 6 months.
Spot rate: £1 = $1.6000
6-month deposit rates: £ 4% per year, $ 6% per year
Show how to create a money market hedge.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate how much to borrow in dollars (present value):
6-month $ interest rate = 6% × 6/12 = 3%
Amount to borrow = $500,000 ÷ 1.03 = $485,437

Step 2: Convert to pounds at spot rate:
Amount in £ = $485,437 ÷ 1.6000 = £303,398

Step 3: Deposit pounds for 6 months:
6-month £ interest rate = 4% × 6/12 = 2%
Amount after 6 months = £303,398 × 1.02 = £309,466

Step 4: In 6 months:
• Receive $500,000 from customer
• Use it to repay the $ loan ($485,437 × 1.03 = $500,000)
• Keep the £309,466 from the pound deposit

Result: The company has effectively locked in an exchange rate of:
$500,000 ÷ £309,466 = 1.6157
So the effective forward rate is £1 = $1.6157

💡 Exam Tip: Money Market vs Forward

You may be asked to compare a money market hedge with a forward contract hedge. Always:

  • Calculate the home currency amount under BOTH methods
  • Choose the method that gives you MORE money (for receipts) or costs LESS (for payments)
  • Remember: money market hedges involve more steps but might be cheaper

Leading and Lagging

These are simple techniques for managing transaction exposure:

  • Leading: Paying or receiving early (before the due date)
  • Lagging: Paying or receiving late (after the due date)

When to Lead or Lag?

If you expect the foreign currency to STRENGTHEN:

  • For payments: LEAD (pay early before it gets more expensive)
  • For receipts: LAG (receive late when it's worth more)

If you expect the foreign currency to WEAKEN:

  • For payments: LAG (pay late when it's cheaper)
  • For receipts: LEAD (receive early before it loses value)

📝 Example 5: Leading vs Normal Payment

A UK company owes €100,000 payable in 3 months.
Current spot: £1 = €1.1500
The company expects the euro to strengthen to £1 = €1.1200 in 3 months.
The supplier offers a 2% discount for immediate payment.
Should the company lead (pay now) or wait?

Solution:

Option 1: Lead (pay now with discount)
Amount due now = €100,000 × 0.98 = €98,000 Cost in £ = €98,000 ÷ 1.1500 = £85,217

Option 2: Wait (pay in 3 months)
Expected cost in £ = €100,000 ÷ 1.1200 = £89,286

Decision: Leading saves £89,286 - £85,217 = £4,069
The company should pay early and take the discount!

3. Interest Rate Risk

Interest rate risk is the risk that interest rates will change, affecting a company's interest payments (on loans) or interest income (on deposits).

Types of Interest Rate Risk

📊 Three Types of Interest Rate Risk

  1. Variable Rate Risk: If you have a variable rate loan and rates rise, your interest payments increase
  2. Fixed Rate Risk: If you have a fixed rate loan and rates fall, you're stuck paying more than the market rate
  3. Reinvestment Risk: If you have cash to invest and rates fall, you earn less interest income

Why Interest Rates Change

Interest rates are influenced by many factors:

  • Central bank policy - e.g., Federal Reserve, Bank of England setting base rates
  • Inflation expectations - Higher expected inflation → higher interest rates
  • Economic growth - Strong growth often leads to higher rates
  • Government borrowing - More borrowing can push rates up
  • International factors - Global interest rate movements
Fisher Effect: (1 + nominal rate) = (1 + real rate) × (1 + inflation rate)

Measuring Interest Rate Risk

📝 Example 6: Impact of Rate Change

A company has a $10 million variable rate loan. Current interest rate is 5% per year. The company is worried rates might rise to 7%.

Calculate the additional annual interest cost if rates rise to 7%.

Solution:

Current annual interest = $10m × 5% = $500,000 New annual interest = $10m × 7% = $700,000 Additional cost = $700,000 - $500,000 = $200,000 per year

Or more directly:
Additional cost = $10m × (7% - 5%) = $10m × 2% = $200,000

Interest Rate Forecasts

Companies can use various methods to forecast future interest rates:

  • Forward rate agreements (FRAs) - Market's expectation of future rates
  • Yield curve analysis - Relationship between interest rates and time to maturity
  • Economic models - Based on inflation, growth, etc.
  • Expert opinion - Economists' forecasts

💡 The Yield Curve

The yield curve shows interest rates for different maturities:

  • Normal yield curve: Long-term rates > short-term rates (upward sloping)
  • Inverted yield curve: Long-term rates < short-term rates (downward sloping) - often predicts recession
  • Flat yield curve: All rates similar

4. Hedging Strategies for Interest Rate Risk

There are several methods companies can use to hedge interest rate risk. Each has advantages and disadvantages.

1. Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)

📋 What is an FRA?

A Forward Rate Agreement is an OTC (over-the-counter) contract between two parties to fix an interest rate for a future period.

  • No actual loan is made - it's just a cash settlement
  • If market rate > agreed rate, the seller pays the buyer the difference
  • If market rate < agreed rate, the buyer pays the seller the difference
  • The company still borrows/lends at the market rate separately
FRA Settlement = Principal × (Market Rate - FRA Rate) × (Days/360 or 365)

📝 Example 7: FRA for a Borrower

A company will borrow $5 million in 3 months for 6 months.
Current 6-month rate: 5%
Company buys a 3-9 FRA at 5.5%
In 3 months, the actual 6-month rate is 7%.
Calculate the FRA settlement and effective borrowing cost.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate FRA settlement (company receives payment since market rate > FRA rate):
FRA payment = $5m × (7% - 5.5%) × (6/12) = $5m × 1.5% × 0.5 = $37,500

Step 2: Calculate actual interest on loan:
Interest paid on loan = $5m × 7% × (6/12) = $175,000

Step 3: Calculate net interest cost:
Net interest = $175,000 - $37,500 = $137,500

Step 4: Calculate effective interest rate:
Effective rate = ($137,500 ÷ $5m) × (12/6) × 100% = 5.5%

Result: The FRA has locked in a 5.5% borrowing rate!

📝 Example 8: FRA When Rates Fall

Same company as Example 7, but now the actual rate in 3 months is 4% (below the FRA rate of 5.5%).

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate FRA settlement (company pays since market rate < FRA rate):
FRA payment = $5m × (5.5% - 4%) × (6/12) = $5m × 1.5% × 0.5 = $37,500 (company pays this)

Step 2: Calculate actual interest on loan:
Interest paid on loan = $5m × 4% × (6/12) = $100,000

Step 3: Calculate net interest cost:
Net interest = $100,000 + $37,500 = $137,500

Step 4: Calculate effective interest rate:
Effective rate = ($137,500 ÷ $5m) × (12/6) × 100% = 5.5%

Result: Again, the effective rate is 5.5%. The FRA works both ways - it protects against rate rises but also eliminates benefit from rate falls.

2. Interest Rate Futures

📋 What are Interest Rate Futures?

Interest rate futures are exchange-traded contracts that allow companies to hedge interest rate risk.

  • Standardized contracts (unlike FRAs which are customized)
  • Price = 100 - interest rate (e.g., if rate is 5%, futures price is 95.00)
  • To hedge a loan (protect against rate rises): SELL futures
  • To hedge a deposit (protect against rate falls): BUY futures
  • Number of contracts = Amount to hedge ÷ Contract size
Number of Contracts = (Amount to Hedge ÷ Contract Size) × (Hedge Period ÷ 3 months)

📝 Example 9: Hedging with Interest Rate Futures

A company will borrow $10 million in 2 months for 6 months.
Current interest rate: 5%
Futures price: 95.00 (implying 5% rate)
Contract size: $1 million
Show how to hedge using futures.

Solution:

Step 1: Determine position (borrower wants to protect against rate rises):
SELL futures contracts

Step 2: Calculate number of contracts needed:
Number of contracts = ($10m ÷ $1m) × (6 months ÷ 3 months) = 10 × 2 = 20 contracts

Step 3: Execute the hedge:
SELL 20 futures contracts at 95.00

Step 4: In 2 months, when borrowing:
• If rates have risen to 7%, futures price will fall to 93.00
• Buy back 20 contracts at 93.00
• Profit on futures = 20 × $1m × (95.00 - 93.00) × (3/12) × 1%
• This profit offsets the higher interest cost on the loan

Note: The calculation uses "tick value" which is the value of a 0.01 price movement

3. Interest Rate Options

📋 What are Interest Rate Options?

Interest rate options give the holder the RIGHT (but not the obligation) to borrow or lend at a specified rate.

  • Call option: Right to BUY (protects against rate rises for borrowers)
  • Put option: Right to SELL (protects against rate falls for depositors)
  • You pay an upfront premium for the option
  • If rates move in your favor, you can let the option expire and benefit from the better rate
  • If rates move against you, you exercise the option

📝 Example 10: Interest Rate Call Option

A company will borrow $5 million in 3 months for 6 months.
Current rate: 5%
Company buys a call option with strike rate 5.5%
Premium: 0.5% of the loan amount
In 3 months, the actual rate is 7%.
Should the company exercise the option?

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate premium cost:
Premium = $5m × 0.5% = $25,000

Step 2: Compare rates:
Market rate: 7%
Option strike rate: 5.5%
Since market rate > strike rate, EXERCISE the option

Step 3: Calculate interest cost with option:
Interest at 5.5% = $5m × 5.5% × (6/12) = $137,500 Plus premium = $25,000 Total cost = $162,500

Step 4: Compare with no hedge:
Interest at 7% = $5m × 7% × (6/12) = $175,000

Result: The option saves $175,000 - $162,500 = $12,500

What if rates had fallen to 4%?
The company would NOT exercise the option, borrow at 4%, and only lose the $25,000 premium. This is the advantage of options over FRAs!

4. Interest Rate Swaps

📋 What is an Interest Rate Swap?

An interest rate swap is an agreement between two parties to exchange interest payments on a notional principal amount.

  • Most common: Fixed-for-floating swap
  • Company with variable rate loan pays fixed, receives floating
  • Company with fixed rate loan pays floating, receives fixed
  • The principal amount is NOT exchanged (it's "notional")
  • Swaps are typically for longer terms (3-10 years)

📝 Example 11: Interest Rate Swap

Company A has a $10 million variable rate loan at LIBOR + 1%.
Company A enters a swap to pay fixed 5% and receive LIBOR.
Calculate Company A's net interest rate.

Solution:

Step 1: Company A's cash flows:
• Pays LIBOR + 1% on the loan
• Pays 5% fixed on the swap
• Receives LIBOR on the swap

Step 2: Net position:
Pays: LIBOR + 1% + 5% = LIBOR + 6% Receives: LIBOR Net: (LIBOR + 6%) - LIBOR = 6% fixed

Result: Company A has effectively converted its variable rate loan to a fixed 6% rate!

5. Financial Derivatives - Deep Dive

Derivatives are financial instruments whose value is derived from an underlying asset, rate, or index. Let's explore each type in more detail.

Forwards vs Futures

📊 Key Differences

Feature Forwards (including FRAs) Futures
Trading venue OTC (over-the-counter) Exchange-traded
Customization Fully customizable Standardized
Counterparty risk Yes (private contract) No (exchange guarantees)
Liquidity Low (hard to exit) High (easy to exit)
Regulation Less regulated Highly regulated
Margin No margin required Daily margin calls

Options - Calls and Puts

📋 Option Terminology

  • Call option: Right to BUY the underlying asset
  • Put option: Right to SELL the underlying asset
  • Strike price (exercise price): The price at which you can buy/sell
  • Premium: The price you pay for the option
  • Expiry date: The date by which you must exercise (or let it expire)
  • In the money: Option has intrinsic value (worth exercising)
  • Out of the money: Option has no intrinsic value (don't exercise)

📝 Example 12: Currency Options

A UK company will receive $1 million in 3 months.
Current spot: £1 = $1.6000
Company buys a put option on dollars with strike price £1 = $1.5800
Premium: £5,000
In 3 months, spot rate is £1 = $1.5500.
Should the company exercise the option?

Solution:

Step 1: Understand the option:
Put option on dollars = right to SELL dollars at $1.5800 per £

Step 2: Compare rates:
Strike rate: £1 = $1.5800 (can sell $ at this rate)
Market rate: £1 = $1.5500 (would sell $ at this rate without option)

Step 3: Calculate proceeds:
With option: $1m ÷ 1.5800 = £632,911 Without option: $1m ÷ 1.5500 = £645,161

Wait! Let's think carefully. The company wants to maximize pounds received.
At strike rate: £632,911
At market rate: £645,161

Decision: DON'T exercise! The market rate is better.
Net proceeds = £645,161 - £5,000 (premium) = £640,161

What if market rate was £1 = $1.6000?
Then: Market = $1m ÷ 1.6000 = £625,000
Option = $1m ÷ 1.5800 = £632,911
Exercise! Net = £632,911 - £5,000 = £627,911 (better than £625,000)

Option Strategies

📊 Common Option Strategies

  1. Protective Put: Buy put option to protect against downside (like insurance)
  2. Covered Call: Own asset + sell call option (generates income)
  3. Collar: Buy put + sell call (limits both downside and upside)
  4. Straddle: Buy call + buy put at same strike (profit from big moves either way)

Swaps - More Detail

📋 Types of Swaps

  • Fixed-for-floating interest rate swap: Most common type
  • Currency swap: Exchange principal and interest in different currencies
  • Commodity swap: Exchange fixed price for floating commodity price
  • Credit default swap (CDS): Insurance against credit default

📝 Example 13: Currency Swap

UK Company needs €10 million for 5 years.
US Company needs £8 million for 5 years.
Current spot: £1 = €1.25
Instead of borrowing directly, they swap:
• UK Company borrows £8 million at 4%
• US Company borrows €10 million at 3%
• They swap the principals and interest payments
Explain the benefit.

Solution:

Without swap:
• UK Company borrows €10m directly at (say) 3.5%
• US Company borrows £8m directly at (say) 4.5%

With swap:
• UK Company borrows £8m at 4% (its home currency, lower rate)
• US Company borrows €10m at 3% (its home currency, lower rate)
• They swap: UK gets €10m, US gets £8m
• They pay each other's interest

Benefit: Each company borrows in its home currency where it has better credit rating, then swaps to get the foreign currency it needs. Both save on interest costs!

6. Netting and Matching

Netting and matching are internal techniques for reducing foreign exchange exposure without using external derivatives.

Matching

📋 What is Matching?

Matching means offsetting foreign currency receipts against foreign currency payments in the SAME currency.

  • If you'll receive €500,000 and pay €400,000, only hedge the net €100,000
  • Reduces transaction costs (fewer conversions needed)
  • Simple and effective
  • Only works if you have both receipts and payments in the same currency

📝 Example 14: Matching

A UK company has the following transactions in 3 months:
• Receive €800,000 from German customer
• Pay €600,000 to French supplier
• Receive €300,000 from Italian customer
• Pay €200,000 to Spanish supplier
Calculate the net exposure and explain the hedging strategy.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate total receipts and payments:
Total receipts = €800,000 + €300,000 = €1,100,000 Total payments = €600,000 + €200,000 = €800,000

Step 2: Calculate net position:
Net position = €1,100,000 - €800,000 = €300,000 receipt

Step 3: Hedging strategy:
• Match €800,000 receipts against €800,000 payments internally
• Only hedge the remaining €300,000 net receipt
• This could be done with a forward contract, money market hedge, or option

Benefit: Instead of hedging €1,900,000 total, only hedge €300,000 - much lower transaction costs!

Netting

📋 What is Netting?

Netting is similar to matching but is done at a GROUP level between subsidiaries.

  • Bilateral netting: Between two subsidiaries
  • Multilateral netting: Among multiple subsidiaries
  • A central treasury function coordinates all inter-company payments
  • Only net amounts are actually transferred
  • Reduces foreign exchange exposure and transaction costs

📝 Example 15: Multilateral Netting

A multinational group has four subsidiaries. Inter-company balances are:
• UK owes US $500,000
• US owes Germany €400,000
• Germany owes UK £300,000
• UK owes Germany €200,000
• Germany owes US $300,000
Explain how netting would work.

Solution:

Step 1: Calculate net position for each subsidiary:
UK: Owes $500k + €200k, Receives £300k US: Receives $500k, Owes €400k + $300k Germany: Receives €400k + $300k, Owes £300k + €200k

Step 2: Net off by currency:
USD: UK owes US $500k, Germany owes US $300k US net receives $500k, pays $300k = net receives $200k So UK pays US $200k net EUR: US owes Germany €400k, UK owes Germany €200k Germany net receives €600k So US and UK pay Germany €600k total GBP: Germany owes UK £300k So Germany pays UK £300k

Step 3: Instead of 5 separate transactions, only 3 net payments needed:
• UK → US: $200,000
• US + UK → Germany: €600,000
• Germany → UK: £300,000

Benefit: Fewer transactions, lower FX exposure, reduced bank charges

Centralized Treasury Management

📋 Benefits of Centralized Treasury

  • Better control: Head office monitors all cash and risk
  • Netting opportunities: Can offset positions across the group
  • Expertise: Specialist treasury team manages risk professionally
  • Economies of scale: Larger transactions get better rates
  • Consistent policy: All subsidiaries follow same risk management approach

⚠️ Disadvantages of Centralized Treasury

  • Subsidiary managers may feel they've lost control
  • May not understand local market conditions as well
  • Communication delays
  • Cost of setting up central treasury function

7. Exam Technique & Tips

Here's how to maximize your marks in the risk management section of the FM exam.

Common Question Types

📊 Section A: Objective Test Questions (OTQs)

  • 2-mark calculations (e.g., calculate forward rate, money market hedge outcome)
  • 1-mark theory questions (e.g., "Which of these is a type of FX risk?")
  • Multiple choice, multiple response, or number entry
  • Tip: Practice calculations until they're automatic - you have about 1.8 minutes per mark

📊 Section B: Multi-Task Questions

  • 5 OTQs based on a scenario (10 marks total)
  • Usually 2-3 calculation questions and 2-3 theory questions
  • Tip: Read the scenario carefully, then tackle each question

📊 Section C: Constructed Response (Long Questions)

  • 20-mark questions requiring written answers and calculations
  • Often asks you to "discuss" hedging strategies or "advise" on risk management
  • Tip: Use headings, show all workings, and make specific recommendations

Calculation Tips

💡 Top Calculation Tips

  1. Always show your workings - Even if the final answer is wrong, you get method marks
  2. Label your steps - "Step 1: Calculate PV", "Step 2: Convert at spot rate", etc.
  3. Use the right number of decimals - Usually 4 decimal places for exchange rates, 2 for money
  4. Check your answer makes sense - If you're receiving foreign currency, you should end up with home currency
  5. Time management - Don't spend 10 minutes on a 2-mark calculation. Move on and come back
  6. Practice with past papers - The exam format is predictable, so practice makes perfect

Discussion Question Framework

For "discuss" or "advise" questions, use this structure:

PEEL Method for Discussion Questions

P - Point: Make your point clearly
Example: "The company should consider using forward contracts to hedge its foreign exchange exposure."
E - Explain: Explain what you mean
Example: "Forward contracts lock in an exchange rate today for a future transaction, eliminating uncertainty."
E - Example: Give an example from the scenario
Example: "For the $500,000 receipt in 3 months, a forward contract would guarantee the pound value."
L - Link: Link back to the question
Example: "This would help the company achieve its objective of reducing financial risk."

Common Exam Mistakes to Avoid

⚠️ Don't Make These Mistakes!

  • Using the wrong exchange rate - Always check if you should use bid or offer
  • Forgetting to annualize interest rates - If given annual rate but hedging for 6 months, divide by 2
  • Mixing up leads and lags - Lead = early, Lag = late
  • Not answering the question - If asked to "discuss", don't just calculate
  • Ignoring transaction costs - Some questions include bank charges or option premiums
  • Poor time management - Don't spend 30 minutes on one 10-mark question
  • Not reading the question properly - Is it a receipt or payment? What currency? What time period?

Key Formulas to Memorize

📝 Essential Formulas

Forward Rate: Spot ± Forward Points

Money Market Hedge (Receipt):
1. Borrow foreign currency: Amount ÷ (1 + r × t)
2. Convert at spot rate
3. Deposit home currency: Amount × (1 + r × t)

Money Market Hedge (Payment):
1. Borrow home currency
2. Convert at spot rate
3. Deposit foreign currency: Amount ÷ (1 + r × t)

FRA Settlement: Principal × (Market Rate - FRA Rate) × (Days/Year)

Interest Rate Futures:
Number of contracts = (Amount ÷ Contract Size) × (Hedge Period ÷ 3 months)

Effective Interest Rate:
(Net Interest ÷ Principal) × (12 ÷ Months) × 100%

Practice Question Framework

📝 Example 16: Comprehensive Risk Management Question

Scenario:
XYZ plc is a UK company that exports to the USA. In 6 months, it will receive $2 million from a US customer. The company is concerned about foreign exchange risk.

Current spot rate: £1 = $1.6000
6-month forward rate: £1 = $1.6100
6-month deposit rates: UK 4% per year, USA 6% per year
6-month borrowing rates: UK 5% per year, USA 7% per year

Required:
(a) Calculate the pound value if XYZ uses a forward contract hedge (2 marks)
(b) Calculate the pound value if XYZ uses a money market hedge (4 marks)
(c) Recommend which hedge XYZ should use (2 marks)
(d) Discuss two other methods XYZ could use to manage this risk (4 marks)
(e) Explain why XYZ might choose NOT to hedge at all (3 marks)

Suggested Answer:

(a) Forward Contract Hedge:
$2,000,000 ÷ 1.6100 = £1,242,236

(b) Money Market Hedge:
Step 1: Borrow $ at 7% for 6 months Amount to borrow = $2m ÷ (1 + 0.07 × 6/12) = $2m ÷ 1.035 = $1,932,367 Step 2: Convert to £ at spot rate $1,932,367 ÷ 1.6000 = £1,207,729 Step 3: Deposit £ at 4% for 6 months Amount after 6 months = £1,207,729 × (1 + 0.04 × 6/12) = £1,207,729 × 1.02 = £1,231,884 Step 4: In 6 months, use $2m receipt to repay loan $1,932,367 × 1.035 = $2,000,000 ✓ Net result: £1,231,884

(c) Recommendation:
Forward contract gives £1,242,236
Money market hedge gives £1,231,884
Recommend forward contract - it provides £10,352 more

(d) Other Methods:
1. Currency Options: Buy a put option on dollars. This gives the right but not obligation to sell $ at a fixed rate. Advantage: protects against downside while keeping upside potential. Disadvantage: premium cost.

2. Leading: Ask the customer to pay early, perhaps offering a discount. This eliminates the FX exposure entirely. Advantage: certainty. Disadvantage: discount cost, customer may refuse.

(e) Reasons Not to Hedge:
1. Transaction costs: Hedging costs money (bank charges, option premiums, etc.) which reduces profit
2. Shareholders can diversify: Individual shareholders can manage their own risk, so company hedging may be unnecessary
3. Upside potential: If the dollar strengthens, the company benefits from higher pound receipts. Hedging eliminates this upside
4. Management speculation: Managers might use hedging for speculation rather than genuine risk reduction
5. Small exposure: If $2m is a small part of overall revenue, the risk may be immaterial
