1. Types of foreign currency risk Syllabus G1(a) · Study Text Ch 13

Three risks — examiners love testing whether you can tell them apart

Transaction risk — the risk that the home-currency value of a specific foreign currency receipt or payment changes between the transaction date and the settlement date.

Translation risk — the risk that the balance sheet value of foreign assets, liabilities, or a foreign subsidiary's results changes when translated into the home currency for consolidated accounts. No cash actually moves; it is an accounting effect.

Economic risk — the risk that the present value of a company's future cash flows, and hence its long-term competitiveness, is affected by unexpected exchange rate movements — even where there is no direct foreign transaction at all.

TypeWhen it arisesReal cash flow effect?Can be hedged with derivatives?
TransactionEvery credit sale or purchase in a foreign currencyYesYes
TranslationConsolidation of foreign subsidiary accountsNoOnly partially (natural hedges via matching)
EconomicOngoing, long-term, affects competitivenessIndirectNo — not with financial instruments
Worked example — telling the three apart

An exporter agrees to sell goods for €1,200,000, receivable in six months.

  • The risk that the € weakens before the receipt is converted → transaction risk.
  • If the exporter also owns a subsidiary in the Eurozone and must consolidate its € balance sheet → translation risk.
  • If a rival Eurozone competitor benefits from a permanently weaker € and starts underpricing the exporter for years to come → economic risk.
Exam tip

If a scenario describes a subsidiary's accounts being consolidated, or balance sheet asset/liability values — that is translation. If cash is due on a specific future date — that is transaction. If the effect is long-term and about competitiveness rather than one deal — that is economic.

Common misconception

Economic risk cannot be eliminated by forwards, options, or money market hedges — those only cover specific, dated cash flows. The main defence against economic risk is diversification: of markets, suppliers, production location, and the currency mix of financing, so that no single exchange rate movement can seriously damage the business.

2. Types of interest rate risk Syllabus G1(b) · Study Text Ch 14

Gap exposure

Gap exposure arises because a company's interest-sensitive assets and liabilities do not match in amount. If floating-rate liabilities exceed floating-rate assets, a rate rise hurts the company on the uncovered "gap".

Worked example — gap exposure

A company has $50m of floating-rate loans but only $20m of floating-rate deposits. The gap is $30m. If rates rise, the company suffers a net increase in interest cost on this $30m, with no offsetting floating-rate income to compensate.

Basis risk

Basis risk arises when offsetting floating-rate assets and liabilities are priced off different reference rates, so they do not move perfectly together — even though the amounts match on paper.

Worked example — basis risk

A company has a $10m floating-rate loan priced at one reference rate +1% and a $10m floating-rate deposit priced off a different reference rate +0.5%. Even though the amounts match, if the two reference rates move by different amounts, the company still faces a net change in interest cost.

Basis risk also shows up in futures hedging

The same term describes the imperfect correlation between a futures price and the spot rate/interest rate being hedged. The two converge only at the futures' expiry date — closing out early always leaves some residual (basis) risk.

Quick check: which of the following describes gap exposure?

B. Gap exposure is the mismatch in the amounts of interest-sensitive assets and liabilities. Option C is actually a description of basis risk in a futures-hedging context — a very common distractor.

3. Causes of exchange rate fluctuations Syllabus G2(a) · Study Text Ch 13

Balance of payments

The balance of payments records all transactions between a country and the rest of the world. A country that persistently imports more than it exports (a current account deficit) generates excess demand for foreign currency, which tends to make its own currency depreciate. A surplus tends to cause appreciation.

Purchasing power parity (PPP) theory

PPP says exchange rates adjust so that the same basket of goods costs the same in any country once converted to a common currency. In practice: a country with higher inflation sees its currency depreciate against a lower-inflation country, roughly in line with the inflation gap.

PPP formula — given on the exam formula sheet
S1 = S0 × (1 + hcounter) / (1 + hbase)

The currency with a value of 1 in the quote is the base currency; the other is the counter currency.

Interest rate parity (IRPT) theory

IRPT says the gap between spot and forward exchange rates reflects the gap in nominal interest rates between two countries — otherwise risk-free arbitrage would be possible. The currency with the higher interest rate trades at a forward discount.

IRPT formula — given on the exam formula sheet
F0 = S0 × (1 + icounter) / (1 + ibase)

Four-way equivalence

Four-way equivalence links together four variables that should move in equilibrium in an efficient market with free capital flows:

  1. The inflation rate differential between two countries
  2. The interest rate differential between two countries
  3. The % actual change in the spot rate over time
  4. The % difference between the forward and spot rate today
Four-way equivalence in one line

Inflation rate differential ≈ Interest rate differential ≈ Forward/spot % difference ≈ Expected % change in the spot rate

Know any one of the four and you can estimate the other three. This links PPP (inflation → spot rate), IRPT (interest rates → forward rate), the Fisher effect (inflation → interest rates), and expectations theory (forward rate = unbiased predictor of the future spot rate).

Identifying base vs counter currency

In a quote "1.72 US$ = £1", sterling (£) is the base currency (value of 1); the US$ is the counter currency. Always apply the counter-currency rate on top of the PPP/IRPT fraction, base-currency rate on the bottom. Getting this backwards is the single most common error in this topic.

4. Forecasting exchange rates — PPP and IRPT in practice Syllabus G2(b) · Study Text Ch 13

Worked example 1 — PPP forecast

Current spot rate: $1.72 = £1. Annual inflation forecast: 4% in the UK, 3% in the US. Forecast the rate in one year.

Base = £, counter = $.

F0 = 1.72 × (1.03 / 1.04) = $1.7035 = £1

The $ strengthens slightly, because UK inflation (4%) exceeds US inflation (3%) — the higher-inflation currency (£) is expected to depreciate under PPP.

Worked example 2 — IRPT forecast

Spot: 10 Swiss Francs (SF) = £1. UK interest rate 7%; Swiss interest rate 3%. Forecast the 1-year forward rate.

F0 = 10 × (1.03 / 1.07) = 9.626 SF = £1

Sterling has the higher interest rate, so under IRPT it is expected to depreciate on the forward market — fewer SF are received per £ than at spot.

Worked example 3 — IRPT with a 6-month period

Spot: 0.94 Euros = $1. US rate 3% p.a.; Euro rate 6% p.a. Calculate the 6-month forward rate.

6-month rates: US = 3% × 6/12 = 1.5%; Euro = 6% × 6/12 = 3%.

F0 = 0.94 × (1.03 / 1.015) = 0.954 Euros = $1

For a two-year rate, either compound one year at a time, or raise the whole fraction to the power of 2.

Built-in sense check

The currency with the higher rate (inflation or interest) should always come out weaker in your answer. If your forecast implies the high-rate currency has strengthened, you've mixed up base and counter — go back and check.

Practice: Spot rate is 1.543 Euros = $1. The 6-month interest rate is 2% p.a. in the US and 5% p.a. in the Eurozone. What is the 6-month forward rate?

C. Base = $, counter = €. 6-month US rate = 1%; 6-month Euro rate = 2.5%. F0 = 1.543 × (1.025/1.01) = €1.566 = $1. The dollar (lower rate) strengthens, consistent with IRPT.

5. Causes of interest rate fluctuations & the yield curve Syllabus G2(c) · Study Text Ch 14

The yield curve plots interest rates on bonds of the same credit quality against time to maturity. It is usually upward sloping, but can be flat or even inverted (downward sloping).

TheoryMain driver of the curve's shape
Expectations theoryThe curve reflects the market's expectation of future short-term rates. Also used to predict future short-term rates from today's long-term rates.
Liquidity preference theoryInvestors demand extra compensation (a liquidity premium) for tying up money for longer — this alone biases the curve upward.
Market segmentation theoryDifferent investors have preferred maturity "habitats" (banks, pension funds, insurers), causing supply/demand imbalances and kinks in the curve that expectations or liquidity preference alone don't explain.
An inverted (downward-sloping) yield curve

Often signals that the market expects interest rates and/or inflation to fall in future, or that current short-term rates are unusually high and expected to normalise downward.

Practice: Which of the following is NOT an explanation of a downward-sloping yield curve?

A. Liquidity preference explains why the curve is naturally biased upward — it doesn't explain a downward slope.

6. Hedging foreign currency risk — internal methods Syllabus G3(a)(i)–(iii),(vi) · Study Text Ch 13

"Internal" or "traditional" hedges use the company's own commercial arrangements rather than a derivative contract with a bank.

Currency of invoice

Invoice foreign customers/suppliers in the home currency, passing all transaction risk to the counterparty. Limitation: only works with enough bargaining power — competitive pressure may force invoicing in the customer's currency instead.

Netting and matching

Netting — group entities owing each other amounts in the same currency settle only the net balance, reducing costs and exposure on the gross flows.
Matching — deliberately matching foreign currency receipts with payments (or assets with liabilities) in the same currency and similar timing, so only the net exposure remains.

Leading and lagging

Leading — paying a liability early, typically because that currency is expected to strengthen.
Lagging — delaying payment, typically because that currency is expected to weaken.

Leading/lagging is not a true hedge

Because it relies on a forecast of rate direction, leading/lagging does not guarantee risk reduction — if the rate moves the other way, the company is worse off. It is speculation dressed up as a hedge, and examiners frequently test this distinction.

Asset and liability management

Match the currency of assets (investments, receivables, foreign operations) with the currency of liabilities (loans, payables), so a rate change affects both sides similarly.

Worked example — choosing the right internal method

An exporter is expecting a Dinar (D) receivable in six months and separately has a Dinar-denominated overdraft. Because the receivable (asset) and overdraft (liability) are both in Dinars, this is asset and liability management — a fall in the Dinar reduces the value of the receivable, but also reduces the D-burden of repaying the overdraft.

MethodGuarantees a fixed rate?CostBest used when
Currency of invoiceYes, for the invoicing partyMay lose sales/competitivenessStrong negotiating power
Netting/matchingReduces exposure, not a full guaranteeLow — mainly admin savingsGroup has multi-directional intercompany flows
Leading/laggingNo — speculativePossible financing cost of paying earlyStrong, confident view on rate direction
Asset/liability managementPartial, natural hedgeLow if borrowing needs align anywayNatural foreign currency flows already exist on both sides

7. Hedging foreign currency risk — forward exchange contracts Syllabus G3(a)(iv) · Study Text Ch 13

Definition

A forward exchange contract (FEC) is an immediately binding agreement to buy or sell a fixed amount of currency at a rate fixed today, for delivery on an agreed future date. Once entered into it must be honoured.

Bid-offer spreads — the rule to never forget
Bank sells LOW  (sounds like "HELLO")
Bank buys HIGH  (sounds like "BYE BYE")

Always work out the rate from the bank's perspective — the spread always favours the bank.

Worked example — picking the correct side of the spread

Quote: 1.4325 – 1.4330 $/£. Company A wants to buy $100,000 (the bank is selling dollars).

Bank sells low → uses 1.4325 → Company A pays $100,000 / 1.4325 = £69,808.

Company B wants to sell $100,000 (the bank is buying dollars).

Bank buys high → uses 1.4330 → Company B receives $100,000 / 1.4330 = £69,784.

Worked example — hedging a receipt

An exporter will receive $400,000 in three months. The bank quotes a forward rate of 1.4565 for the exporter selling dollars.

$400,000 ÷ 1.4565 = £274,631, fixed today
Worked example — hedging a payment

A company must pay D3,000,000 in six months. Six-month forward rate: 1.94 dinars per $1.

D3,000,000 ÷ 1.94 = $1,546,392, fixed today
Exam tip — comparing a forward hedge to a lead payment

Bring both figures to the same point in time before comparing — usually by compounding the lead payment forward at the home-currency borrowing rate over the deferral period, since paying early means the company must borrow those funds now.

8. Hedging foreign currency risk — the money market hedge Syllabus G3(a)(v) · Study Text Ch 13

A money market hedge (MMH) uses borrowing and depositing, rather than a forward contract, to lock in an effective exchange rate today. Core principle: create an equal and opposite currency position now.

The golden rule

Future foreign currency receipt (an asset) → create a matching foreign currency liability today: borrow in that currency now, convert to home currency at spot, deposit.

Future foreign currency payment (a liability) → create a matching foreign currency asset today: deposit in that currency now (so it grows to the payment amount), funded by borrowing home currency today.

Method — future foreign currency PAYMENT

  1. Calculate the present value of the foreign amount needed, using the foreign deposit rate pro-rated for the period.
  2. Convert to home currency today at spot.
  3. Assume this home-currency amount is borrowed today at the home borrowing rate, to compare on a future-value basis with a forward quote.
Worked example — money market hedge for a payment

A company must pay $450,000 in three months. Annual $ deposit rate = 5%. Annual £ borrowing rate = 7.5%. Spot rate $1.70 = £1.

Step 1: 3-month $ deposit rate = 1.25%. Deposit now = $450,000 ÷ 1.0125 = $444,444.

Step 2: Convert at spot: $444,444 ÷ $1.70 = £261,438 needed today.

Step 3: Borrow £261,438 at 7.5% (3-month rate 1.875%): £261,438 × 1.01875 = £266,340 repayable in 3 months.

Result: the company knows today it will cost £266,340 to settle the $ liability — directly comparable to a forward contract quote.

Method — future foreign currency RECEIPT

  1. Borrow, in the foreign currency, the present value of the receipt (using the foreign borrowing rate).
  2. Convert the borrowed amount to home currency today at spot.
  3. Deposit the home-currency amount until the receipt date — its maturity value is the guaranteed home-currency value.
Worked example — money market hedge for a receipt

A company will receive MS300,000 in 3 months. MS borrowing rate = 4% p.a. Spot: MS1.1250 = $1 (bank buys the counter currency MS high).

Step 1: 3-month MS rate = 1%. Borrow now = MS300,000 ÷ 1.01 = MS297,030.

Step 2: Convert at spot: MS297,030 ÷ 1.1250 = $264,027 deposited today — the guaranteed dollar value of the future receipt.

Tailored to exact needs?YesYes

9. Foreign currency derivatives — futures, options, swaps Syllabus G3(c) · Study Text Ch 13 · No numerical questions set on this topic

Currency futures

Standardised, exchange-traded contracts to buy/sell a fixed amount of currency at a fixed future date. Unlike forwards: standard contract sizes and maturity dates, tradeable on an exchange (closeable before maturity), require a margin deposit. Fixed contract sizes mean hedges are rarely "perfect" — any residual must be rounded to a whole number of contracts.

Currency options

The right but not the obligation to buy (call) or sell (put) currency at a pre-set exercise price by/on a future date. A premium is paid upfront regardless of use.

  • Spot more favourable than the exercise price → let the option lapse, deal at spot.
  • Spot less favourable → exercise, deal at the guaranteed rate.

Options may be over-the-counter (OTC) — tailored by a bank, suited to smaller companies — or exchange-traded — standardised sizes, suited to larger companies, and can be sold on before expiry.

Worked example — currency option outcome

An exporter due to receive $25m in 3 months buys a put option to sell $ at $1.50 = £1, premium £30,000.

Spot moves to $1.60 = £1 (worse than the option rate): exercise. $25m ÷ $1.50 = £16.67m, less £30,000 premium = net £16.64m.

Spot moves to $1.40 = £1 (better than the option rate): let it lapse, deal at spot. $25m ÷ $1.40 = £17.86m, less £30,000 premium = net £17.83m.

Currency swaps

An agreement to exchange equivalent amounts of debt (principal and/or interest) denominated in different currencies — often used to obtain more favourable long-term financing or to hedge translation exposure on long-term foreign investments.

Exam tip — why options for tender contracts?

An option is particularly useful when tendering for a foreign currency contract, because the company does not yet know if it will win the tender. A forward or money market hedge would leave it obligated to deal in currency it might not need. An option can simply lapse (at the cost of the premium) if the tender is unsuccessful.

10. Hedging interest rate risk — internal methods Syllabus G4(a)(i)–(ii) · Study Text Ch 14

Matching

Match the interest rate basis of assets and liabilities — e.g. finance a fixed-income asset with fixed-rate debt, or a floating-income asset with floating-rate debt — so rate changes affect income and expense in the same direction.

Smoothing

Maintain a mixture of fixed and floating rate debt (e.g. 50:50) rather than being 100% one or the other. This reduces, but does not eliminate, the impact of a rate change.

Common exam trap

Smoothing does not mean interest payments stay fixed — they still rise if rates rise (just by less than if 100% floating), and the company does not get the full benefit of a rate fall either. Never describe smoothing as eliminating interest rate risk.

Asset and liability management

Match the maturity of interest-bearing assets and liabilities, e.g. ensuring a floating-rate deposit matures around the same time a floating-rate loan needs refinancing, reducing the risk of a gap arising.

Worked example — matching vs smoothing

A company funds a fixed-income bond portfolio entirely with fixed-rate debt of similar maturity — this is matching.

A different company keeps its $200m debt at 60% fixed / 40% floating rather than 100% floating — this is smoothing (partial protection, not elimination).

11. Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs) Syllabus G4(a)(iii) · Study Text Ch 14

Definition

An FRA is an over-the-counter agreement with a bank that fixes the interest rate on a notional short-term loan or deposit for an agreed future period, without affecting the underlying loan/deposit — which is still taken out separately at whatever the prevailing market rate is on that date. The FRA is a completely separate contract that simply pays or receives a compensating cash difference.

How an FRA works — mechanics
  • To hedge a future loan — the company buys the FRA.
  • To hedge a future deposit — the company sells the FRA.
  • The actual loan/deposit is taken out as normal, at the prevailing market rate.
  • If the market rate is higher than the FRA rate, the bank pays the company the difference (compensating a borrower).
  • If the market rate is lower than the FRA rate, the company pays the bank the difference.
  • Net effect: locked into the FRA rate regardless of market movements.

Reading an FRA quote

E.g. "2–5 FRA at 5.00 – 4.70":

  • Starts in 2 months, ends in 5 months (a 3-month period, beginning 2 months from now).
  • Rates are simple annual interest rates.
  • The higher rate (5.00%) is the borrowing rate; the lower (4.70%) is the deposit rate.
Worked example — FRA to hedge a loan

A company needs to borrow $8m in 2 months for 3 months. It agrees a 2–5 FRA at 5.00% – 4.70% (borrowing rate = 5.00%). Net effective cost if the market rate turns out to be (a) 7% or (b) 4%?

Market rate 7%Market rate 4%
Interest on actual loan: $8m × rate × 3/12(140,000)(80,000)
FRA compensation: $8m × (rate − 5%) × 3/12+40,000(20,000)
Net effective cost(100,000)(100,000)

Both scenarios net to $100,000 — exactly $8m × 5% × 3/12. The company is locked into 5% regardless of the market rate.

Worked example — FRA to hedge a deposit

A company deposits $1m for 3 months and uses a 5.5% – 5.0% FRA (deposit rate 5.0%, the lower rate). If the market rate is 5.5%, the company must actually pay the bank to bring its return down to the locked-in 5.0%: 0.5% × $1m × 3/12 = $1,250 payment.

Exam tip — quick FRA method

Net compensating amount = Notional × (Market rate − FRA rate) × time/12. Borrowing & market rate > FRA rate → receive. Borrowing & market rate < FRA rate → pay. Reverse the direction for a deposit-hedging FRA.

Practice: A company needs a $10m 6-month loan from 1 October and hedges with an FRA at 6% on 30 June. In 6 months, the market rate is 9%. What is the outcome?

A. Compensation = $10m × (9% − 6%) × 6/12 = $150,000, receivable from the bank — the market rate is above the FRA rate on a loan hedge.

12. Interest rate derivatives — futures, options, IRGs, caps/floors/collars, swaps Syllabus G4(b) · Study Text Ch 14 · No numerical questions set on this topic

Interest rate futures

Standardised, exchange-traded contracts locking in an effective interest rate. Standard sizes and dates; hedge against both favourable and adverse moves (no flexibility); can be closed out at any time, though the ideal close-out date is expiry, when the futures price converges with spot. Borrowers sell futures to hedge a rate rise (buying back later); lenders/depositors buy futures to hedge a rate fall.

Interest rate options

The right, not the obligation, to deal at a set rate. Premium paid upfront regardless of use. Protects against adverse moves while keeping upside potential.

Interest rate guarantees (IRGs)

An IRG is an option on an FRA — the right (for a fee) to enter an FRA at a pre-agreed rate, or walk away and deal at market if more favourable.

SituationBest instrumentWhy
Confident rates will riseFRACheaper — no premium, fully locks in the rate
Uncertain which way rates will moveIRGMore expensive, but allows benefit from a favourable fall

Caps, floors, and collars

  • Cap — sets a maximum rate (useful for borrowers). If actual rates are lower, the cap lapses and the borrower enjoys the lower market rate.
  • Floor — sets a minimum rate (useful for depositors). If actual rates are higher, the floor lapses and the investor enjoys the higher market rate.
  • Collar — combines a cap and a floor. A borrower buys a cap and sells a floor — the floor premium received offsets the cap's cost, at the price of giving up some benefit from very low rates. A depositor does the opposite.
Why use a collar rather than just a cap?

Buying an option alone costs a premium. Selling the opposite option earns a premium that reduces (or eliminates) the net cost — in exchange for giving up some potential upside if rates move very favourably.

Interest rate swaps

An agreement to exchange a stream of interest payments — typically fixed for floating, or vice versa — on a notional principal. Only the interest cash flows are exchanged; the principal is never exchanged, and the original borrower remains legally liable to its original lender.

Worked example — why use a swap?

A company has a $200m floating-rate loan and expects rates to rise over 5 years. Rather than repaying early (penalty charges) and taking a new fixed-rate loan, it enters a 5-year swap, exchanging floating for fixed payments with a counterparty — often cheaper than restructuring the actual borrowing.

A currency swap is a variant swapping debt obligations denominated in different currencies rather than different interest rate types.

Practice: Which TWO derivative instruments are characterised by a standard contract size?

C. Futures and exchange-tradable options have standardised sizes; forwards, FRAs, swaps and OTC options are all tailored to the customer's exact needs.

Test yourself

Answer before you scroll. If you get one wrong, jump back to the relevant section rather than just reading the explanation.

1. A company expects an exchange loss when a foreign subsidiary's results are consolidated into the group accounts. What type of risk is this?

D. A consolidation/accounting effect with no direct cash movement is translation risk.

2. Which relationship explains the difference between forward and spot exchange rates by reference to relative interest rates?

C. IRPT links the forward/spot differential to interest rate differentials between the two countries.

3. Which TWO statements about the interest rate technique of "smoothing" are true?

B. Smoothing means holding a mix of fixed/floating debt; interest costs still move with rates, just by less. A describes matching, not smoothing; C and D are false since smoothing gives only partial protection.

4. A company has a $14m variable-rate loan and buys a 4–11 FRA at 2.85% – 2.35%. If the actual rate turns out to be 3.75%, what amount is received from the bank (to the nearest dollar)?

B. Period = 7 months (month 4 to month 11). Borrowing rate fixed by FRA = 2.85% (the higher rate). Difference = 3.75% − 2.85% = 0.90%. Amount received = $14m × 0.9% × 7/12 = $73,500.

5. Which of the following about an over-the-counter (OTC) interest rate option is/are correct? (1) It is an agreement with a financial institution. (2) It can be traded. (3) An immediate premium is payable. (4) It must be exercised.

B. OTC options are agreements with a bank (1) with an upfront premium (3). They cannot be traded (2 is false — OTC means tailor-made) and never have to be exercised (4 is false).

6. Which TWO hedging techniques can a depositor use to protect against a FALL in interest rates?

B. A floor directly protects a depositor against falling rates; a collar (from the depositor's side, buying a floor and selling a cap) also achieves this while reducing net premium cost.

7. An interest rate swap:

A. A swap exchanges interest payment streams only; the principal is never exchanged. B describes an IRG; C describes a future; D is simply false — no swap (interest rate or currency) transfers the underlying principal permanently.

8. An inverse (downward-sloping) yield curve is most likely to indicate:

A. A downward-sloping curve suggests the market expects interest rates to fall — often because current short-term rates are unusually high and expected to normalise.
