1. Why Risk Management matters in FM

Section G is the final syllabus area in FM and typically appears in Question 1 or 2 of Section B (as part of the compulsory 20-mark case-study style questions), worth roughly 15–20% of the total exam marks when combined with related topics. It is a highly "learnable" topic — much of it is discursive (descriptive) rather than heavy calculation, and the numerical parts follow a small number of very repeatable formulas.

G1: Nature & types of risk G2: Causes of FX & interest rate movements G3: Hedging FX risk G4: Hedging interest rate risk

Good news about this topic

The ACCA examining team has confirmed that no numerical questions will be set on foreign currency derivatives (futures, options) or on interest rate derivatives (futures, options, swaps). You only need to describe how these instruments work. The numerical work in this topic is limited to:

  • Forward exchange contracts
  • Money market hedges
  • Purchasing power parity (PPP) and interest rate parity (IRPT) forecasts
  • Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)

Master these four calculations and you have covered almost all the marks available for numbers in this topic.

How this topic is examined

Expect a mix of: multiple choice / objective test questions (OTQs) on definitions and theory, a short calculation (forward contract, money market hedge, PPP/IRPT, or FRA), and a discursive requirement such as "explain three methods of managing this risk" or "discuss the differences between X and Y."

2. Types of Foreign Currency Risk G1(a)

Syllabus reference: Describe and discuss different types of foreign currency risk: (i) translation risk (ii) transaction risk (iii) economic risk.

A company that trades internationally, borrows in foreign currency, or owns overseas subsidiaries is exposed to movements in exchange rates. There are three distinct types of exposure — examiners love testing whether you can tell them apart.

TypeWhat it isWhen it arisesCash flow effect?
Transaction risk The risk that the home-currency value of a specific foreign currency receipt or payment changes between the date the transaction is agreed and the date it is settled. Every time a company buys or sells on credit in a foreign currency. Yes — real cash flow impact.
Translation risk The risk that the balance sheet value of foreign assets, liabilities, or a foreign subsidiary's results changes when translated into the home currency for consolidated accounts. At each year-end / consolidation date, for companies with foreign subsidiaries. No — it's an accounting risk only; no cash actually moves.
Economic risk The risk that the present value of a company's future cash flows (and hence its competitiveness and long-term value) is affected by unexpected exchange rate movements. Long-term, ongoing — even companies with no direct foreign trade can be exposed (e.g. if a foreign competitor becomes cheaper). Indirect — affects competitiveness over time.

Worked example — telling the three risks apart

An exporter agrees to sell goods for €1,200,000, receivable in six months' time.

  • The risk that the € weakens before the receipt is converted to home currency → transaction risk.
  • If the exporter also owns a German subsidiary and must consolidate its € balance sheet into the group's home-currency accounts → translation risk.
  • If a rival German competitor benefits from a permanently weaker € and starts underpricing the exporter for the next ten years → economic risk.

Key point: economic risk cannot be hedged with financial instruments

Because economic risk is about long-term competitive position, it cannot be eliminated by forwards, options or money market hedges. The main "hedge" against economic risk is diversification — of markets, suppliers, production location, and currency of debt — so that no single exchange rate movement can seriously damage the business.

Exam tip

If a question describes a subsidiary's accounts being consolidated, or asset/liability values on the statement of financial position — that is translation, not transaction. If cash is due to be paid or received on a specific future date — that is transaction. If the scenario talks about "long-term competitiveness" or effects that are not tied to one deal — that is economic.

3. Types of Interest Rate Risk G1(b)

Syllabus reference: Describe and discuss different types of interest rate risk: (i) gap exposure (ii) basis risk.

Gap exposure

Gap exposure arises because a company's interest-sensitive assets and liabilities do not match in amount or in the timing of their interest rate resets. If a company has more floating-rate liabilities than floating-rate assets, a rise in interest rates will hurt it — the "gap" between the two is the exposed amount.

Worked example — gap exposure

A company has $50m of floating-rate loans but only $20m of floating-rate deposits/investments. The "gap" is $30m. If interest rates rise, the company suffers a net increase in interest cost on this $30m gap, because it has no offsetting floating-rate income to compensate.

Basis risk

Basis risk arises when a company holds offsetting assets and liabilities that are both at floating rates, but the floating rates are set with reference to different benchmarks (or reset at different times), so they do not move perfectly together. Even though the company looks "matched" on paper, the two rates can drift apart.

Worked example — basis risk

A company has a $10m floating-rate loan priced at SOFR + 1% and a $10m floating-rate deposit priced at a different reference rate + 0.5%. Even though the amounts match exactly, if the two reference rates move by different amounts, the company is still exposed to a net change in interest cost. This mismatch in the "basis" (reference rate) used is basis risk.

Basis risk also appears in hedging with futures

Basis risk is also the term used to describe the imperfect correlation between the price of a futures contract and the price/rate of the underlying item being hedged. Because a futures price and the spot rate only converge exactly on the expiry date, closing out a futures position early leaves some residual (basis) risk — this is a key reason futures hedges are described as "imperfect."

Quick check: Which of the following describes gap exposure?

A. The difference between short-term and long-term interest rates
B. The difference between the amounts of interest-sensitive assets and liabilities
C. The difference between spot interest rates and futures interest rates
D. The difference between fixed and floating interest rates

Show answer

Answer: B. Gap exposure is the mismatch between the amounts of interest-sensitive assets and liabilities. (Note: this exact OTQ has appeared in the Exam Kit — many students incorrectly choose C, which actually describes basis risk in a futures context.)

4. Causes of Exchange Rate Fluctuations G2(a)

Syllabus reference: Describe the causes of exchange rate fluctuations, including: (i) balance of payments (ii) purchasing power parity theory (iii) interest rate parity theory (iv) four-way equivalence.

(i) Balance of payments

The balance of payments records all transactions between a country and the rest of the world (trade in goods/services, investment flows, etc.). If a country consistently imports more than it exports (a current account deficit), there is more demand for foreign currency than for the home currency, which tends to cause the home currency to depreciate. A surplus tends to cause appreciation.

(ii) Purchasing power parity (PPP) theory

PPP states that exchange rates adjust over time so that the same basket of goods costs the same amount in any country, once converted to a common currency. In practice, this means that a country with higher inflation than another will see its currency depreciate against the other, roughly in line with the inflation differential.

Future spot rate = Current spot rate × (1 + inflation rate in counter currency country) / (1 + inflation rate in base currency country)

Note: the exchange rate is always quoted as "counter currency per 1 unit of base currency." The currency you multiply by 1 in the quote is the base currency; the other is the counter currency.

(iii) Interest rate parity (IRPT) theory

IRPT states that the difference between the spot and forward exchange rates for two currencies reflects the difference in nominal (money market) interest rates between the two countries. This exists because arbitrage would otherwise allow risk-free profit. The currency with the higher interest rate will trade at a forward discount (depreciate on the forward market).

F0 = S0 × (1 + icounter) / (1 + ibase)

This is exactly the formula given on the ACCA formula sheet — you do not need to memorise it, but you must know how to apply it and identify base vs counter currency correctly.

(iv) Four-way equivalence

Four-way equivalence (also examinable as the "international Fisher effect" family of relationships) links together four variables that should, in theory, move in equilibrium with each other:

  1. The difference in inflation rates between two countries
  2. The difference in interest rates between two countries
  3. The % change in the spot exchange rate (actual, over time)
  4. The % difference between the forward and spot exchange rate

The theory says that, in an efficient market with no barriers to capital flow, all four of these should be approximately equal in size, because:

  • PPP links inflation differentials to changes in the spot rate.
  • IRPT links interest rate differentials to the forward/spot difference.
  • The Fisher effect links inflation differentials to interest rate differentials (nominal rates move to compensate for expected inflation).
  • The (unbiased) expectations theory of forward rates says the forward rate is the market's best unbiased predictor of the future spot rate.

Four-way equivalence diagram (in words)

Inflation rate differential ≈ Interest rate differential ≈ Forward/spot % difference ≈ Expected % change in spot rate

If you know any one of these four figures, four-way equivalence lets you estimate the other three.

Exam tip — identifying base vs counter currency

In a quote such as "1.72 US$ = £1", sterling (£) is the base currency (it has a value of 1) and the US$ is the counter currency. Always apply the counter-currency rate on top of the fraction and the base-currency rate on the bottom in the PPP/IRPT formula.

5. Forecasting Exchange Rates — PPP and IRPT in Practice G2(b)

Syllabus reference: Forecast exchange rates using: (i) purchasing power parity (ii) interest rate parity.

Worked example 1 — PPP forecast

The current spot rate is $1.72 = £1. Annual inflation is expected to be 4% in the UK and 3% in the US. Forecast the exchange rate in one year's time using PPP.

Step 1: Identify base (£) and counter ($) currency.
Step 2: Apply the formula:

F0 = 1.72 × (1.03 / 1.04) = $1.7035 = £1

The US$ is forecast to strengthen slightly against £, because UK inflation (4%) is higher than US inflation (3%) — the higher-inflation currency (£) is expected to depreciate, consistent with PPP.

Worked example 2 — IRPT forecast

Spot rate: 10 Swiss Francs (SF) = £1. UK interest rate 7% per year; Swiss interest rate 3% per year. Forecast the 1-year forward rate.

F0 = 10 × (1.03 / 1.07) = 9.626 SF = £1

Sterling has the higher interest rate (7% vs 3%), so under IRPT sterling is expected to depreciate on the forward market (fewer SF are received per £ than at spot) — consistent with the rule "high interest rate currency = forward discount."

Worked example 3 — IRPT with non-annual periods

Spot rate 0.94 Euros = $1. US interest rate 3% per year; Euro interest rate 6% per year. Calculate the 6-month forward rate.

6-month rates: US = 3% × 6/12 = 1.5%  |  Euro = 6% × 6/12 = 3%
F0 = 0.94 × (1.03 / 1.015) = 0.954 Euros = $1

Note: simple pro-rating (rate × months/12) is normally used for periods under a year unless the question specifically says to compound. For a two-year forward rate, you can either compound one year at a time or raise the whole fraction to the power of 2: F0 = 0.94 × (1.06² / 1.03²).

Common error to avoid

Do not confuse which currency's interest/inflation rate goes on top. A quick sense-check: the currency with the higher rate (inflation or interest) should always come out weaker (depreciate) in your answer. If your calculated forward rate implies the high-rate currency has strengthened, you have made an error — check which currency is base and which is counter.

Practice: The spot rate is 1.543 Euros = $1. The 6-month interest rate is 2% per year in the US and 5% per year in the Eurozone. What is the 6-month forward rate?

Show answer

Base currency = $, counter currency = €.
6-month US rate = 2% × 6/12 = 1%. 6-month Euro rate = 5% × 6/12 = 2.5%.
F0 = 1.543 × (1.025/1.01) = €1.566 = $1.
The dollar (lower interest rate) strengthens — consistent with IRPT.

6. Causes of Interest Rate Fluctuations & the Yield Curve G2(c)

Syllabus reference: Describe the causes of interest rate fluctuations, including: (i) structure of interest rates and yield curves (ii) expectations theory (iii) liquidity preference theory (iv) market segmentation.

The yield curve

The yield curve plots interest rates (yields) on bonds of the same credit quality against their time to maturity. It is usually upward sloping (long-term rates higher than short-term rates), but can be flat, or even downward sloping ("inverted").

(i) Expectations theory

The shape of the yield curve reflects the market's expectations of future short-term interest rates. If rates are expected to rise, the yield curve slopes upward; if rates are expected to fall, it can slope downward (invert). This theory also underlies the idea that current long-term rates can be used to predict future short-term rates.

(ii) Liquidity preference theory

Investors generally prefer short-term, more liquid investments and must be offered a higher return (liquidity premium) to tie up their money for longer. This alone tends to push the yield curve upward sloping even if interest rates are expected to stay flat.

(iii) Market segmentation theory

Different investors (e.g. banks, pension funds, insurance companies) have preferred maturity "habitats" driven by the maturity of their own liabilities. Because the short, medium and long ends of the market are dominated by different groups of investors/borrowers with limited overlap, supply and demand can differ in each segment, which can cause kinks or discontinuities in the yield curve that are not explained by expectations or liquidity preference alone.

TheoryMain driver of yield curve shape
Expectations theoryMarket's view on future short-term interest rate movements
Liquidity preference theoryInvestors demand extra compensation for tying up money longer (biases curve upward)
Market segmentation theoryDistinct investor "habitats" for different maturities, causing kinks

An inverted (downward-sloping) yield curve

This is often seen as a signal that the market expects interest rates (and/or inflation) to fall in future, or that current short-term rates are unusually high (e.g. due to tight central bank policy) and are expected to normalise downward.

Practice: Which of the following is NOT an explanation of a downward-sloping yield curve?

A. Liquidity preference   B. Expectations theory   C. Government policy   D. Market segmentation

Show answer

Answer: A. Liquidity preference theory explains why the curve is naturally biased upward sloping (investors need compensation for illiquidity) — it does not explain a downward slope.

7. Hedging Foreign Currency Risk — Internal (Traditional) Methods G3(a)

Syllabus reference: Discuss and apply traditional and basic methods of foreign currency risk management, including: currency of invoice, netting and matching, leading and lagging, forward exchange contracts, money market hedging, asset and liability management. Also: compare and evaluate these methods.

"Internal" or "traditional" hedges do not use derivative contracts with a bank — they use the company's own commercial arrangements to reduce risk.

Currency of invoice

The simplest method: invoice foreign customers/suppliers in the home currency, so all transaction risk is passed to the counterparty. Limitation: only works if you have enough bargaining power — competitive pressure may force you to invoice in the customer's currency instead.

Netting and matching

Netting: where a group has multiple subsidiaries owing each other amounts in the same currency (or offsetting positions), only the net balance is settled between them, reducing transaction costs and currency exposure on the gross flows.

Matching: a company deliberately matches foreign currency receipts with payments in the same currency, so that inflows and outflows offset each other and only the net exposure remains. This can also apply to matching foreign currency assets with foreign currency liabilities (loans) of the same currency and similar maturity.

Leading and lagging

Leading: paying a foreign currency liability early, typically because the currency you must pay in is expected to strengthen — paying now avoids paying more later.

Lagging: delaying payment, typically because the currency you must pay in is expected to weaken — paying later means you need fewer units of home currency.

Leading/lagging is not a true "hedge"

Because it relies on a forecast of the direction of the exchange rate, leading and lagging does not guarantee risk reduction — if the exchange rate moves in the opposite direction to that expected, the company can be worse off. It is a speculative technique dressed up as a hedge, and examiners frequently test this distinction.

Asset and liability management

A company matches the currency of its assets (e.g. investments, receivables, overseas operations) with the currency of its liabilities (e.g. loans, payables). For example, financing a foreign subsidiary's assets with a loan in the same foreign currency means that a change in the exchange rate affects both sides similarly, reducing net exposure (this can hedge both transaction and translation risk).

Worked example — choosing the right internal method

A UK exporter is expecting a Dinar (D) receivable in six months and separately has a Dinar-denominated overdraft. Because the receivable (asset) and the overdraft (liability) are both in Dinars, this is asset and liability management — a fall in the Dinar reduces the value of the receivable but also reduces the D-value burden of repaying the overdraft, providing a natural hedge.

Comparing the internal methods

MethodGuarantees a fixed rate?CostBest used when
Currency of invoiceYes, for the invoicing partyMay lose sales/competitivenessYou have strong negotiating power
Netting/matchingReduces exposure, not a full guaranteeLow — mainly admin savingsGroup has multi-directional intercompany flows
Leading/laggingNo — speculativePossible financing cost of paying earlyStrong, confident view on rate direction

8. Hedging Foreign Currency Risk — Forward Exchange Contracts G3(a)(iv)

What is a forward exchange contract (FEC)?

A forward contract is an immediately binding agreement to buy or sell a specific amount of currency, at a rate fixed today, for delivery/settlement at an agreed future date. Once entered into, it must be honoured — this is both its strength (certainty) and its weakness (no ability to benefit from favourable rate movements).

Understanding bid-offer (buy-sell) spreads

Banks always quote two rates — you must pick the correct one. Remember these two rules:

Bank sells LOW  (sounds like "HELLO")
Bank buys HIGH  (sounds like "BYE BYE")

Think of it from the bank's perspective — the bank always structures the spread to make a profit.

Worked example — picking the correct rate

Quote: 1.4325 – 1.4330 $/£. Company A wants to buy $100,000 (pay £ to receive $ — the bank is selling dollars).

The bank sells low → uses 1.4325 → Company A pays $100,000 / 1.4325 = £69,808.

Company B wants to sell $100,000 (receive £, the bank is buying dollars).

The bank buys high → uses 1.4330 → Company B receives $100,000 / 1.4330 = £69,784.

Worked example — hedging a foreign receipt with a forward contract

An exporter will receive $400,000 in three months. The bank quotes a forward rate with the exporter selling dollars to the bank at 1.4565.

Exporter receives = $400,000 ÷ 1.4565 = £274,631, fixed today, regardless of what the spot rate turns out to be in three months.

Worked example — hedging a foreign payment with a forward contract

PZK Co must make a payment of D3,000,000 in six months. Six-month forward rate: 1.94 dinars per $1 (PZK is buying dinars, i.e. the bank is selling dinars).

Cost = D3,000,000 ÷ 1.94 = $1,546,392, fixed today.

Exam tip — comparing a forward hedge to leading

If a question asks you to compare a forward contract with a "lead payment" (paying now instead of later), remember to bring both figures to the same point in time before comparing — usually by compounding the lead payment forward at the home-currency borrowing rate for the deferral period, since paying early means the company must borrow the funds now.

9. Hedging Foreign Currency Risk — The Money Market Hedge G3(a)(v)

A money market hedge (MMH) uses borrowing and depositing (rather than a forward contract) to lock in an effective exchange rate today. The core principle: create an equal and opposite currency position now, so the future currency movement no longer matters.

The golden rule of money market hedging

If you have a future foreign currency receipt (an asset), you must create a matching foreign currency liability today — i.e. borrow in that foreign currency now, convert to home currency at spot, and deposit/use the home currency. The loan is then repaid using the foreign currency received later.

If you have a future foreign currency payment (a liability), you must create a matching foreign currency asset today — i.e. deposit in that foreign currency now (so it grows with interest to the amount you need to pay), funding this by borrowing in home currency today.

Step-by-step method for a future foreign currency PAYMENT

  1. Calculate the present value of the foreign currency amount needed, using the foreign deposit rate pro-rated for the period, so that with interest it grows to exactly the payment amount.
  2. Convert this amount to home currency today, at the spot rate (using the correct side of the spread).
  3. This home-currency amount is generally assumed to be borrowed today at the home currency borrowing rate, so it can be compared on a like-for-like future-value basis with a forward contract quote.

Worked example — money market hedge for a future payment

Thakur Co must pay $450,000 in three months. Annual $ deposit rate = 5%. Annual £ borrowing rate = 7.5%. Spot rate $1.70 = £1.

Step 1: 3-month $ deposit rate = 5% × 3/12 = 1.25%.
Amount to deposit now = $450,000 ÷ 1.0125 = $444,444.

Step 2: Convert to £ at spot: $444,444 ÷ $1.70 = £261,438 needed today.

Step 3: Assume this £261,438 is borrowed today at 7.5% p.a. (3-month rate = 1.875%):
£261,438 × 1.01875 = £266,340 repayable in 3 months.

Conclusion: Thakur Co knows today it will cost £266,340 to settle the $ liability in 3 months — this figure is then directly compared with the cost under a forward contract to choose the cheaper hedge.

Method for a future foreign currency RECEIPT

  1. Borrow, in the foreign currency, the present value of the receipt (using the foreign borrowing rate), so the loan (with interest) exactly equals the amount you will receive.
  2. Convert the foreign currency borrowed into home currency today at spot.
  3. Deposit the home-currency amount (at the home deposit rate) until the receipt date — the deposit's maturity value is the guaranteed home-currency value of the hedge.

Worked example — money market hedge for a future receipt

Marigold Co will receive MS300,000 (M shillings) in 3 months. MS borrowing rate = 4% p.a. Spot: MS1.1250 = $1 (bank buys the counter currency MS high).

Step 1: 3-month MS borrowing rate = 4% × 3/12 = 1%.
Amount to borrow today = MS300,000 ÷ 1.01 = MS297,030.

Step 2: Convert to $ at spot (bank buys MS high, so divide by 1.1250):
MS297,030 ÷ 1.1250 = $264,027 deposited today.

This $264,027 (plus $ deposit interest, if asked) is the guaranteed dollar value of the future MS receipt.

10. Foreign Currency Derivatives — Futures, Options, Swaps G3(c)

Syllabus reference: Identify the main types of foreign currency derivatives used to hedge foreign currency risk and explain how they are used in hedging. No numerical questions will be set on this topic — description only.

Currency futures

Standardised, exchange-traded contracts to buy or sell a fixed amount of currency at a fixed future date. Unlike forwards, futures have standard contract sizes and standard maturity dates, are traded on an exchange (so can be closed out / sold on before maturity), and require a margin deposit. Because contract sizes are fixed, hedges are rarely "perfect" — any shortfall/excess must be rounded to a whole number of contracts.

Currency options

An option gives the holder the right but not the obligation to buy (call option) or sell (put option) a currency at a pre-set exercise price by/on a future date. A premium is payable upfront, whether or not the option is ever exercised.

  • If the spot rate on the exercise date is more favourable than the exercise price, the company lets the option lapse and deals at spot.
  • If the spot rate is less favourable, the company exercises the option and gets the guaranteed rate.

Options may be over-the-counter (OTC) — tailored by a bank to the customer's exact needs, suited to smaller companies — or exchange-traded — standardised contract sizes, suited to larger companies, and can be sold on before expiry.

Worked example — currency option outcome

A UK exporter due to receive $25m in 3 months buys a put option to sell $ at $1.50 = £1, premium £30,000.

If spot moves to $1.60 = £1 (worse than the option rate): exercise the option. $25m ÷ $1.50 = £16.67m, less £30,000 premium = net £16.64m.

If spot moves to $1.40 = £1 (better than the option rate): let the option lapse, deal at spot. $25m ÷ $1.40 = £17.86m, less £30,000 premium = net £17.83m.

Options therefore protect against the downside while preserving upside potential — at the cost of the premium.

Currency swaps

An agreement between two parties to exchange equivalent amounts of debt (principal and/or interest obligations) denominated in different currencies, often used to obtain more favourable long-term financing or to hedge translation exposure on long-term foreign investments.

Exam tip — why options for tender contracts?

A currency option is particularly useful when tendering for a foreign currency contract, because the company does not yet know if it will win the contract. A forward or money market hedge would leave the company obligated to deal in a currency amount it might not actually need. An option can simply be allowed to lapse (at the cost of the premium) if the tender is unsuccessful.

11. Hedging Interest Rate Risk — Internal Methods G4(a)(i)(ii)

Syllabus reference: Discuss and apply traditional and basic methods of interest rate risk management, including: matching and smoothing, asset and liability management, forward rate agreements.

Matching

A company matches the interest rate basis of its assets and liabilities — e.g. financing a fixed-income-generating asset with fixed-rate debt, or financing a floating-rate-generating asset with floating-rate debt — so that changes in interest rates affect income and expense in the same direction, cancelling out the net effect.

Smoothing

Rather than being 100% fixed-rate or 100% floating-rate, the company maintains a mixture of fixed and floating rate debt (e.g. 50:50). This does not eliminate interest rate risk, but it reduces (smooths) the impact of any rate change, since only the floating portion is affected.

Common exam trap

Smoothing does not mean interest payments stay completely fixed — they will still rise if rates rise (just by less than if 100% floating), and the company will not get the full benefit of a rate fall either (because part of the debt is fixed). Do not describe smoothing as eliminating interest rate risk entirely.

Asset and liability management

The company matches the maturity of its interest-bearing assets and liabilities — e.g. ensuring a floating-rate deposit matures around the same time a floating-rate loan needs refinancing — reducing the risk of a mismatch (gap exposure) arising.

Worked example — matching vs smoothing

A company with a fixed-income government bond portfolio funds it entirely with fixed-rate debt of a similar maturity — this is matching (both sides move together, or rather, neither side moves).

A different company keeps its $200m debt portfolio at 60% fixed / 40% floating rather than 100% floating — this is smoothing (partial protection, not elimination, of interest rate risk).

12. Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs) G4(a)(iii)

What is an FRA?

An FRA is an over-the-counter agreement with a bank that fixes the interest rate on a notional short-term loan or deposit, for an agreed future period, without affecting the actual underlying loan/deposit which is still taken out separately at whatever the prevailing market rate is on that date. The FRA is a completely separate contract that simply pays or receives a compensating cash difference.

How an FRA works — the mechanics

  • To hedge a future loan (borrowing), the company buys the FRA.
  • To hedge a future deposit (lending), the company sells the FRA.
  • The actual loan/deposit is taken out as normal, at whatever the market rate turns out to be.
  • Under the FRA: if the market rate is higher than the agreed FRA rate, the bank pays the company the difference (compensating a borrower for the higher cost).
  • If the market rate is lower than the agreed FRA rate, the company pays the bank the difference.
  • Net effect: the company is "locked in" to the FRA rate regardless of what happens to market rates — protected from adverse moves, but unable to benefit from favourable moves.

Reading an FRA quote

An FRA is quoted as, e.g., "2–5 FRA at 5.00 – 4.70". This means:

  • The agreement starts in 2 months and ends in 5 months (i.e. covers a 3-month period, starting 2 months from now).
  • Rates are quoted as simple annual interest rates.
  • The higher rate (5.00%) is always the borrowing rate; the lower rate (4.70%) is the deposit/lending rate.

Worked example — FRA to hedge a loan

Enfield Co needs to borrow $8m in 2 months' time for 3 months. It agrees a 2–5 FRA at 5.00% – 4.70% (borrowing rate = 5.00%). Calculate the net effective interest cost if the market rate in 2 months turns out to be (a) 7% or (b) 4%.

FRA compensation: $8m × (rate − 5%) × 3/12+40,000 (from bank)(20,000) (to bank)
Net effective cost(100,000)(100,000)

In both cases the net cost equals $8m × 5% × 3/12 = $100,000 — the company is locked into the 5% FRA rate whichever way the market moves.

Worked example — FRA to hedge a deposit (paying, not receiving, compensation)

F company deposits $1m for 3 months and uses a 5.5% – 5.0% FRA (deposit rate 5.0%, the lower rate). If the market rate turns out to be 5.5%, F company must actually pay the bank compensation to bring its return down to the locked-in 5.0%: 0.5% × $1m × 3/12 = $1,250 payment from F company to the bank.

Exam tip — quick FRA method

You do not need to calculate the actual loan interest separately in most OTQs — the net compensating amount is simply: Notional amount × (Market rate − FRA rate) × time/12. If borrowing and market rate > FRA rate → receive. If borrowing and market rate < FRA rate → pay. (Reverse the direction for a deposit/lending FRA.)

Practice: Greg Co needs a $10m 6-month loan from 1 October and hedges with an FRA at 6% on 30 June. In 6 months, the market rate is 9%. What is the outcome?

A. $150,000 receivable   B. $150,000 payable   C. $450,000 receivable   D. $450,000 payable

Show answer

Answer: A. Compensation = $10m × (9% − 6%) × 6/12 = $150,000, receivable from the bank because the market rate is above the FRA rate on a loan hedge.

13. Interest Rate Derivatives — Futures, Options, IRGs, Caps/Floors/Collars, Swaps G4(b)

Syllabus reference: Identify the main types of interest rate derivatives used to hedge interest rate risk and explain how they are used in hedging. No numerical questions will be set on this topic — description only.

Interest rate futures

Standardised, exchange-traded contracts that lock in an effective interest rate. Like currency futures, they have standard contract sizes and dates, hedge against both favourable and adverse rate movements (no flexibility), and can be closed out (traded) at any time before expiry — although the ideal close-out date is expiry itself, when the futures price converges with the spot rate. Borrowers sell futures to hedge against a rate rise (and buy them back later); depositors/lenders buy futures to hedge against a rate fall.

Interest rate options

The right, but not the obligation, to deal at a set interest rate. A premium is paid upfront regardless of use. Like currency options, they protect against adverse movements while preserving the ability to benefit from favourable ones.

Interest rate guarantees (IRGs)

An IRG is essentially an option on an FRA — it gives the company the right (for a fee) to enter into an FRA at a pre-agreed rate, or to walk away and deal at the market rate if that is more favourable.

SituationBest instrumentWhy
Confident rates will riseFRACheaper — no premium, fully locks in the rate
Uncertain which way rates will moveIRGMore expensive, but allows benefit from a favourable fall

Interest rate caps, floors, and collars

These use interest rate options to set boundaries on the rate paid or received:

  • Cap — sets a maximum interest rate (useful for a borrower). If actual rates are lower, the cap option lapses and the borrower enjoys the lower market rate.
  • Floor — sets a minimum interest rate (useful for a depositor/investor). If actual rates are higher, the floor option lapses and the investor enjoys the higher market rate.
  • Collar — combines both a cap and a floor to create a confined range. A borrower buys a cap (to fix the maximum they'll pay) and simultaneously sells a floor (receiving a premium that partly offsets the cost of the cap, but giving up the benefit of very low rates). A depositor does the opposite — buys a floor and sells a cap.

Why use a collar rather than just a cap?

Buying an option (cap) alone costs a premium. By simultaneously selling the opposite option (floor), the company receives a premium that reduces (or in some cases eliminates) the net cost of the hedge — in exchange for giving up some of the potential upside if rates move very favourably.

Interest rate swaps

An agreement between two parties to exchange a stream of interest payments — typically fixed for floating, or vice versa — calculated on a notional principal amount. Only the interest cash flows are exchanged; the principal itself is never exchanged, and the original borrower remains legally liable to its original lender even after entering a swap.

Worked example — why use a swap?

A company has a $200m floating-rate loan. The treasurer believes rates will rise over the next 5 years and wants certainty. Rather than repaying the loan early (incurring penalty charges) and taking out a new fixed-rate loan, the company enters a 5-year interest rate swap, exchanging its floating payments for fixed payments with a counterparty. This can be cheaper than restructuring the actual borrowing.

A currency swap is a variant where the parties swap debt obligations denominated in different currencies rather than just different interest rate types.

B. A downward-sloping curve suggests the market expects interest rates to fall in future.

Q5. Which TWO statements about the interest rate hedging technique of "smoothing" are true?
A. The debt portfolio will consist of a mixture of fixed and floating rate debt
B. Interest payments will still increase if interest rates rise
C. Investments with a fixed cash flow will be financed with fixed rate debt
D. Full benefit will be obtained from a fall in interest rates

Q6. A company has a $14m variable-rate loan and buys a 4–11 FRA at 2.85% – 2.35%. If the actual interest rate turns out to be 3.75%, what amount is received from the bank under the FRA (to the nearest dollar)?

Show answer

Period = 7 months (month 4 to month 11). Borrowing rate fixed by FRA = 2.85% (the higher rate, since the company is borrowing).
Difference = 3.75% − 2.85% = 0.90%.
Amount received = $14m × 0.9% × 7/12 = $73,500.

Show answer

Base = £, counter = €. 3-month rates: £ = 5.75%/4 = 1.4375%; € = 4.75%/4 = 1.1875%.

Sterling (higher rate) depreciates slightly on the forward market, consistent with IRPT.

Q11. Discursive practice (plan your answer): "Discuss THREE traditional methods a company could use to manage transaction risk arising from a foreign currency receivable, and explain ONE limitation of each." (10 marks — allow yourself 18 minutes)

Show model answer points

1. Forward exchange contract — locks in a rate today for settlement on the receipt date, removing all uncertainty. Limitation: binding, so the company cannot benefit if the exchange rate moves favourably.

2. Money market hedge — the company borrows the foreign currency now (against the future receipt), converts to home currency, and deposits/uses it immediately. Limitation: requires the company to have (or be able to arrange) borrowing facilities in the foreign currency, and brings forward the cash flow, which may not suit the company's cash position.

3. Currency option — gives the right to sell the currency at a guaranteed rate but allows the company to deal at spot if more favourable. Limitation: a non-refundable premium must be paid upfront regardless of whether the option is exercised, making it a relatively expensive hedge.

Q12. Discursive practice: "Explain the difference between an interest rate cap and an interest rate collar, and why a company might prefer a collar." (5 marks)

Show model answer points

A cap uses a purchased option to set a maximum interest rate a borrower will pay — if rates fall below the cap, the option lapses and the borrower pays the lower market rate. The premium is a cost.

A collar combines the purchase of a cap with the simultaneous sale of a floor. The premium received from selling the floor offsets some (or all) of the cost of buying the cap, reducing the net cost of the hedge. In exchange, the company gives up the ability to benefit fully if interest rates fall below the floor rate, since it must then pay compensation to the floor buyer.

A company might prefer a collar when it wants to reduce the net premium cost of hedging and is willing to accept a limited (rather than unlimited) benefit from favourable interest rate movements.
