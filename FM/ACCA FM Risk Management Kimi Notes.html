5.3 Internal Hedging Techniques for Interest Rate Risk

5.3.1 Matching

Match floating-rate assets with floating-rate liabilities, and fixed-rate assets with fixed-rate liabilities. If a company has floating-rate investments and floating-rate borrowings, the interest rate movements may naturally offset each other.

5.3.2 Smoothing

Maintain a mix of fixed-rate and floating-rate debt. This ensures that the company is never fully exposed to either rising or falling interest rates. For example, 50% fixed and 50% floating provides a natural hedge.

5.3.3 Asset and Liability Management

Choose assets and liabilities with matching durations and repricing characteristics. If a company knows it will receive cash in 6 months, it can arrange a loan that reprices or matures at the same time.

Advantages of Internal Techniques: No external costs, no complex documentation, no counterparty risk.

Disadvantages: May not perfectly eliminate risk; opportunities may be limited by the company's existing asset/liability structure.

5.4 Forward Rate Agreements (FRAs)

A Forward Rate Agreement is an over-the-counter contract that fixes the interest rate on a notional principal amount for a future period. It is used to hedge against adverse interest rate movements on planned borrowing or lending.

Key Feature: An FRA is a binding agreement on a notional amount. No actual loan takes place. The borrower/lender still deals with their bank, but the FRA provider compensates them if rates move against them (or vice versa).

FRA Notation

FRAs are quoted as 3v6, 6v9, 3v9, etc.

  • 3v6 = Starts in 3 months, covers a 3-month period (ends in 6 months)
  • 6v9 = Starts in 6 months, covers a 3-month period (ends in 9 months)
  • 3v9 = Starts in 3 months, covers a 6-month period (ends in 9 months)
FORMULA
FRA Settlement:

Settlement = Notional x (Reference Rate - FRA Rate) x (Period / 12) / (1 + Reference Rate x Period/12)

If Reference Rate > FRA Rate: Borrower RECEIVES compensation
If Reference Rate < FRA Rate: Borrower PAYS compensation

Example: FRA for a Borrower

Scenario: A company plans to borrow GBP 5 million for 6 months, starting in 3 months. It is worried that interest rates will rise.

Current 3-month rate: 4%

FRA rate (3v9): 5%

Notional amount: GBP 5,000,000

Step 1: In 3 months, the reference rate (say LIBOR) is 6.5%.

Step 2: Calculate the settlement

Settlement = GBP 5,000,000 x (6.5% - 5%) x (6/12) / (1 + 6.5% x 6/12)

Settlement = GBP 5,000,000 x 1.5% x 0.5 / 1.0325

Settlement = GBP 37,500 / 1.0325 = GBP 36,320

Step 3: The company receives GBP 36,320 from the FRA provider.

Step 4: The company borrows GBP 5,000,000 at the market rate of 6.5%

Interest cost = GBP 5,000,000 x 6.5% x 6/12 = GBP 162,500

Net cost: GBP 162,500 - GBP 36,320 = GBP 126,180

Effective rate: GBP 126,180 / GBP 5,000,000 x (12/6) = 5.05% (approximately the FRA rate)

Advantages of FRAs:
  • Simple and easy to arrange
  • No premium payable
  • Can be tailored to exact amounts and periods
  • Off-balance sheet (no actual loan)
Disadvantages:
  • Binding obligation - cannot benefit if rates fall
  • Counterparty risk
  • Only available for major currencies and standard periods

5.5 Interest Rate Futures

Interest rate futures are standardised exchange-traded contracts to buy or sell a notional short-term deposit at a future date. The price is quoted as 100 minus the implied interest rate.

Key Concept: If interest rates RISE, futures prices FALL. If interest rates FALL, futures prices RISE. This inverse relationship is crucial for determining whether to go LONG or SHORT.

Position to Take

If you are...And fear rates will...You should...Because...
A borrowerRiseSELL futures (go SHORT)If rates rise, futures price falls, and your short position gains
An investor/lenderFallBUY futures (go LONG)If rates fall, futures price rises, and your long position gains
FORMULA
Number of Contracts:

Contracts = (Loan amount x Loan period in months) / (Contract size x Contract period in months)

Tick Value:
Tick size is usually 0.01% (one basis point)
Tick value = Contract size x Period x 0.0001

For a 3-month contract of GBP 500,000:
Tick value = GBP 500,000 x (3/12) x 0.0001 = GBP 12.50

Example: Interest Rate Futures for a Borrower

Scenario: A company will borrow GBP 10 million for 6 months starting in 3 months. It fears rates will rise.

Current 3-month rate: 4%

Futures price (3-month contract): 96.00 (implied rate = 4%)

Contract size: GBP 500,000

Contract period: 3 months

Step 1: Calculate number of contracts

Contracts = (GBP 10,000,000 x 6) / (GBP 500,000 x 3) = 60,000,000 / 1,500,000 = 40 contracts

Step 2: Determine position

Company is a borrower fearing rate rises, so SELL 40 contracts at 96.00

Step 3: Outcome in 3 months

Assume the reference rate is now 5.5% and futures price is 94.50

Gain on futures: (96.00 - 94.50) = 1.50 = 150 ticks

Gain = 150 ticks x GBP 12.50 x 40 contracts = GBP 75,000

Interest on loan: GBP 10,000,000 x 5.5% x 6/12 = GBP 275,000

Net interest cost: GBP 275,000 - GBP 75,000 = GBP 200,000

Effective rate: GBP 200,000 / GBP 10,000,000 x (12/6) = 4.0% (approximately the hedged rate)

Advantages of Interest Rate Futures:
  • Liquid and standardised
  • Low counterparty risk (exchange-traded)
  • Flexible closing - can close position before maturity
Disadvantages:
  • Standardised contract sizes and dates may not match exact needs
  • Basis risk (futures price may not perfectly track actual borrowing rate)
  • Margin calls require cash management
  • Only whole contracts allowed

5.6 Interest Rate Options

Interest rate options (often called interest rate guarantees or caps/floors) give the holder the right, but not the obligation, to fix an interest rate for a future period.

Types of Interest Rate Options

TypeRight to...Use When...
Call Option on interest rate futuresBuy futures (benefit from falling rates)Investor fearing rate falls
Put Option on interest rate futuresSell futures (benefit from rising rates)Borrower fearing rate rises
Interest Rate CapSets a MAXIMUM interest rateBorrower - limits cost if rates rise
Interest Rate FloorSets a MINIMUM interest rateInvestor - limits loss if rates fall

Example: Interest Rate Cap

Scenario: A company has a GBP 5 million floating-rate loan. It buys an interest rate cap at 6% for a premium of 0.5% of the notional amount.

Premium: GBP 5,000,000 x 0.5% = GBP 25,000 (paid upfront)

Scenario A: Market rate rises to 8%

  • Without cap: Interest = GBP 5,000,000 x 8% = GBP 400,000
  • With cap: Interest = GBP 5,000,000 x 6% = GBP 300,000
  • Cap provider pays: GBP 100,000
  • Net cost = GBP 300,000 + GBP 25,000 premium = GBP 325,000

Scenario B: Market rate falls to 4%

  • Without cap: Interest = GBP 5,000,000 x 4% = GBP 200,000
  • With cap: The company lets the cap expire and pays market rate
  • Net cost = GBP 200,000 + GBP 25,000 premium = GBP 225,000

Key Insight: The cap guarantees a maximum rate of 6% plus premium, but allows the company to benefit if rates fall below 6%.

5.7 Interest Rate Swaps

An interest rate swap is an agreement between two parties to exchange fixed-rate interest payments for floating-rate interest payments on a notional principal amount over a period of time.

Key Principle: In an interest rate swap, the notional principal is not exchanged. Only the interest payments are swapped. One party pays fixed and receives floating; the other pays floating and receives fixed.

Why Use Interest Rate Swaps?

  1. Transform liability structure: Convert fixed-rate debt to floating, or floating to fixed
  2. Comparative advantage: Exploit differences in borrowing costs between fixed and floating markets
  3. Manage risk: Align interest rate exposure with risk appetite

Example: Comparative Advantage and Swap

Company A (highly rated) can borrow at:

  • Fixed: 5%
  • Floating: LIBOR + 0.5%

Company B (lower rated) can borrow at:

  • Fixed: 7%
  • Floating: LIBOR + 1.5%

Comparative advantage:

  • Company A has an absolute advantage in both markets
  • But the difference in fixed rates is 2% (7% - 5%)
  • The difference in floating rates is only 1% (LIBOR+1.5% - LIBOR+0.5%)
  • So Company A has a comparative advantage in fixed-rate borrowing
  • Company B has a comparative advantage in floating-rate borrowing (or rather, less disadvantage)

Total potential gain from swap: 2% - 1% = 1%

Swap arrangement:

  1. Company A borrows fixed at 5%
  2. Company B borrows floating at LIBOR + 1.5%
  3. They enter a swap where:
    • Company A pays LIBOR to Company B
    • Company B pays 6% fixed to Company A

Net results:

Company A:

  • Pays 5% fixed to bank
  • Pays LIBOR to Company B
  • Receives 6% from Company B
  • Net cost: 5% + LIBOR - 6% = LIBOR - 1%
  • Saving vs direct floating: (LIBOR + 0.5%) - (LIBOR - 1%) = 1.5%

Company B:

  • Pays LIBOR + 1.5% to bank
  • Pays 6% fixed to Company A
  • Receives LIBOR from Company A
  • Net cost: LIBOR + 1.5% + 6% - LIBOR = 7.5% fixed
  • Saving vs direct fixed: 7% - 7.5% = -0.5% (wait, this needs adjustment)

Better swap terms: If they split the 1% gain equally (0.5% each):

  • Company B should pay 5.5% fixed to A
  • Company A net cost = 5% + LIBOR - 5.5% = LIBOR - 0.5% (saving 1%)
  • Company B net cost = LIBOR + 1.5% + 5.5% - LIBOR = 7.0% fixed (saving 0%)

Actually, with 5.5%: A saves 1%, B saves 0%. To split 0.5% each: B pays 6% to A.

Company A net = 5% + LIBOR - 6% = LIBOR - 1% (saving 1.5% vs LIBOR+0.5%)

Company B net = LIBOR + 1.5% + 6% - LIBOR = 7.5% (costing 0.5% MORE than direct fixed)

This shows the importance of structuring swaps correctly. In practice, a bank intermediary would structure it so both parties benefit.

5.8 Caps, Floors and Collars

InstrumentWhat It DoesUsed ByCost
CapSets a MAXIMUM interest rateBorrowerPremium paid
FloorSets a MINIMUM interest rateInvestor/LenderPremium paid
CollarCombines a cap and a floor. Rate is capped at maximum but also floored at minimum.Borrower or InvestorPremium on cap is offset by premium received on floor

Example: Interest Rate Collar

Scenario: A company has a floating-rate loan. It wants protection against rate rises but finds a cap too expensive.

Strategy: Buy a cap at 7% and sell a floor at 4%

  • Cap premium: 1.0% of notional
  • Floor premium received: 0.8% of notional
  • Net premium: 0.2% (much cheaper than the cap alone)

Outcomes:

  • If rates rise above 7%: Company pays maximum 7% (cap protects)
  • If rates fall below 4%: Company pays 4% (floor obligates)
  • If rates are between 4% and 7%: Company pays market rate

Trade-off: The company sacrifices the benefit of very low rates (below 4%) in exchange for cheaper protection against high rates.

6. Value at Risk (VaR)

Value at Risk (VaR) is a statistical measure of the maximum potential loss on a portfolio over a specific time period, at a given confidence level.

FORMULA
VaR Definition:

"We are X% confident that we will not lose more than $Y over the next N days."

Where:
X% = Confidence level (e.g., 95%, 99%)
$Y = VaR amount
N = Time horizon

Methods of Calculating VaR

6.1 Historical Simulation

Uses actual historical data to estimate potential losses. The steps are:

  1. Collect historical returns data (e.g., last 250 trading days)
  2. Sort the returns from worst to best
  3. At 95% confidence, the VaR is the 5th percentile (12.5th worst day out of 250)
  4. At 99% confidence, the VaR is the 1st percentile (2.5th worst day)

Example: Historical Simulation VaR

A portfolio is worth GBP 10 million. Historical daily returns over 250 days show the worst 5 returns as:

  • -2.5%, -2.1%, -1.8%, -1.5%, -1.3%...

95% confidence VaR (1-day): The 5th percentile of 250 days = the 12.5th worst day, approximately -1.5%

VaR amount = GBP 10,000,000 x 1.5% = GBP 150,000

Interpretation: "We are 95% confident that we will not lose more than GBP 150,000 in one day."

6.2 Variance-Covariance (Parametric) Method

Assumes returns are normally distributed. Uses the standard deviation of returns.

FORMULA
VaR = Portfolio Value x Z-score x Standard Deviation x sqrt(Time)

For 95% confidence: Z = 1.645
For 99% confidence: Z = 2.33

Standard Deviation = Daily standard deviation of returns
Time = Time horizon in days (use square root rule for longer periods)

Example: Parametric VaR

Portfolio value: GBP 20 million

Daily standard deviation: 1.2%

Confidence level: 95%

Time horizon: 1 day

Calculation:

VaR = GBP 20,000,000 x 1.645 x 1.2%

VaR = GBP 20,000,000 x 0.01974 = GBP 394,800

For 10-day VaR:

VaR = GBP 394,800 x sqrt(10) = GBP 394,800 x 3.162 = GBP 1,248,358

6.3 Monte Carlo Simulation

Uses computer models to generate thousands of random possible future scenarios based on statistical parameters. More flexible than other methods but computationally intensive.

Limitations of VaR

  • Assumes normal distribution: Financial returns often have "fat tails" (extreme events are more likely than normal distribution suggests)
  • Does not measure maximum loss: It says "we are 95% confident we won't lose more than X" - but the 5% tail risk is not quantified
  • Backward-looking: Historical methods assume the future will be like the past
  • Correlation breakdown: In crises, correlations between assets increase, reducing diversification benefits
VaR is widely used but should be supplemented with:
  • Stress testing (what happens in extreme scenarios?)
  • Scenario analysis
  • Expected Shortfall (CVaR) - measures the average loss in the tail beyond VaR

7. Exam Tips and Common Pitfalls

7.1 Most Common Exam Mistakes

MistakeHow to Avoid
Using the wrong exchange rate (bid vs offer) Always ask: "Am I buying or selling foreign currency?" If buying, use the higher (offer) rate. If selling, use the lower (bid) rate.
Forgetting to annualise or de-annualise interest rates Check whether rates are given as annual or for the period. If annual, divide by the number of periods per year.
Mixing up domestic and foreign rates in IRP/PPP Domestic rate always goes on top, foreign rate on the bottom. If rate is GBP/EUR, UK is domestic, Eurozone is foreign.
Getting futures direction wrong (long vs short) Borrower fearing rate rises = SELL futures. Investor fearing rate falls = BUY futures. For forex: payable = BUY futures, receivable = SELL futures.
Ignoring the premium in option calculations Always include the premium in the total cost/receipt. Premium is paid upfront and is a sunk cost.
Rounding errors in money market hedge Keep full precision through all steps. Only round at the final answer.
Not making a clear recommendation After calculating all hedging methods, ALWAYS state which you recommend and WHY. Consider cost, flexibility, risk appetite, and certainty.

7.2 Time Management Tips

  • Hedging comparison questions can take 15-20 minutes. Set up a clear table format before starting calculations.
  • Write down the formula you are using before substituting numbers - this earns method marks even if your final answer is wrong.
  • For options, always show BOTH scenarios (exercise and lapse) to demonstrate understanding.
  • When comparing hedges, calculate the effective rate or total cost for each method in the same currency.
