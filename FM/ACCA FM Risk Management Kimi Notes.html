1. Introduction to Risk Management

What is Risk?

Risk is the possibility that actual outcomes will differ from expected outcomes. In business and finance, risk refers to the uncertainty surrounding future cash flows, profits, or returns. Every business faces risk, and the key challenge for financial managers is to identify, measure, and manage these risks effectively.

Key Principle: Risk management does NOT mean eliminating all risk. Some risk is necessary for returns. The goal is to manage risk to an acceptable level that aligns with the company's risk appetite.

Why Do Companies Manage Risk?

  1. Protect cash flows - Ensure the company can meet its obligations
  2. Reduce earnings volatility - Make financial planning more reliable
  3. Protect shareholder value - Stable earnings lead to higher valuations
  4. Avoid financial distress - Prevent situations where the company cannot pay its debts
  5. Support investment decisions - Certainty about future cash flows improves decision-making

The Risk Management Process

StepActionDescription
1IdentifyRecognise what risks the company faces
2MeasureQuantify the potential impact of each risk
3EvaluateDecide which risks are acceptable and which need managing
4ManageImplement hedging or other risk reduction strategies
5MonitorRegularly review and adjust risk management strategies

2. Types of Risk

Before we can manage risk, we must understand the different types of risk a company faces. In ACCA FM, we focus on financial risks - those that directly affect the company's cash flows and financial position.

2.1 Business Risk vs Financial Risk

Business Risk (Operating Risk)

This is the risk inherent in the company's operations, even without any debt. It arises from:

  • Variability in sales demand
  • Changes in input prices
  • Competitive pressures
  • Technological changes
  • Regulatory changes

Business risk is measured by operational gearing: the proportion of fixed costs in the cost structure. Higher fixed costs = higher business risk.

Financial Risk

This is the additional risk that arises from using debt financing (financial gearing). When a company borrows money, it must make fixed interest payments regardless of profits. This magnifies the variability of returns to shareholders.

Remember: Total Risk = Business Risk + Financial Risk. Business risk exists in ALL companies. Financial risk only exists in companies that use debt.

2.2 Categories of Financial Risk

Risk TypeWhat It MeansExample
Foreign Exchange Risk The risk that exchange rate movements will affect the value of foreign currency cash flows A UK company importing goods from the US faces the risk that the dollar strengthens, making imports more expensive
Interest Rate Risk The risk that changes in interest rates will affect borrowing costs or investment returns A company with a variable-rate loan faces higher interest payments if rates rise
Credit Risk The risk that a counterparty will fail to meet its obligations A company selling goods on credit faces the risk that the customer does not pay
Liquidity Risk The risk that the company cannot meet its short-term obligations A company cannot pay its suppliers because cash is tied up in inventory
Commodity Price Risk The risk that changes in commodity prices will affect costs or revenues An airline faces higher fuel costs if oil prices rise
EXAM FOCUS ACCA FM concentrates heavily on Foreign Exchange Risk and Interest Rate Risk. You must be able to calculate hedging outcomes using various techniques and recommend the most appropriate method.

3. Foreign Exchange Risk

Foreign exchange (forex) risk is one of the most heavily tested areas in ACCA FM. It arises whenever a company has future cash flows denominated in a foreign currency. Let's build your understanding from the ground up.

3.1 Types of Forex Exposure

Transaction Exposure

This is the risk that the exchange rate will change between the time a transaction is agreed and the time it is settled (cash is actually paid or received).

Example: Transaction Exposure

On 1 January, a UK company agrees to buy machinery from a German supplier for EUR 500,000, payable on 31 March. The spot rate on 1 January is GBP/EUR 1.20 (meaning GBP 1 = EUR 1.20).

Expected cost on 1 January: EUR 500,000 / 1.20 = GBP 416,667

On 31 March, the spot rate has moved to GBP/EUR 1.15 (the pound has weakened - it buys fewer euros).

Actual cost on 31 March: EUR 500,000 / 1.15 = GBP 434,783

Additional cost due to exchange rate movement: GBP 434,783 - GBP 416,667 = GBP 18,116

This is transaction exposure - the company is exposed to exchange rate movements during the period between agreeing the transaction and making payment.

Translation Exposure

This is the risk that the value of a company's foreign assets, liabilities, or subsidiary results will change when translated back into the parent company's reporting currency for consolidation purposes. This is an accounting risk - it affects the balance sheet and income statement but does NOT involve actual cash flows.

Example: Translation Exposure

A UK parent company has a US subsidiary with assets worth USD 10 million. The exchange rate is GBP/USD 1.30.

Value in GBP: USD 10,000,000 / 1.30 = GBP 7,692,308

At year-end, the rate has moved to GBP/USD 1.25.

New value in GBP: USD 10,000,000 / 1.25 = GBP 8,000,000

The GBP value of the subsidiary's net assets has increased by GBP 307,692 due to exchange rate movement. This is a translation gain that appears in the group's financial statements but does not involve any actual cash flow.

Economic Exposure

This is the long-term risk that exchange rate movements will affect a company's competitive position and future cash flows. It is broader than transaction exposure because it considers the impact on the company's overall market position, not just specific transactions.

Example: Economic Exposure

A UK-based car manufacturer exports cars to Europe. If the pound strengthens against the euro:

  • UK cars become MORE EXPENSIVE for European buyers
  • The company may lose market share to European competitors
  • Future sales and profits decline

This is economic exposure - the long-term impact on the company's competitive position and future cash flows, not just a single transaction.

Key Distinction:
  • Transaction exposure = Cash flow risk on specific transactions (short-term)
  • Translation exposure = Accounting risk on consolidated financial statements (no cash flow)
  • Economic exposure = Long-term competitive position and future cash flows

3.2 Exchange Rate Quotations

Understanding how exchange rates are quoted is absolutely essential. There are two ways to quote exchange rates:

Direct Quote vs Indirect Quote

TypeFormatMeaningExample
Direct Quote Domestic / Foreign How much domestic currency you need to buy ONE unit of foreign currency GBP/USD 0.77 means GBP 0.77 buys USD 1
Indirect Quote Foreign / Domestic How much foreign currency you get for ONE unit of domestic currency USD/GBP 1.30 means USD 1.30 buys GBP 1
ACCA Convention: In ACCA FM, exchange rates are typically quoted as GBP/EUR or GBP/USD, which means GBP per unit of foreign currency (direct quote from a UK perspective). So GBP/EUR 0.85 means GBP 0.85 = EUR 1.

Bid-Offer Spread

Banks quote two rates:

  • Bid rate - The rate at which the bank will BUY the base currency from you
  • Offer (Ask) rate - The rate at which the bank will SELL the base currency to you
FORMULA
Bid-Offer Spread: The bank always wins! They buy low and sell high.

If you are BUYING foreign currency, the bank SELLS it to you at the offer rate (higher rate).
If you are SELLING foreign currency, the bank BUYS it from you at the bid rate (lower rate).

Example: Bid-Offer Spread

The bank quotes GBP/USD: 0.7680 - 0.7690

  • Bid: 0.7680 - The bank will buy USD from you at this rate
  • Offer: 0.7690 - The bank will sell USD to you at this rate

Scenario 1: You need to pay USD 100,000 to a US supplier. You are BUYING USD from the bank.

Cost = USD 100,000 x 0.7690 (offer rate) = GBP 76,900

Scenario 2: You have received USD 100,000 from a US customer. You are SELLING USD to the bank.

Receipt = USD 100,000 x 0.7680 (bid rate) = GBP 76,800

Bank's profit (spread): GBP 76,900 - GBP 76,800 = GBP 100

Cross Rates

A cross rate is an exchange rate between two currencies calculated using a third currency (usually USD). This is needed when there is no direct market between two currencies.

FORMULA
Cross Rate Calculation:
If GBP/USD = a and EUR/USD = b, then GBP/EUR = a / b

Example: Cross Rate

Given:

  • GBP/USD = 0.77 (GBP 0.77 = USD 1)
  • EUR/USD = 0.92 (EUR 0.92 = USD 1)

To find GBP/EUR:

GBP/EUR = GBP/USD divided by EUR/USD = 0.77 / 0.92 = 0.8369

So GBP 0.8369 = EUR 1, or equivalently, GBP 1 = EUR 1.195

3.3 Interest Rate Parity (IRP)

Interest Rate Parity is a theory that links the spot exchange rate, forward exchange rate, and interest rates in two countries. It explains why forward rates differ from spot rates.

FORMULA
Interest Rate Parity (IRP):

F = S x (1 + id) / (1 + if)

Where:
F = Forward rate
S = Spot rate
id = Domestic interest rate (for the period)
if = Foreign interest rate (for the period)
Understanding IRP: If the domestic interest rate is HIGHER than the foreign interest rate, the domestic currency will trade at a forward discount (the forward rate will be less favorable than the spot rate). This is because higher interest rates attract capital inflows, which would appreciate the spot rate, but the forward rate adjusts to eliminate arbitrage opportunities.

Example: Interest Rate Parity

Given:

  • Spot rate GBP/USD = 0.77
  • UK interest rate = 5% per year
  • US interest rate = 3% per year
  • Time period = 6 months

Step 1: Convert annual rates to 6-month rates

UK 6-month rate = 5% / 2 = 2.5%

US 6-month rate = 3% / 2 = 1.5%

Step 2: Apply IRP formula

F = 0.77 x (1 + 0.025) / (1 + 0.015)

F = 0.77 x 1.025 / 1.015

F = 0.77 x 1.00985 = 0.7776

Interpretation: The 6-month forward rate is GBP/USD 0.7776. Since the UK interest rate is higher, the pound trades at a forward discount (it takes more pounds to buy a dollar in the forward market).

3.4 Purchasing Power Parity (PPP)

Purchasing Power Parity is a theory that links exchange rates to inflation rates. It states that the exchange rate between two currencies should adjust to reflect differences in inflation rates between the two countries.

FORMULA
Purchasing Power Parity (PPP):

S1 = S0 x (1 + id) / (1 + if)

Where:
S1 = Expected future spot rate
S0 = Current spot rate
id = Domestic inflation rate
if = Foreign inflation rate
Understanding PPP: If a country has HIGHER inflation than another, its currency should DEPRECIATE to maintain purchasing power parity. High inflation erodes the value of money, so you need more of that currency to buy the same goods.

Example: Purchasing Power Parity

Given:

  • Current spot rate GBP/EUR = 0.85
  • UK inflation = 3% per year
  • Eurozone inflation = 1.5% per year

Step 1: Apply PPP formula for expected rate in one year

S1 = 0.85 x (1 + 0.03) / (1 + 0.015)

S1 = 0.85 x 1.03 / 1.015

S1 = 0.85 x 1.01478 = 0.8626

Interpretation: The expected spot rate in one year is GBP/EUR 0.8626. Since UK inflation is higher, the pound is expected to depreciate (it takes more pounds to buy a euro).

Important: PPP is a long-term theory. In the short term, exchange rates can deviate significantly from PPP due to speculation, capital flows, and other factors.

3.5 Forecasting Future Exchange Rates

In the exam, you may need to forecast future exchange rates using either IRP or PPP. The key is to know which theory to apply:

TheoryUse WhenKey Input
IRPCalculating forward rates or when interest rate differentials are givenInterest rates
PPPForecasting long-term spot rates or when inflation differentials are givenInflation rates
Exam Trap: Make sure you use the correct rates in the formula. If the rate is quoted as GBP/EUR, then UK is the "domestic" country and Eurozone is the "foreign" country. The domestic rate goes on top, foreign rate goes on the bottom.

4. Hedging Foreign Exchange Risk

Hedging means taking action to reduce or eliminate the risk of adverse exchange rate movements. There are two broad categories:

  • Internal hedging - Using the company's own resources and operations
  • External hedging - Using financial instruments and markets

4.1 Internal Hedging Techniques

4.1.1 Invoice in Home Currency

The simplest approach: invoice foreign customers in your own currency and ask foreign suppliers to invoice you in your own currency. This transfers the exchange rate risk to the other party.

Example: Invoicing in Home Currency

A UK company sells goods to a US customer for USD 100,000. Instead of invoicing in USD, the UK company invoices in GBP 77,000 (at current rate GBP/USD 0.77).

Advantage: The UK company knows exactly how much GBP it will receive - GBP 77,000. No exchange rate risk.

Disadvantage: The US customer bears the exchange rate risk. The US customer may refuse to deal with the UK company or demand a price discount. Also, the UK company may lose competitive advantage if competitors are willing to invoice in USD.

4.1.2 Leading and Lagging

This involves changing the timing of foreign currency payments or receipts based on expected exchange rate movements.

  • Leading: Pay early if you expect the foreign currency to STRENGTHEN (making future payments more expensive)
  • Lagging: Delay payment if you expect the foreign currency to WEAKEN (making future payments cheaper)

Example: Leading and Lagging

A UK company owes EUR 500,000 to a German supplier, due in 3 months. Current spot rate: GBP/EUR 0.85.

Scenario A: The UK company expects the euro to strengthen (GBP/EUR rate to increase, meaning more pounds needed per euro).

Action: LEAD - Pay early to lock in the current favorable rate.

Scenario B: The UK company expects the euro to weaken (GBP/EUR rate to decrease).

Action: LAG - Delay payment hoping the euro weakens, reducing the GBP cost.

4.1.3 Netting

If a company has both payables and receivables in the same foreign currency, it can net them off and only hedge the net exposure.

Example: Netting

A UK company has:

  • EUR payable of EUR 800,000 (to a German supplier)
  • EUR receivable of EUR 500,000 (from a German customer)

Gross exposure: EUR 800,000 + EUR 500,000 = EUR 1,300,000

Net exposure: EUR 800,000 - EUR 500,000 = EUR 300,000 payable

The company only needs to hedge EUR 300,000, not the full EUR 800,000. This reduces hedging costs significantly.

4.1.4 Matching

Creating foreign currency assets to match foreign currency liabilities. For example, borrowing in the same currency as your receivables.

4.1.5 Offsetting Assets and Liabilities

Creating a foreign currency asset (like a bank deposit) to offset a foreign currency liability (like a payable).

Advantages of Internal Hedging: No external costs, no complex instruments, easy to implement.

Disadvantages: May not be possible for all transactions, may damage supplier/customer relationships, limited effectiveness.

4.2 Forward Contracts

A forward contract is an agreement to buy or sell a specific amount of foreign currency at a predetermined exchange rate on a specific future date.

Key Feature: A forward contract is a binding obligation. Once entered into, both parties MUST complete the transaction at the agreed rate, regardless of what the spot rate is on the settlement date.
FORMULA
Forward Contract Outcome:

Amount in domestic currency = Foreign currency amount x Forward rate

(Remember to use the correct bid or offer rate depending on whether you are buying or selling)

Example: Forward Contract - Payable

Scenario: A UK company must pay USD 500,000 to a US supplier in 3 months.

Current spot rate: GBP/USD 0.7680 - 0.7690

3-month forward rate: GBP/USD 0.7650 - 0.7660

Step 1: The company needs to BUY USD in 3 months. The bank will SELL USD at the offer rate.

Step 2: Use the forward offer rate: GBP/USD 0.7660

Step 3: Calculate the guaranteed GBP cost

GBP cost = USD 500,000 x 0.7660 = GBP 383,000

Result: The company has locked in a cost of GBP 383,000. Regardless of where the spot rate moves in 3 months, the company knows exactly what it will pay.

Example: Forward Contract - Receivable

Scenario: A UK company will receive EUR 400,000 from a German customer in 6 months.

Current spot rate: GBP/EUR 0.8480 - 0.8490

6-month forward rate: GBP/EUR 0.8520 - 0.8530

Step 1: The company will SELL EUR in 6 months. The bank will BUY EUR at the bid rate.

Step 2: Use the forward bid rate: GBP/EUR 0.8520

Step 3: Calculate the guaranteed GBP receipt

GBP receipt = EUR 400,000 x 0.8520 = GBP 340,800

Result: The company has locked in a receipt of GBP 340,800. Even if the euro weakens significantly, the company is protected.

Advantages of Forward Contracts:
  • Simple and easy to understand
  • Provides certainty - you know exactly what you will pay/receive
  • No upfront premium or cost (the cost is embedded in the forward rate)
  • Can be tailored to exact amounts and dates
Disadvantages:
  • Binding obligation - you cannot benefit if the rate moves in your favor
  • May require credit lines or collateral
  • Not available for all currencies or all time periods
  • Counterparty risk (though minimal with banks)

4.3 Money Market Hedge

A money market hedge uses the domestic and foreign money markets (borrowing and lending) to create a synthetic forward contract. It achieves the same outcome as a forward contract but uses interest rate differentials instead.

Money Market Hedge for a PAYABLE

If you owe foreign currency in the future:

  1. Calculate the present value of the foreign currency payable using the foreign deposit rate
  2. Borrow that amount in domestic currency today
  3. Convert the borrowed domestic currency to foreign currency at the spot rate
  4. Deposit the foreign currency to earn interest until the payment date
  5. On the payment date, the deposit (with interest) equals the payable amount

Example: Money Market Hedge - Payable

Scenario: A UK company must pay USD 1,000,000 in 6 months.

Spot rate: GBP/USD 0.77 (bid) - 0.7710 (offer)

UK interest rate (borrowing): 5% per year

US interest rate (deposit): 3% per year

Step 1: Calculate the present value of USD 1,000,000

PV = USD 1,000,000 / (1 + 0.03/2) = USD 1,000,000 / 1.015 = USD 985,221.67

Step 2: Convert USD to GBP at the spot rate

The company needs to BUY USD, so use the offer rate: GBP/USD 0.7710

GBP needed = USD 985,221.67 x 0.7710 = GBP 759,605.91

Step 3: Borrow GBP 759,605.91 in the UK at 5% per year

Repayment in 6 months = GBP 759,605.91 x (1 + 0.05/2) = GBP 759,605.91 x 1.025 = GBP 778,596.06

Step 4: Deposit USD 985,221.67 in the US at 3% per year

Value in 6 months = USD 985,221.67 x 1.015 = USD 1,000,000 (exactly the payable!)

Result: The effective cost is GBP 778,596.06. This is the money market hedge outcome.

Money Market Hedge for a RECEIVABLE

If you will receive foreign currency in the future:

  1. Borrow the present value of the foreign currency receivable using the foreign borrowing rate
  2. Convert the borrowed foreign currency to domestic currency at the spot rate
  3. Use the domestic currency as needed (or invest it)
  4. On the receipt date, the foreign currency receivable pays off the foreign currency loan

Example: Money Market Hedge - Receivable

Scenario: A UK company will receive EUR 800,000 in 3 months.

Spot rate: GBP/EUR 0.8480 (bid) - 0.8490 (offer)

UK interest rate (deposit): 4% per year

Eurozone interest rate (borrowing): 2% per year

Step 1: Calculate the present value of EUR 800,000

PV = EUR 800,000 / (1 + 0.02/4) = EUR 800,000 / 1.005 = EUR 796,019.90

Step 2: Borrow EUR 796,019.90 in the Eurozone

Step 3: Convert EUR to GBP at the spot rate

The company is SELLING EUR, so use the bid rate: GBP/EUR 0.8480

GBP received = EUR 796,019.90 x 0.8480 = GBP 675,024.88

Step 4: In 3 months, the EUR receivable of EUR 800,000 pays off the EUR loan

EUR loan repayment = EUR 796,019.90 x 1.005 = EUR 800,000 (exactly!)

Result: The company has GBP 675,024.88 today (or can invest it). The effective locked-in rate is equivalent to the money market hedge outcome.

Money Market Hedge vs Forward Contract:
  • In theory, both should give very similar outcomes due to IRP
  • In practice, small differences may exist due to market imperfections
  • Money market hedge is more complex but may be available when forward markets are not
  • Forward contracts are simpler and more commonly used

4.4 Currency Futures

Currency futures are standardised contracts traded on an exchange to buy or sell a specific amount of currency at a future date. Unlike forward contracts, futures are:

  • Standardised (fixed contract sizes and settlement dates)
  • Traded on exchanges (not over-the-counter)
  • Marked-to-market daily (daily settlement of gains/losses)
  • Require margin deposits
Key Concept: Futures contracts are used to lock in an exchange rate, similar to forwards. However, because futures are standardised, the hedge is rarely perfect (there may be over-hedge or under-hedge). The difference between the futures rate and the spot rate on the settlement date determines the gain or loss on the futures position.

How Currency Futures Work

If you have...You should...Because...
A foreign currency PAYABLEBUY futures (go LONG)You need to buy foreign currency in the future; a long futures position gains if the foreign currency strengthens
A foreign currency RECEIVABLESELL futures (go SHORT)You will sell foreign currency in the future; a short futures position gains if the foreign currency weakens
FORMULA
Number of Contracts:

Number of contracts = (Amount to hedge / Contract size) x (Rate conversion if needed)

Round to the nearest whole number. The hedge may not be perfect.

Example: Currency Futures - Payable

Scenario: A UK company must pay USD 2,000,000 in 4 months.

Current spot rate: GBP/USD 0.77

Futures price (4-month): GBP/USD 0.7680

Contract size: GBP 62,500 per contract

Step 1: Determine the position

The company needs to BUY USD in the future, so it should BUY (go LONG) GBP/USD futures.

Step 2: Calculate number of contracts

USD 2,000,000 at futures rate 0.7680 = GBP 1,536,000

Number of contracts = GBP 1,536,000 / GBP 62,500 = 24.576 contracts

Round to 25 contracts (slight over-hedge)

Step 3: Outcome at maturity

Assume the spot rate in 4 months is GBP/USD 0.7750 (USD has strengthened - bad for the company)

Without hedge: Cost = USD 2,000,000 x 0.7750 = GBP 1,550,000

Futures gain: (0.7750 - 0.7680) x 25 x GBP 62,500 = 0.0070 x GBP 1,562,500 = GBP 10,937.50 gain

Net cost with futures: GBP 1,550,000 - GBP 10,937.50 = GBP 1,539,062.50

This is close to the futures-locked rate of GBP 1,536,000. The small difference is due to the over-hedge (25 contracts instead of 24.576).

Example: Currency Futures - Receivable

Scenario: A UK company will receive EUR 1,500,000 in 3 months.

Current spot rate: GBP/EUR 0.85

Futures price (3-month): GBP/EUR 0.8480

Contract size: EUR 125,000 per contract

Step 1: Determine the position

The company will SELL EUR in the future, so it should SELL (go SHORT) GBP/EUR futures.

Step 2: Calculate number of contracts

Number of contracts = EUR 1,500,000 / EUR 125,000 = 12 contracts (perfect hedge!)

Step 3: Outcome at maturity

Assume the spot rate in 3 months is GBP/EUR 0.8400 (EUR has weakened - bad for the company)

Without hedge: Receipt = EUR 1,500,000 x 0.8400 = GBP 1,260,000

Futures gain: (0.8480 - 0.8400) x 12 x EUR 125,000 = 0.0080 x EUR 1,500,000 = EUR 12,000

Convert futures gain to GBP: EUR 12,000 x 0.8400 = GBP 10,080 gain

Net receipt with futures: GBP 1,260,000 + GBP 10,080 = GBP 1,270,080

This is close to the futures-locked receipt of EUR 1,500,000 x 0.8480 = GBP 1,272,000.

Advantages of Futures:
  • Liquid market - easy to close position before maturity
  • Standardised and regulated
  • Lower counterparty risk (exchange guarantees)
Disadvantages:
  • Standardised contract sizes may not match exact hedging needs
  • Daily margin calls require cash management
  • More complex than forwards
  • Basis risk (futures price may not perfectly track spot price)

4.5 Currency Options

Currency options give the holder the RIGHT but NOT the OBLIGATION to buy (call option) or sell (put option) a currency at a predetermined rate (the strike price) on or before a specific date.

Key Difference from Forwards/Futures: Options provide downside protection while allowing you to benefit from favorable rate movements. This flexibility comes at a cost - the option premium.

Types of Currency Options

Option TypeGives the right to...Use when you have...
Call OptionBUY the foreign currencyA foreign currency PAYABLE
Put OptionSELL the foreign currencyA foreign currency RECEIVABLE

Option Terminology

TermMeaning
Strike Price (Exercise Price)The rate at which you can buy/sell the currency
PremiumThe cost of the option (paid upfront, non-refundable)
Expiry DateThe last date the option can be exercised
In-the-MoneyExercising the option would be profitable
Out-of-the-MoneyExercising the option would NOT be profitable
At-the-MoneyStrike price equals current spot price

Example: Currency Call Option - Payable

Scenario: A UK company must pay USD 1,000,000 in 3 months.

Current spot rate: GBP/USD 0.77

Option details:

  • Call option (right to buy USD)
  • Strike price: GBP/USD 0.7750
  • Premium: GBP 0.0080 per USD

Step 1: Calculate the premium cost

Premium = USD 1,000,000 x GBP 0.0080 = GBP 8,000 (paid upfront)

Step 2: Analyze outcomes at expiry

Scenario A: Spot rate in 3 months = GBP/USD 0.7850 (USD strengthened - bad!)

  • Spot cost would be: USD 1,000,000 x 0.7850 = GBP 785,000
  • Exercise the option: USD 1,000,000 x 0.7750 = GBP 775,000
  • Exercise the option! Save GBP 10,000 compared to spot
  • Total cost = GBP 775,000 + GBP 8,000 premium = GBP 783,000

Scenario B: Spot rate in 3 months = GBP/USD 0.7600 (USD weakened - good!)

  • Spot cost would be: USD 1,000,000 x 0.7600 = GBP 760,000
  • Option exercise cost: USD 1,000,000 x 0.7750 = GBP 775,000
  • Let the option lapse! Buy at the cheaper spot rate
  • Total cost = GBP 760,000 + GBP 8,000 premium = GBP 768,000

Key Insight: The option protects against USD strengthening (Scenario A) but allows the company to benefit if USD weakens (Scenario B). The maximum cost is capped at GBP 783,000.

Example: Currency Put Option - Receivable

Scenario: A UK company will receive EUR 2,000,000 in 6 months.

Current spot rate: GBP/EUR 0.85

Option details:

  • Put option (right to sell EUR)
  • Strike price: GBP/EUR 0.8450
  • Premium: GBP 0.0120 per EUR

Step 1: Calculate the premium cost

Premium = EUR 2,000,000 x GBP 0.0120 = GBP 24,000 (paid upfront)

Step 2: Analyze outcomes at expiry

Scenario A: Spot rate in 6 months = GBP/EUR 0.8300 (EUR weakened - bad!)

  • Spot receipt would be: EUR 2,000,000 x 0.8300 = GBP 1,660,000
  • Exercise the option: EUR 2,000,000 x 0.8450 = GBP 1,690,000
  • Exercise the option! Gain GBP 30,000 compared to spot
  • Net receipt = GBP 1,690,000 - GBP 24,000 premium = GBP 1,666,000

Scenario B: Spot rate in 6 months = GBP/EUR 0.8600 (EUR strengthened - good!)

  • Spot receipt would be: EUR 2,000,000 x 0.8600 = GBP 1,720,000
  • Option exercise receipt: EUR 2,000,000 x 0.8450 = GBP 1,690,000
  • Let the option lapse! Sell at the better spot rate
  • Net receipt = GBP 1,720,000 - GBP 24,000 premium = GBP 1,696,000

Key Insight: The put option guarantees a minimum receipt while allowing the company to benefit if EUR strengthens.

Advantages of Options:
  • Flexibility - can choose whether to exercise
  • Protection against adverse movements
  • Ability to benefit from favorable movements
  • Maximum loss is limited to the premium paid
Disadvantages:
  • Premium cost (can be expensive)
  • More complex than forwards
  • Premium is paid upfront and is a sunk cost
  • Over-the-counter options may have credit risk

4.6 Currency Swaps

A currency swap is an agreement between two parties to exchange principal and interest payments in different currencies over a period of time.

How a Currency Swap Works

  1. Two parties exchange principal amounts in different currencies at the start (at the prevailing spot rate)
  2. During the life of the swap, they exchange interest payments
  3. At the end, they re-exchange the principal amounts at the same rate as the initial exchange

Example: Currency Swap

Scenario: A UK company wants to borrow USD 10 million for 5 years to fund a US subsidiary. A US company wants to borrow GBP 7.7 million for 5 years to fund a UK subsidiary.

Spot rate: GBP/USD 0.77

UK interest rate: 5%

US interest rate: 3%

Without swap:

  • UK company borrows USD at US rate + spread (maybe 4%)
  • US company borrows GBP at UK rate + spread (maybe 6%)

With swap:

  • UK company borrows GBP 7.7 million in UK at 5%
  • US company borrows USD 10 million in US at 3%
  • They swap: UK company gets USD 10 million, US company gets GBP 7.7 million
  • UK company pays 3% on USD to US company
  • US company pays 5% on GBP to UK company
  • Both benefit from lower borrowing costs in their home markets

Result: Both companies effectively borrow in the foreign currency at better rates than they could achieve directly.

Advantages of Currency Swaps:
  • Access to cheaper borrowing in foreign markets
  • Hedge long-term foreign currency exposures
  • Can be structured to match exact needs
Disadvantages:
  • Counterparty risk
  • Complex to arrange
  • Difficult to terminate early

In the exam, you may be asked to compare different hedging methods and recommend the best one. Here is a structured approach:

5. Interest Rate Risk

Interest rate risk is the risk that changes in interest rates will adversely affect a company's borrowing costs or investment returns. This is the second major risk area in ACCA FM.

5.1 Types of Interest Rate Risk

5.1.1 Basis Risk

The risk that the interest rate on a company's borrowing does not move in line with the reference rate used for hedging. For example, a company borrows at LIBOR + 2% but hedges using futures based on a different benchmark rate.

5.1.2 Gap Exposure

The risk arising from mismatches between the timing of interest rate repricing of assets and liabilities. If a company has short-term liabilities and long-term assets, rising rates increase costs faster than returns.

5.1.3 Yield Curve Risk

The risk that changes in the shape of the yield curve (the relationship between interest rates and time to maturity) will affect the company's financial position.

5.2 The Yield Curve

The yield curve shows the relationship between interest rates (yields) and the time to maturity of debt instruments. It is a crucial tool for understanding interest rate risk.

Shapes of the Yield Curve

ShapeDescriptionWhat It Suggests
Normal (Upward Sloping)Long-term rates > Short-term ratesExpectation of economic growth; higher rates for longer commitments
Inverted (Downward Sloping)Long-term rates < Short-term ratesExpectation of recession; investors expect rates to fall
FlatLong-term rates = Short-term ratesUncertainty about future economic direction
HumpedMedium-term rates highestExpectation of short-term rate increases followed by decreases

Theories Explaining the Yield Curve

TheoryExplanation
Expectations Theory The yield curve reflects market expectations of future interest rates. An upward-sloping curve means the market expects rates to rise.
Liquidity Preference Theory Investors demand a premium (higher yield) for holding longer-term bonds because they are less liquid and more sensitive to interest rate changes. This explains why the yield curve is normally upward sloping.
Market Segmentation Theory Different investors operate in different maturity segments. Supply and demand in each segment determines rates independently. Pension funds may demand long bonds, pushing long rates down.
Remember: The yield curve is NOT a prediction of future rates - it reflects the CURRENT market prices of bonds of different maturities. However, under the expectations theory, it can be used to infer market expectations.
