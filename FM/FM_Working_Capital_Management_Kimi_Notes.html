1. Working Capital Management Overview

Definition: Working Capital = Current Assets − Current Liabilities
Net Current Assets: Current Assets minus Current Liabilities (same as working capital)
Objective: Balance profitability vs. liquidity — enough WC to operate smoothly without holding excessive idle resources.

Components of Working Capital

Current Assets Current Liabilities
Inventory (Raw materials, WIP, Finished goods) Trade payables
Trade receivables Bank overdraft
Cash and cash equivalents Short-term loans
Short-term investments Accruals / Tax payable

Working Capital Investment Policies

🔴 Aggressive Policy

  • Low levels of inventory, receivables, and cash
  • Higher profitability (less money tied up)
  • Higher risk of stock-outs and liquidity problems
  • Higher RETURN but higher RISK

🟢 Conservative Policy

  • High levels of inventory, receivables, and cash
  • Lower profitability (more money tied up)
  • Lower risk — always have buffer stock and cash
  • Lower RETURN but lower RISK

Working Capital Financing Policies

Uses the concept of Permanent Current Assets (minimum level always needed) vs Fluctuating Current Assets (seasonal peaks).

Policy Permanent CA Financed By Fluctuating CA Financed By Risk/Return
Matching (Hedging) Long-term funds Short-term funds Moderate risk & return
Conservative Long-term funds Partly long-term funds (some idle cash) Low risk, low return
Aggressive Partly short-term funds Short-term funds High risk, high return
📖 Example: Financing Policy Identification

A company has permanent current assets of $500,000 and fluctuating current assets that vary between $0 and $300,000. It finances $600,000 of total current assets with long-term funds.

Solution:
Total CA range = $500,000 to $800,000
Long-term finance = $600,000
This covers all permanent CA ($500k) + $100k of fluctuating CA
→ Conservative policy because some fluctuating CA is financed by long-term funds (excess cash during low seasons).
In the exam, always state the policy name AND explain WHY using the numbers given. Don't just name it — justify with the financing mix!

2. Cash Operating Cycle (Working Capital Cycle)

The Cash Operating Cycle measures the time between paying cash to suppliers and receiving cash from customers. Shorter = Better (less cash tied up).
Cash Operating Cycle Formula
Cash Operating Cycle = Inventory Days + Receivables Days − Payables Days

Or expanded:
= Raw Material Days + WIP Days + Finished Goods Days + Receivables Days − Payables Days
Component Formulas
Inventory Days = (Average Inventory / Cost of Sales) × 365
Receivables Days = (Average Receivables / Credit Sales) × 365
Payables Days = (Average Payables / Credit Purchases) × 365

Note: If credit purchases unknown, use Cost of Sales as approximation.
📖 Example: Calculating the Cash Operating Cycle

Given data for the year:

  • Revenue: $2,400,000 (all on credit)
  • Cost of sales: $1,800,000
  • Inventory: $300,000
  • Trade receivables: $400,000
  • Trade payables: $250,000
  • Purchases: $1,500,000
Step 1 — Inventory Days:
($300,000 / $1,800,000) × 365 = 60.8 days

Step 2 — Receivables Days:
($400,000 / $2,400,000) × 365 = 60.8 days

Step 3 — Payables Days:
($250,000 / $1,500,000) × 365 = 60.8 days

Step 4 — Cash Operating Cycle:
60.8 + 60.8 − 60.8 = 60.8 days

Interpretation: Cash is tied up for approximately 61 days from paying suppliers to receiving from customers.
Always use AVERAGE balances (opening + closing) / 2 when available. If only closing balances given, use those but note the limitation. Never use revenue for inventory/payables calculations — use COS or purchases!

How to Reduce the Cash Operating Cycle

⬇️ Reduce Inventory Days

  • JIT inventory systems
  • Better demand forecasting
  • Reduce WIP through efficiency
  • Faster production processes

⬇️ Reduce Receivables Days

  • Early settlement discounts
  • Factoring / Invoice discounting
  • Tighter credit control
  • Improved collection procedures

⬆️ Increase Payables Days

  • Negotiate longer credit terms
  • Take full advantage of credit period
  • But don't damage supplier relationships!

3. Inventory Management CALCULATION HEAVY

Economic Order Quantity (EOQ) Model

The EOQ minimizes total inventory costs (holding + ordering costs).

EOQ Formula
EOQ = √(2 × Co × D / Ch)

Where:
Co = Cost per order (ordering cost)
D = Annual demand (units)
Ch = Holding cost per unit per year

Assumptions: Constant demand, constant lead time, no bulk discounts, instant replenishment.
Total Annual Inventory Cost
Total Cost = (D/Q × Co) + (Q/2 × Ch)

Ordering Cost + Holding Cost
At EOQ: Ordering Cost = Holding Cost
📖 Example: Basic EOQ Calculation

A company has annual demand of 10,000 units. Ordering cost is $50 per order. Holding cost is $2 per unit per annum.

EOQ = √(2 × 50 × 10,000 / 2)
= √(1,000,000 / 2)
= √500,000
= 707 units (rounded)

Number of orders per year = 10,000 / 707 = 14.1 orders
Total annual cost = (14.1 × $50) + (353.5 × $2) = $705 + $707 = $1,412

EOQ with Bulk Discounts

When suppliers offer discounts for larger orders, compare total costs at EOQ vs. discount break quantities.

1Calculate EOQ and total cost at EOQ (including purchase cost)
2Calculate total cost at each discount break quantity
3Choose the quantity with the LOWEST total cost
Total Cost with Discounts
Total Cost = Purchase Cost + Ordering Cost + Holding Cost

Purchase Cost = D × Price per unit
Ordering Cost = (D/Q) × Co
Holding Cost = (Q/2) × Ch   [Note: Ch may include % of purchase price]
📖 Example: EOQ with Bulk Discount

Annual demand = 10,000 units. Order cost = $100. Holding cost = 10% of purchase price. Normal price = $20/unit.

Discounts: 2% for orders ≥ 500 units; 3% for orders ≥ 1,000 units.

Step 1 — EOQ at normal price:
Ch = 10% × $20 = $2
EOQ = √(2 × 100 × 10,000 / 2) = 1,000 units

Step 2 — Total cost at EOQ (1,000 units):
This qualifies for 3% discount! Price = $19.40
Purchase = 10,000 × $19.40 = $194,000
Ordering = (10,000/1,000) × $100 = $1,000
Holding = (1,000/2) × (10% × $19.40) = 500 × $1.94 = $970
Total = $195,970

Step 3 — Total cost at 500 units (2% discount, price = $19.60):
Purchase = 10,000 × $19.60 = $196,000
Ordering = (10,000/500) × $100 = $2,000
Holding = (500/2) × (10% × $19.60) = 250 × $1.96 = $490
Total = $198,490

Step 4 — Total cost at 1 unit (no discount):
Not practical, but EOQ = 1,000 gives lowest cost.

Decision: Order 1,000 units — Total cost $195,970
When Ch is expressed as a PERCENTAGE of purchase price, recalculate Ch at EACH discount level. The holding cost per unit changes with the price!

Re-order Level and Safety Inventory

Re-order Level (ROL)
ROL = Maximum Usage × Maximum Lead Time

Or with buffer stock:
ROL = (Average Usage × Average Lead Time) + Buffer Stock
Buffer Stock (Safety Stock)
Buffer Stock = ROL − (Average Usage × Average Lead Time)

Or:
Buffer Stock = (Max Usage × Max Lead Time) − (Average Usage × Average Lead Time)
Maximum Inventory Level
Max Inventory = Buffer Stock + EOQ

Minimum Inventory Level
Min Inventory = Buffer Stock
📖 Example: ROL and Buffer Stock

A company has: Max usage = 120 units/day, Min usage = 60 units/day, Average = 90 units/day. Max lead time = 10 days, Min lead time = 5 days, Average = 7 days. EOQ = 1,000 units.

Buffer Stock:
= (Max Usage × Max Lead Time) − (Avg Usage × Avg Lead Time)
= (120 × 10) − (90 × 7) = 1,200 − 630 = 570 units

Re-order Level:
= (Avg Usage × Avg Lead Time) + Buffer Stock
= (90 × 7) + 570 = 630 + 570 = 1,200 units

Maximum Inventory:
= Buffer Stock + EOQ = 570 + 1,000 = 1,570 units

Minimum Inventory:
= 570 units (the buffer)

Just-In-Time (JIT) Inventory System

  • Concept: Inventory arrives just as it is needed in production
  • Goal: Minimize/zero inventory holding
  • Requirements: Reliable suppliers, excellent forecasting, flexible production, quality assurance, strong supplier relationships
  • Benefits: Lower holding costs, less waste, better cash flow, reduced obsolescence
  • Risks: Production stoppage if supply disrupted, vulnerable to supplier failure, less bulk discount

4. Receivables Management CALCULATION HEAVY

Credit Policy Evaluation

When considering changes to credit policy, compare:

Net Benefit of Credit Policy Change
Net Benefit = Increase in Contribution − Increase in Costs

Costs to consider:
• Incremental finance cost on extra receivables
• Incremental bad debts
• Cost of early settlement discounts
• Additional collection costs
📖 Example: Extending Credit Period

Current policy: 30 days credit, Sales = $2m, Contribution margin = 30%. Receivables = $200k. Bad debts = 1%.

Proposed: 60 days credit, Sales = $2.4m. Bad debts = 2%. Cost of capital = 10%.

Current Situation:
Receivables days = ($200k / $2m) × 365 = 36.5 days (close to 30, OK)
Current bad debts = 1% × $2m = $20,000

Proposed Situation:
New receivables = ($2.4m / 365) × 60 = $394,521
Increase in receivables = $394,521 − $200,000 = $194,521

Incremental Benefits:
Extra contribution = 30% × ($2.4m − $2m) = $120,000

Incremental Costs:
Finance cost on extra receivables = 10% × $194,521 = $19,452
Extra bad debts = (2% × $2.4m) − (1% × $2m) = $48,000 − $20,000 = $28,000
Total extra costs = $19,452 + $28,000 = $47,452

Net Benefit = $120,000 − $47,452 = $72,548

Decision: EXTEND the credit period — it is financially beneficial.
When calculating finance cost on receivables, use the FULL receivables balance (not just the variable cost portion) unless the question specifically says otherwise. Some older texts use variable cost only, but ACCA FM typically uses the full receivables amount financed at the cost of capital.

Early Settlement Discounts

Cost of Early Settlement Discount (Annualised)
Cost = [d / (100 − d)] × [365 / (Normal Period − Discount Period)]

Where d = discount %

Compare this cost with the company's cost of short-term finance. If cost of discount > cost of finance, DON'T offer it.
📖 Example: Evaluating a Discount

Terms: 2% discount if paid within 10 days, otherwise 30 days. Cost of capital = 12%.

Annualised cost of discount:
= [2 / (100 − 2)] × [365 / (30 − 10)]
= (2 / 98) × (365 / 20)
= 0.0204 × 18.25
= 37.2%

Decision: 37.2% > 12% cost of capital. The discount is VERY EXPENSIVE for the company.
However, from the CUSTOMER's perspective, NOT taking the discount means paying 37.2% implicit interest — so customers will likely take it!

For the company: Only offer if the reduced receivables days and improved cash flow justify this high cost.

Factoring

Factoring = Selling receivables to a factor (financial institution).

Type Description Key Feature
With Recourse Factor can claim back from seller if debtor doesn't pay Lower fee, risk stays with seller
Without Recourse Factor bears bad debt risk Higher fee, risk transferred
Advance Factoring Factor pays % of invoice value immediately (e.g., 80%) Immediate cash flow benefit
Maturity Factoring Factor pays on due date No immediate cash, just admin service
Net Benefit of Factoring
Benefits:
• Admin cost savings (if factor manages ledger)
• Interest saved on reduced receivables (if advance given)
• Bad debts saved (if non-recourse)

Costs:
• Factor fee (% of turnover)
• Interest on advances (if applicable)

Net Benefit = Benefits − Costs
📖 Example: Factoring Decision

Annual credit sales = $6m. Receivables days = 60. Admin costs = $50,000/year. Bad debts = 2%. Cost of capital = 10%.

Factor offers: 80% advance at 8% interest. Factor fee = 1.5% of turnover. Will reduce receivables days to 40. Non-recourse (factor bears bad debts). Admin savings = $50,000.

Current receivables: ($6m / 365) × 60 = $986,301
New receivables: ($6m / 365) × 40 = $657,534
Reduction: $986,301 − $657,534 = $328,767

Benefits:
• Interest saved on reduced receivables = 10% × $328,767 = $32,877
• Admin cost savings = $50,000
• Bad debts saved = 2% × $6m = $120,000
• Total Benefits = $202,877

Costs:
• Factor fee = 1.5% × $6m = $90,000
• Interest on advance: 80% of $657,534 = $526,027 advanced
  Interest = 8% × $526,027 = $42,082
• Total Costs = $132,082

Net Benefit = $202,877 − $132,082 = $70,795

Decision: ACCEPT factoring — positive net benefit.

Invoice Discounting

  • Borrowing money using receivables as security
  • Company retains control of sales ledger
  • Customers don't know about it (confidential)
  • Typically 80% of invoice value advanced
  • Interest charged on amount advanced
  • Good for single large invoices or occasional needs
Don't confuse factoring with invoice discounting! Factoring usually involves selling the debt AND administration of the ledger. Invoice discounting is just borrowing against receivables — the company still chases payments.

5. Payables Management

Cost of NOT Taking Early Payment Discount

This is the SAME formula as the cost of offering a discount — but from the buyer's perspective.

Annualised Cost of NOT Taking Discount
Cost = [d / (100 − d)] × [365 / (Total Period − Discount Period)]

If this cost > company's cost of finance → TAKE THE DISCOUNT (borrow if necessary to pay early)
If this cost < company's cost of finance → DON'T TAKE THE DISCOUNT (use the full credit period)
📖 Example: Should We Take the Discount?

Supplier offers: 1% discount if paid in 10 days, otherwise 45 days. Company's overdraft rate = 8%.

Annualised cost of NOT taking discount:
= [1 / (100 − 1)] × [365 / (45 − 10)]
= (1 / 99) × (365 / 35)
= 0.0101 × 10.43
= 10.5%

Decision: 10.5% > 8% overdraft rate.
It is cheaper to borrow at 8% to pay early and get the 1% discount than to use the full 45 days.

TAKE THE DISCOUNT.

Trade Credit as a Source of Finance

Advantages: Free (if no discount foregone), flexible, no formal agreement needed, widely available.

Disadvantages: Risk of damaging supplier relationships, may lose discounts, reputation risk if payments delayed too long, suppliers may refuse future credit.

Extending Payables Period

  • Effectively an interest-free loan from suppliers
  • But risks: damaged credit rating, loss of supplier goodwill, supply interruptions, loss of early payment discounts
  • Ethical considerations — deliberate late payment harms supply chain

6. Cash Management CALCULATION HEAVY

Reasons for Holding Cash

  • Transaction motive: Day-to-day operations
  • Precautionary motive: Buffer for unexpected needs
  • Speculative motive: Take advantage of opportunities

Baumol Model (Cash Management)

Applies EOQ logic to cash management. Assumes cash is used at a STEADY, CONSTANT rate.

Baumol Model Formula
Optimal Cash Transfer = √(2 × Ct × T / Ch)

Where:
Ct = Fixed cost per transaction (selling investments / withdrawing)
T = Total cash needed for the period
Ch = Holding cost (opportunity cost / interest rate)

Number of transfers = T / Optimal Transfer
Average cash balance = Optimal Transfer / 2
📖 Example: Baumol Model

A company needs $120,000 cash over the next year. Transaction cost = $50 per sale of investments. Interest rate = 6% p.a.

Optimal transfer:
= √(2 × 50 × 120,000 / 0.06)
= √(12,000,000 / 0.06)
= √200,000,000
= $14,142

Number of transfers: $120,000 / $14,142 = 8.5 → 9 transfers
Average cash balance: $14,142 / 2 = $7,071

Total cost:
Transaction cost = 8.5 × $50 = $425
Holding cost = $7,071 × 6% = $424
Total = $849 (approximately equal, as expected at optimal point)
Baumol assumes CONSTANT cash usage. In reality, cash flows are unpredictable. The Miller-Orr model is better for volatile cash flows.

Miller-Orr Model (Cash Management)

For UNCERTAIN, volatile cash flows. Sets upper and lower limits for cash balance.

Miller-Orr Formulas
Return Point = Lower Limit + (Spread / 3)

Spread = 3 × [ (3/4) × (Transaction Cost × Variance of Cash Flows) / Interest Rate ]^(1/3)

Or simplified:
Spread = 3 × ∛[(3 × Transaction Cost × Variance) / (4 × Daily Interest Rate)]

Upper Limit = Lower Limit + Spread
Decision Rules:
• If cash hits Upper Limit → Buy investments = (Upper Limit − Return Point)
• If cash hits Lower Limit → Sell investments = (Return Point − Lower Limit)
• Cash balance always returns to the Return Point
📖 Example: Miller-Orr Model

Lower limit = $10,000. Transaction cost = $50. Variance of daily cash flows = $625,000. Daily interest rate = 0.02%.

Step 1 — Calculate Spread:
Spread = 3 × ∛[(3 × 50 × 625,000) / (4 × 0.0002)]
= 3 × ∛[93,750,000 / 0.0008]
= 3 × ∛117,187,500,000
= 3 × 4,895
= $14,685

Step 2 — Calculate Return Point:
= $10,000 + ($14,685 / 3)
= $10,000 + $4,895
= $14,895

Step 3 — Upper Limit:
= $10,000 + $14,685
= $24,685

Actions:
• Cash hits $24,685 → Buy $9,790 of investments (back to $14,895)
• Cash hits $10,000 → Sell $4,895 of investments (back to $14,895)
In the exam, variance might be given as "variance of daily cash flows" or you might need to calculate it from standard deviation (Variance = SD²). Make sure you use the correct daily interest rate — if given annual, divide by 365!

Cash Budgets

A cash budget forecasts cash receipts and payments over a period.

📖 Example: Simple Cash Budget

Company has: 40% cash sales, 60% credit sales. Credit customers pay: 50% in month after sale, 50% two months after. Sales: Jan $100k, Feb $120k, Mar $150k.

March Cash Receipts:
• Cash sales (40% of Mar): 0.4 × $150,000 = $60,000
• Credit sales from Feb (50%): 0.6 × $120,000 × 50% = $36,000
• Credit sales from Jan (50%): 0.6 × $100,000 × 50% = $30,000
Total March receipts = $126,000

Always track the timing lag carefully — draw a timeline if needed!

7. Foreign Currency Working Capital

Leading and Lagging

Technique When to Use Action
Leading Expect foreign currency to WEAKEN (you owe foreign currency) Pay EARLY before currency falls
Lagging Expect foreign currency to STRENGTHEN (you owe foreign currency) Pay LATE to buy cheaper domestic currency
Leading Expect foreign currency to STRENGTHEN (you are owed foreign currency) Collect EARLY before currency rises
Lagging Expect foreign currency to WEAKEN (you are owed foreign currency) Collect LATE hoping currency recovers
📖 Example: Leading and Lagging

A UK company owes $100,000 to a US supplier in 3 months. The company expects the USD to strengthen against GBP.

Analysis:
If USD strengthens → more GBP needed to buy $100,000 in 3 months.
Action: LEAD — pay early NOW to avoid higher GBP cost later.

Conversely, if they expected USD to weaken → LAG — pay late to buy dollars more cheaply in future.

Managing Foreign Receivables & Payables

  • Invoice in home currency: Transfer risk to customer/supplier
  • Netting: Offset receivables against payables in same currency
  • Matching: Match foreign currency inflows with outflows
  • Forward contracts: Lock in exchange rate now for future transaction
  • Money market hedging: Borrow/deposit in foreign currency to create synthetic forward
Leading and lagging is a natural hedge — no transaction costs, but carries risk if exchange rate moves opposite to expectations. It also depends on supplier/customer agreeing to change payment terms.

8. Overtrading (Under-capitalisation)

Definition: Overtrading occurs when a company grows its revenue too quickly without adequate long-term capital (equity or long-term debt) to finance the increased working capital needs.

Symptoms of Overtrading

📉 Financial Signs

  • Rapid increase in revenue/sales
  • Rapid increase in current assets
  • Decreasing profit margins
  • Decreasing current ratio
  • Decreasing quick ratio

⚠️ Operational Signs

  • Increasing overdraft (short-term finance)
  • Increasing payables days (paying suppliers late)
  • Decreasing inventory turnover
  • Increasing receivables days
  • Longer cash operating cycle

Solutions to Overtrading

  1. Inject new long-term capital: Issue shares or long-term loans
  2. Improve working capital management: Reduce inventory, collect receivables faster, negotiate better payment terms
  3. Reduce growth rate: Slow down expansion to match available finance
  4. Improve profitability: Better margins generate more internal cash
  5. Lease assets: Instead of buying (preserves cash)
  6. Factor receivables: Convert receivables to immediate cash
📖 Example: Identifying Overtrading

Compare Year 1 and Year 2 for a company:

Year 1Year 2
Revenue$1,000k$2,000k
Inventory$100k$300k
Receivables$150k$400k
Payables$80k$250k
Overdraft$50k$350k
Equity$500k$500k
Analysis:
• Revenue doubled but equity stayed same → no new long-term finance
• Inventory tripled, receivables more than doubled → massive WC increase
• Overdraft increased 7x → relying heavily on short-term finance
• Current ratio: Y1 = ($100+$150)/($80+$50) = 1.92; Y2 = ($300+$400)/($250+$350) = 1.17
• Receivables days: Y1 = 55 days; Y2 = 73 days
• Payables days: Y1 = 29 days; Y2 = 46 days

Conclusion: Clear signs of OVERTRADING. The company has grown too fast without adequate long-term capital.

9. Key Ratios Summary Sheet

Ratio Formula Interpretation
Current Ratio Current Assets / Current Liabilities Liquidity. >1 is generally good. Too high = inefficient WC use.
Quick (Acid Test) Ratio (Current Assets − Inventory) / Current Liabilities Stricter liquidity test. Inventory excluded (least liquid).
Inventory Days (Avg Inventory / COS) × 365 How long inventory held. Industry-dependent.
Receivables Days (Avg Receivables / Credit Sales) × 365 How long customers take to pay. Compare to credit terms.
Payables Days (Avg Payables / Credit Purchases) × 365 How long company takes to pay. Too high = relationship risk.
Cash Operating Cycle Inv Days + Rec Days − Pay Days Time cash tied up in WC. Shorter = better.
Inventory Turnover COS / Average Inventory How many times inventory sold/replaced. Higher = more efficient.
Receivables Turnover Credit Sales / Average Receivables How many times receivables collected per year.
Working Capital to Sales Working Capital / Sales How much WC needed per $ of sales.
In the exam, always calculate ratios for MULTIPLE years or compare to industry averages. A single ratio tells you very little — trend analysis reveals the real story!

🎯 Final Exam Strategy Tips

⏱️ Time Management

WC questions are often 10-15 marks. Spend proportionate time. Set up formulas clearly before calculating.

📝 Show Your Workings

Always show formulas and steps. Even if final answer wrong, method marks are awarded.

💰 Use Full Receivables

For finance cost calculations, use full receivables balance × cost of capital unless told otherwise.

📊 State Assumptions

If using COS instead of purchases, state it. If using year-end instead of average balances, mention the limitation.

✅ Always Conclude

After calculations, always write a clear recommendation: "Accept/Reject" with brief justification using the numbers.

🔢 Check Units

Watch for $000s, $millions, days vs. months. Convert everything consistently before calculating.
