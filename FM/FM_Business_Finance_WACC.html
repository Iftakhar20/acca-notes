Sources of Business Finance HIGH

Syllabus E1(a), (b) · The vocabulary that underpins the whole section

Where the financing decision sits

Financial management rests on three connected decisions: the investment decision (which projects to accept), the financing decision (how to pay for them), and the dividend decision (how much profit to return to shareholders). They interlock — every dollar paid out as a dividend is a dollar that cannot fund a project, and every dollar borrowed changes the company's risk profile and therefore the discount rate used to appraise the next project.

The starting question for the financing decision is always the same: how long do we need the money for? Finance should be matched to the life of the asset it funds. Using an overdraft (repayable on demand) to buy a twenty-year factory is dangerous; using a twenty-year bond to fund a seasonal inventory build-up is wasteful.

Short-term sources

SourceHow it works, and its trade-offs
OverdraftA facility to go into deficit on the current account up to an agreed limit. Advantage: extremely flexible — interest is charged only on the amount actually drawn each day, so it is very cheap if used lightly. Disadvantage: it is legally repayable on demand, so the bank can withdraw it at any time. Relying on an overdraft for permanent funding is a classic sign of poor financial management.
Short-term loanA fixed sum, for a fixed term (typically under one year), with a set repayment schedule. Advantage: certainty — the bank cannot recall it early. Disadvantage: interest is paid on the whole amount whether or not it is needed, so it is more expensive than an overdraft for fluctuating needs.
Trade creditDelaying payment to suppliers. Effectively an interest-free loan and the single largest source of short-term finance for most businesses. Disadvantage: stretching payments damages supplier relationships, may forfeit early-settlement discounts (which can be very expensive in annualised terms), and can lead to loss of supply.
Lease financePaying to use an asset rather than buying it. Avoids a large upfront outflow and often includes maintenance. Can be short term (operating lease) or effectively a purchase in disguise (finance lease).

Long-term sources

Equity finance (ordinary shares)

Ordinary shareholders are the owners of the company. They receive a dividend only if the directors declare one, and in a liquidation they are paid last — after every other claim has been settled. They therefore carry the highest risk of any provider of finance and demand the highest return. From the company's perspective this makes equity the most expensive source of finance, but also the safest, because there is no legal obligation to pay anything in a bad year.

Debt finance

Loan notes, bonds, debentures and bank loans. Interest must be paid whether the company makes a profit or not, and debt is usually secured on specific assets. For the investor this is low risk; for the company it is cheap — doubly so, because interest is a tax-deductible expense while dividends are not. The cost is that fixed interest obligations increase the risk of insolvency.

Lease finance

An alternative to borrowing to buy. The lessor retains legal ownership; the lessee gets use of the asset in exchange for rentals. Attractive where the lessee cannot use tax allowances (e.g. it is loss-making) but the lessor can, or where obtaining a bank loan is difficult because the asset itself provides the lessor's security.

Venture capital

Equity finance provided by specialist funds to young, high-risk, high-growth businesses that cannot access a stock market listing or conventional bank lending. Venture capitalists typically require a substantial equity stake, board representation, tight reporting, and a defined exit route — usually a trade sale or a flotation within 3–7 years. The expected return is very high (often 30%+) to compensate for the many investments that fail.

💡
The single most important sentence in Section E

The riskier a source of finance is for the investor, the higher the return that investor demands, and therefore the more expensive that source is for the company. Every cost-of-capital calculation you will do is an application of this one idea.

Methods of Raising Equity Finance HIGH — calculations examinable

Syllabus E1(c) · Rights issues are the calculation-heavy part of this topic

The four methods

MethodDescription and evaluation
Rights issueNew shares offered to existing shareholders, in proportion to their current holding (e.g. "1 for 4"), at a price below the current market price. Cheap to administer, no prospectus needed, and pre-emption rights protect existing shareholders from dilution — provided they take up their rights. The most common way listed companies raise new equity.
PlacingShares sold directly to a small number of institutional investors (pension funds, insurers) chosen by the issuing house. Advantages: fast, low issue costs, no underwriting needed. Disadvantages: dilutes existing shareholders' control, concentrates ownership in a few institutional hands, and shares may not be widely marketable afterwards.
Public offer
(offer for sale)		Shares offered to the general public, usually at a fixed price, via a published prospectus. Advantages: raises very large sums, creates a wide shareholder base, raises the company's public profile. Disadvantages: very expensive (underwriting fees, legal and accounting fees, advertising, prospectus), and it takes months.
Stock exchange listing
(flotation / IPO)		Admitting the company's shares to trading on a recognised exchange for the first time. Advantages: access to a huge pool of capital, a market price for the shares, easier future fundraising, shares usable as acquisition currency, improved credibility with lenders and customers. Disadvantages: heavy regulatory and disclosure burden, loss of privacy, cost, dilution of founders' control, and exposure to short-term market pressure and takeover risk.

Rights issues — the mechanics you must be able to calculate

A rights issue creates a puzzle: the company issues new shares below the market price, which must drag the share price down — yet pre-emption rights are supposed to protect shareholders. The resolution is that shareholders receive something of value (the right to buy cheap shares), and the value of that right exactly compensates them for the fall in the share price. If a shareholder either takes up the rights or sells them, their wealth is unchanged. Only a shareholder who does nothing loses out.

Step 1 — The theoretical ex-rights price (TERP)

TERP is simply a weighted average of the value of the old shares and the new shares, spread over the enlarged share count.

FORMULA — Theoretical ex-rights price TERP = (N × cum-rights price) + (1 × issue price) N + 1
N = number of existing shares required to obtain one new share (the "N" in "1 for N")
Cum-rights price = the market price per share before the rights issue takes effect
Issue price = the discounted price at which the new shares are offered

You can also compute it from total values, which is safer when the ratio is awkward (e.g. "3 for 7"):

TERP = Market value of all existing shares + Total funds raised Total number of shares after the issue

Step 2 — The value of a right

The "right" is the entitlement to buy one new share at the issue price when it is immediately worth the TERP. Its value is therefore the gain available:

Value of a right (per new share) = TERP − Issue price Value of a right per existing share = TERP − Issue priceN
⚠
Read the requirement precisely

"Value of a right" and "value of a right per existing share" are different numbers, differing by a factor of N. Examiners deliberately test whether you noticed which one was asked for. If asked for "the theoretical value of a right", give the per-new-share figure unless "per existing share" is stated.

EXAMPLE 2.1 — TERP AND THE VALUE OF A RIGHT
A company has 5 million ordinary shares in issue with a cum-rights market price of $4.00 per share. It announces a 1 for 5 rights issue at an issue price of $2.50.

Calculate (a) the number of new shares, (b) the funds raised, (c) the TERP, (d) the value of a right, and (e) the value of a right per existing share.
Solution
(a) New shares = 5,000,000 ÷ 5 = 1,000,000 shares (b) Funds raised = 1,000,000 × $2.50 = $2,500,000

(c) TERP — using the total-value method:

Check: the share price fell from $4.00 to $3.75 — a fall of $0.25 — which is exactly the value of the right attaching to each existing share. That is the whole logic of a rights issue in one line.

💡
The conclusion to write

A rights issue is wealth-neutral for a shareholder who either takes up or sells the rights. The fall in the share price from the cum-rights price to the TERP is exactly offset by the value of the right received. Only a shareholder who takes no action at all is worse off.

Step 4 — When the funds are invested in a project (yield-adjusted TERP)

The plain TERP assumes the cash raised simply sits in the business earning nothing extra. In reality a rights issue funds a project. If the market believes that project has a positive NPV, the share price after the issue will be higher than the plain TERP, because the NPV accrues to shareholders immediately.

Adjusted price = MV of existing shares + Funds raised + NPV of the project Total number of shares after the issue

📝
The examiner's favourite discussion point

Candidates often assume the size of the discount matters to shareholder wealth. It does not. A deeper discount simply means more new shares issued at a lower price and a lower TERP — the two effects cancel. The discount exists only to make the issue attractive enough that shareholders subscribe, reducing the risk that the issue fails and cutting underwriting costs. Shareholder wealth changes only because of the NPV of what the money is spent on.

Working backwards — a common variant

Rights issue checklist for the exam

Islamic Finance LOW — no calculations required

Syllabus E1(d) · Explicitly stated in the study guide as "calculations are not required"

The core difference: riba

Islamic finance operates under Shariah law, whose defining prohibition for our purposes is riba — usually translated as interest, but more precisely a prohibition on making money purely from money, without sharing in the underlying risk. Under Shariah, a return must be earned by taking genuine risk in a real, tangible asset or a genuine business venture.

Two further principles follow: transactions must be linked to real assets (no purely speculative derivatives), and there must be risk and profit sharing rather than a guaranteed fixed return to a passive lender. The result is that Islamic instruments have conventional-looking equivalents, but the legal structure and the distribution of risk differ.

The five instruments in the syllabus

InstrumentEquivalentHow the return is generated
MurabahaTrade creditThe financier buys the asset the customer wants and immediately resells it to them at a pre-agreed mark-up, payable in instalments. The profit is a trading margin on a real asset, not interest on a loan.
IjaraLease financeThe financier buys the asset and leases it to the customer for rental payments. The financier retains ownership and therefore bears the ownership risks (e.g. major maintenance), which is what justifies the rental return.
MudarabaEquity financeOne party (rab al-mal) supplies all the capital; the other (mudarib) supplies expertise and management. Profits are shared in a pre-agreed ratio, but any financial loss falls entirely on the capital provider — the manager loses only their time and effort.
SukukDebt finance (bonds)Certificates giving the holder part-ownership of a tangible, income-generating asset. Holders receive a share of the income the asset produces, not interest. The asset must be identifiable and Shariah-compliant.
MusharakaVenture capitalA genuine joint venture: all parties contribute capital, all may contribute management, and profits are shared in a pre-agreed ratio while losses are shared strictly in proportion to capital contributed.
⚠
The distinction most often confused

Mudaraba vs Musharaka. In mudaraba only one party provides capital and that party bears all financial losses. In musharaka all parties provide capital and share losses in proportion to their contribution. A one-line memory hook: Mudaraba = money + management (separate parties); Musharaka = partnership (everyone puts money in).

Internal Sources of Finance & Dividend Policy MEDIUM

Syllabus E1(e) · Links the financing decision to the dividend decision

Retained earnings

For most companies, retained earnings are the single largest source of long-term finance. Their attractions are considerable: no issue costs, no dilution of control, no need for shareholder approval, no negative signal to the market, and immediate availability.

⚠
Retained earnings are not free

A very common exam error is to describe retained earnings as costless because "we already have the money". They carry an opportunity cost equal to the return shareholders could have earned had the cash been distributed to them — which is precisely the cost of equity. Retained earnings are shareholders' money reinvested on their behalf, so their required return is Ke, exactly as for new equity (just without the issue costs).

Their limitation is that the amount available is capped by profitability, by the cash actually generated, and by shareholders' dividend expectations.

Improved working capital management

Cash tied up in the operating cycle is finance the company has already raised but is not using productively. Reducing the receivables collection period, extending payables (within reason), and cutting excess inventory all release cash without borrowing a cent or issuing a single share.

EXAMPLE 4.1 — CASH RELEASED BY FASTER COLLECTION
A company has annual credit sales of $20 million and trade receivables of $4 million. It funds working capital with an overdraft at 12% per year. It plans to reduce the collection period to 60 days. Assume a 365-day year. What annual finance cost saving is achieved?
Solution
Current collection period = ($4,000,000 ÷ $20,000,000) × 365 = 73 days Receivables at 60 days = ($20,000,000 × 60) ÷ 365 = $3,287,671 Cash released = $4,000,000 − $3,287,671 = $712,329 Annual interest saving = $712,329 × 12% = $85,479

Note what has happened: the company has "raised" $712,329 of finance from inside the business, at a stroke, and permanently reduced its interest bill.

The relationship between dividend policy and the financing decision

Profit can be paid out or retained — it cannot be both. So the dividend decision is a financing decision. A company with ambitious investment plans that pays a generous dividend must make up the shortfall by raising external finance, incurring issue costs and either dilution (equity) or increased financial risk (debt).

Theoretical approaches to the dividend decision

  • Residual theory — invest in every positive-NPV project first, and pay out only what is genuinely left over. Logically impeccable, but produces a wildly fluctuating dividend that investors dislike.
  • Dividend irrelevance (M&M) — in a perfect market, shareholders are indifferent to dividend policy, because a shareholder wanting cash can simply sell shares (creating a "home-made dividend"), and one not wanting cash can reinvest the dividend. Value comes from investment decisions, not from how returns are packaged.
  • Signalling theory — in the real world, managers know more than investors. A dividend increase signals management confidence; a cut signals trouble. This is why companies are extremely reluctant to cut dividends and prefer a stable, gradually rising payout.
  • Clientele effect — different investor groups are attracted to different payout policies (e.g. pensioners wanting income vs growth investors wanting capital gains). A sudden change in policy upsets the existing clientele, so consistency matters.

Practical influences on the dividend decision

  • Legal constraints — dividends can generally only be paid out of distributable (accumulated realised) profits, not out of capital.
  • Liquidity — a profit is not cash. A company can be highly profitable and still lack the cash to pay a dividend.
  • Loan covenants — lenders frequently restrict dividend payments to protect their position.
  • Shareholder expectations — an established payout pattern is difficult to break without damaging the share price.
  • Investment opportunities — a company with many positive-NPV projects has a stronger case for retention.

Alternatives to a cash dividend

  • Scrip (bonus) dividend — extra shares issued instead of cash. Conserves cash; shareholder wealth is theoretically unchanged since the share price falls proportionately.
  • Share repurchase (buyback) — the company buys back its own shares. Returns cash to those who want it, leaves others' holdings intact, increases EPS and gearing, and offers more flexibility than committing to a higher permanent dividend.

Finance for Small and Medium-Sized Entities LOW — discussion only

Syllabus E5 · Short, memorisable, and occasionally worth 4–5 easy marks

The financing needs of small businesses

SMEs typically need finance for start-up costs, working capital to bridge the gap between paying suppliers and being paid by customers, and capital investment to grow. Their problem is not that finance does not exist — it is that the finance available is poorly matched to what they need.

The three gaps — learn these by name

ProblemExplanation
The funding gapSMEs need amounts that are too large for the owner's savings, friends and family, or a simple overdraft — but too small to justify the fixed costs of a stock exchange listing or a bond issue. They fall between two stools.
The maturity gapSMEs need long-term finance to fund growth, but lenders — lacking reliable information about the business and its prospects — prefer to lend short-term so they can withdraw quickly. The result is that SMEs often fund long-term assets with short-term money, a dangerous mismatch.
Inadequate securityLenders want tangible assets as collateral. SMEs are often asset-light (especially service businesses) and have no track record, so lenders either refuse to lend, demand personal guarantees from the owner, or charge a very high risk premium.

Underlying all three is information asymmetry: the owner knows whether the business is sound; the lender does not, and cannot cheaply find out because SMEs are not required to publish detailed audited information. Lenders therefore price for the average SME, which penalises good ones.

Measures to ease SME financing problems

SourceHow it helps
Business angel financingWealthy individuals investing their own money in early-stage businesses in exchange for equity, often bringing valuable industry experience and contacts. Fills the gap below the level at which venture capital funds will engage.
Government assistanceGrants, tax incentives for investors, and — most directly relevant — loan guarantee schemes, where the government guarantees a proportion of a bank loan, directly addressing the inadequate-security problem.
Supply chain financingThe SME's invoices to a large, creditworthy customer are financed by a bank at a rate based on the customer's credit standing rather than the SME's. Includes factoring and invoice discounting. Directly attacks both the security and information problems.
Crowdfunding / peer-to-peerRaising many small amounts from a large number of individuals via an online platform — either as debt (P2P lending) or equity (equity crowdfunding). Bypasses banks entirely and can also serve as market validation and marketing.
Venture capitalFor SMEs with genuinely high growth potential, though VCs invest in only a small fraction of applicants and demand a large equity stake and an exit route.

Risk, Return and the Creditor Hierarchy HIGH

Syllabus E3(a), (b) · A guaranteed source of discussion marks

The creditor hierarchy

The creditor hierarchy is the legal order in which financial claims are settled if a company is liquidated. It matters enormously for the cost of capital, because an investor's position in the hierarchy determines how likely they are to get their money back — and therefore what return they demand for providing it.

RankClaimantPosition and consequence
1stSecured debt
(fixed charge, then floating charge)		Backed by a legal charge over specific assets. In a liquidation these creditors are paid first out of the proceeds of the charged assets. Lowest risk → lowest required return → cheapest source for the company.
2ndUnsecured debtNo specific assets pledged; ranks behind secured creditors and shares in whatever is left. Higher risk than secured debt → higher required return.
3rdPreference sharesLegally equity, not debt, so ranks behind all creditors. But ahead of ordinary shares, and normally carries a fixed dividend. Risk and return sit between debt and equity.
4thOrdinary shareholdersPaid only after every other claim has been met in full — frequently they receive nothing. Highest risk of non-payment → highest required return → most expensive source for the company.
💡
Why debt is cheaper still — the tax shield

There are two reasons debt is cheaper than equity, and full marks require both. First, debt holders bear less risk (creditor hierarchy plus security plus a contractual right to interest regardless of profits), so they demand a lower return. Second, interest is tax-deductible whereas dividends are not — so the government effectively subsidises part of the interest cost. This is why the cost of debt used in WACC is always the post-tax figure Kd(1 − T).

📝
Examiner's comment — how marks are lost here

Past examiner reports note that candidates asked about the creditor hierarchy often just list the sources of finance in order and stop. That earns almost nothing. You must go on to explain the connection: rank → risk of non-payment → required return → cost to the company. Then, if the question gives you calculated figures, use them as evidence — e.g. "this hierarchy is illustrated by the company's own figures: cost of equity 13.0%, cost of preference shares 11.0%, and pre-tax cost of secured debt 7.8%."

Cost of Equity — The Dividend Growth Model HIGH

Syllabus E2(a)(i) · Appears in almost every WACC question

The idea behind the model

Why would anyone buy a share? Because of the cash they expect to receive from it — the future stream of dividends. It follows that the value of a share today is the present value of all its expected future dividends, discounted at the return shareholders require.

This gives us the dividend valuation model. But notice what we can do with it: for a listed company we already know the share price and the dividend. So we can turn the model around and solve for the one unknown — the shareholders' required return. And the shareholders' required return, viewed from the company's side of the table, is exactly the cost of equity.

✅
Terminology

You will see re (shareholders' required return) and Ke (cost of equity) used interchangeably in textbooks and exam answers. They are the same number viewed from opposite sides. Don't let the notation confuse you.

Case 1 — Constant dividend (no growth)

If the dividend is expected to stay the same forever, it is a perpetuity:

FORMULA — DVM, no growth P0 = Dre   ⟺   Ke = DP0
P0 = current ex-dividend market price per share
D = constant annual dividend per share
Ke = cost of equity (= re, shareholders' required return)
EXAMPLE 7.1 — NO GROWTH
A company has paid a dividend of 30c per share for many years and expects to continue at this level indefinitely. The current share price is $1.50 ex div. Calculate the cost of equity.
Solution
Ke = $0.30$1.50 = 0.20 = 20%

Case 2 — Dividends growing at a constant rate

Far more realistic, and far more commonly examined. If dividends grow at a constant rate g forever:

FORMULA — DGM with growth (given on the exam formula sheet) P0 = D0(1 + g)re − g = D1re − g Rearranged for the cost of equity: Ke = D0(1 + g)P0 + g   =   D1P0 + g
D0 = the dividend that has just been paid
D1 = the dividend expected in one year's time = D0(1 + g)
g = constant annual growth rate in dividends, as a decimal
P0 = ex-dividend market price per share

Read the formula as: dividend yield + growth rate. Shareholders earn their return in two parts — the income they receive now, and the growth in that income (which also drives capital growth in the share price).

EXAMPLE 7.2 — STANDARD GROWTH CALCULATION
P Co has just paid a dividend of 10c per share. Shareholders expect dividends to grow at 7% per year. P Co's current share price is $2.05 ex div. Calculate the cost of equity.
Solution
D1 = D0(1 + g) = $0.10 × 1.07 = $0.107
Ke = $0.107$2.05 + 0.07 = 0.0522 + 0.07 = 0.1222 = 12.2%
⚠
Trap 1 — Which dividend have you been given?

If the question says the dividend has just been paid, or gives you "the dividend for the year just ended", that is D0 and you must multiply by (1 + g). If it says "the dividend expected next year" or "the forthcoming dividend", that is already D1 — do not grow it again. Growing D1 a second time is one of the most frequently penalised errors in this topic.

⚠
Trap 2 — Cum div vs ex div

The DGM is a perpetuity formula, so it assumes the first cash flow arrives in one year — meaning the price must be an ex-dividend price. If you are given a cum-div price (a price which still includes the dividend about to be paid), convert it first:

P0 (ex div) = P0 (cum div) − D0

Examiner reports repeatedly flag failure to deduct the dividend from a cum-div price as a common error.

Method 2 — The earnings retention model (Gordon's growth model)

The logic is different and more forward-looking: a company grows by reinvesting profits. The more it retains, and the more profitably it reinvests, the faster it grows.

EXAMPLE 7.5 — GORDON'S GROWTH MODEL FEEDING INTO THE DGM
A company is about to pay an ordinary dividend of 16c per share. The share price is 200c. The accounting rate of return on equity is 12.5% and 20% of earnings are paid out as dividends. Calculate the cost of equity.
Step 1 — Retention rate and growth
b = 1 − 0.20 = 0.80 (80% of earnings retained) g = 0.80 × 12.5% = 10%
Step 2 — Apply the DGM

A company reports profit after tax of $20m and paid ordinary dividends of $8m. Opening shareholders' funds were $200m. Calculate the expected dividend growth rate.

Cost of Equity — The Capital Asset Pricing Model HIGH

Syllabus E2(a)(ii)–(iv) · Systematic risk, portfolio theory and the CAPM

Systematic and unsystematic risk

Suppose you own shares in one company. If its factory burns down, you lose. But if you own shares in fifty companies across many industries, one factory fire barely registers — and in a large enough portfolio, the good surprises in some companies offset the bad surprises in others.

This is portfolio theory, and it splits total risk into two components:

TypeAlso calledNature
Unsystematic riskSpecific risk
Unique risk
Diversifiable risk		Risk arising from factors specific to one company or industry — a strike, a product recall, losing a key contract, a fire. Can be eliminated by holding a diversified portfolio; research suggests 15–20 well-chosen shares removes most of it.
Systematic riskMarket risk
Undiversifiable risk		Risk arising from economy-wide factors that affect all companies to some degree — interest rate changes, inflation, recession, tax changes, exchange rates. Cannot be diversified away, because it affects every share in the portfolio at once.
💡
The logical leap that produces CAPM

If rational investors hold diversified portfolios, they have already eliminated unsystematic risk — at no cost. The market will therefore not pay them to bear it. The only risk investors need compensation for is systematic risk. This single insight is why the CAPM measures risk using beta (a measure of systematic risk only) and ignores total risk entirely.

Beta — measuring systematic risk

Beta (β) measures how sensitive a share's returns are to movements in the market as a whole. It is a relative measure: the market itself has a beta of exactly 1.

BetaInterpretation
β = 1The share moves exactly in line with the market. If the market rises 10%, this share rises 10%.
β > 1More volatile than the market — an "aggressive" share. β = 1.5 means a 10% market rise produces a 15% rise in this share (and a 10% fall produces a 15% fall). Typical of cyclical industries: construction, luxury goods, airlines.
β < 1Less volatile than the market — a "defensive" share. β = 0.6 means a 10% market move produces only a 6% move here. Typical of utilities, food retail, and healthcare — demand holds up in a recession.
β = 0No systematic risk at all — the return is unaffected by market movements. This is the risk-free asset.

The CAPM formula

FORMULA — CAPM (given on the exam formula sheet) E(ri) = Rf + βi (Rm − Rf)
E(ri) = required return on the share = the cost of equity Ke
Rf = risk-free rate of return (proxied by short-dated government debt yields)
Rm = expected return on the market portfolio
(Rm − Rf) = the equity risk premium, also called the market risk premium
βi = the equity beta of the share

Read it as: the return on a risk-free asset, plus a premium for bearing systematic risk, scaled by how much systematic risk this particular share carries.

⚠
The single most common CAPM error in the exam

Questions may give you either the market return Rm or the equity risk premium (Rm − Rf) — and they look almost identical on the page. If you are given the risk premium, do not subtract Rf from it again.

  • "The return on the market is 11%, Rf is 4%" → premium = 11 − 4 = 7%
  • "The equity risk premium is 7%, Rf is 4%" → premium is already 7%; do not subtract

Both cases give Ke = 4% + β × 7%. Underline the relevant words in the question before you start.

EXAMPLE 8.1 — BASIC CAPM (MARKET RETURN GIVEN)
The risk-free rate is 5.5%, the return on the market is 10.5%, and a company's equity beta is 0.7. Calculate the cost of equity.
Solution
Equity risk premium = 10.5% − 5.5% = 5.0% Ke = 5.5% + 0.7 × 5.0% = 5.5% + 3.5% = 9.0%

Sense-check: β < 1, so Ke (9.0%) should fall between Rf (5.5%) and Rm (10.5%), closer to the market. It does.

EXAMPLE 8.2 — RISK PREMIUM GIVEN DIRECTLY
The risk-free rate is 4%, the equity risk premium is 6%, and a company's equity beta is 1.4. Calculate the cost of equity.
Solution
Ke = 4% + 1.4 × 6% = 4% + 8.4% = 12.4%

The wrong answer here is 4% + 1.4 × (6% − 4%) = 6.8%, produced by treating the given 6% as if it were Rm. Sense-check: β > 1, so Ke must exceed the market return of 10% (= 4% + 6%). 12.4% does; 6.8% obviously does not.

EXAMPLE 8.3 — REVERSING CAPM TO FIND BETA
A share offers a required return of 9%. The risk-free rate is 5.5% and the market return is 10.5%. What is the share's beta, and what does it tell you?
Solution
9% = 5.5% + β × (10.5% − 5.5%) 3.5% = β × 5% β = 3.5 ÷ 5 = 0.7

Since β < 1, this share's returns are less volatile than the market's — a defensive share, carrying below-average systematic risk.

CAPM assumptions, strengths and weaknesses

Assumptions
  • Investors hold well-diversified portfolios, so only systematic risk is relevant
  • A perfect capital market exists — no transaction costs or taxes, perfect information, many buyers and sellers
  • All investors have the same expectations about returns and risks (homogeneous expectations)
  • Investors can borrow and lend without limit at the risk-free rate
  • It is a single-period model
StrengthsWeaknesses
Considers risk explicitly and specifically — a clear theoretical advantage over the DGMThe key variables are hard to establish reliably. The equity risk premium comes from statistical analysis of long historic periods and different studies produce different figures
Considers only the risk that diversified investors actually need to be paid for (systematic risk)A truly risk-free asset does not exist; the yield on short-dated government debt is only a substitute
Can produce a project-specific discount rate, not just a company-wide average — the practical advantage over WACCBeta is derived from historic data and is assumed constant, yet betas change regularly in practice
Usable for companies that pay no dividend, unlike the DGMAssumes a perfect capital market, whereas real markets are semi-strong form efficient at best, and assumes all investors are diversified, which many are not
Widely used and understood in practice; there is no better practical alternative currently availableFinding genuinely comparable proxy companies is difficult, because most companies operate across several business areas
📝
Answering "discuss the merits of CAPM over the DGM"

This exact requirement has appeared repeatedly, and examiner reports note that candidates lose marks by writing everything they know about each model instead of comparing them. Structure your answer around the differences:

  • Risk: DGM reflects risk only implicitly, through the share price. CAPM handles it explicitly, via beta, and isolates the systematic component that diversified investors are actually paid to bear.
  • The growth rate problem: the DGM depends critically on a constant growth rate that in reality varies year to year, with no objective way to choose between the arithmetic mean, geometric mean, or latest year.
  • Applicability: the DGM cannot be used at all for a company that pays no dividend; CAPM can.
  • Project appraisal: CAPM can generate a discount rate for a specific project in a different industry; the DGM only produces a historic, company-wide figure.
  • Balance: CAPM has its own data problems (Rf, Rm, and β are all difficult to estimate reliably). Note that in a perfect world with perfect data, both models should give the same cost of equity.

Asset Betas and Project-Specific Discount Rates HIGH

Syllabus E3(e)(iii), (iv) · De-gearing and re-gearing — a reliable Section C requirement

Why a company's own beta is often the wrong beta

A company's equity beta bundles together two quite different sources of systematic risk:

  • Business risk — the systematic risk of the industry it operates in. A supermarket has low business risk; a mining exploration company has high business risk.
  • Financial risk — the extra volatility in returns to shareholders caused by gearing. Fixed interest payments must be met first, so with high gearing, whatever operating profit is left over for shareholders swings much more violently.

Now suppose a company wants to appraise a project in a different industry. Its own equity beta is useless, because it reflects the wrong business risk. But it cannot simply borrow a competitor's equity beta either, because that competitor has a different level of gearing and therefore different financial risk.

The solution is to take a proxy company's equity beta, strip out its financial risk to isolate pure business risk (the asset beta), then add back the investing company's financial risk. This is de-gearing and re-gearing.

💡
Two betas, one job

Asset beta (βa) — also called the ungeared beta — reflects business risk only. It is what a company's beta would be if it had no debt at all.
Equity beta (βe) — also called the geared beta — reflects business risk plus financial risk. Because financial risk can only add to risk, βe is always ≥ βa.

The asset beta formula

FORMULA — asset beta (given on the exam formula sheet) βa = VeVe + Vd(1 − T) βe  +  Vd(1 − T)Ve + Vd(1 − T) βd
Ve = market value of equity  ·  Vd = market value of debt  ·  T = corporation tax rate
βd = debt beta — in the FM exam this is always assumed to be zero (debt is treated as risk-free)

Because βd = 0, the entire second term disappears and you only ever need the simplified version:

SIMPLIFIED — the version you will actually use βa = βe × VeVe + Vd(1 − T)

The four-step procedure

  1. Identify a proxy company already operating in the business area of the proposed project, and take its equity beta and its gearing (Ve : Vd).
  2. De-gear the proxy's equity beta into an asset beta, using the proxy's Ve and Vd. If several proxies are given, calculate an asset beta for each and take a simple average.
  3. Re-gear that asset beta into an equity beta, using the investing company's Ve and Vd.
  4. Apply CAPM with the re-geared equity beta to obtain the project-specific cost of equity.
⚠
The error that costs the most marks

Whose gearing do you use? De-gear using the proxy company's Ve and Vd. Re-gear using the investing company's Ve and Vd. Writing the proxy's gearing into both steps is the single most common mistake, and it produces a wrong answer at every subsequent stage.

Two further points: the (1 − T) factor applies only to the debt term, never to equity. And Ve : Vd can be given as a ratio (e.g. "5:2") or as market values in $m — either works, since only the proportions matter.

Worked examples

EXAMPLE 9.1 — FULL DE-GEAR / RE-GEAR / CAPM
Hubbard Co is appraising an investment in the hot-air balloon industry, an area entirely new to it. A listed balloon manufacturer has an equity beta of 1.1 and an equity : debt ratio of 5 : 2. Hubbard Co is financed 80% equity and 20% debt. Corporation tax is 30%, the risk-free rate is 4% and the market return is 10%. Corporate debt is assumed risk-free.

Calculate a suitable risk-adjusted cost of equity for the new investment.
Step 1 & 2 — De-gear the proxy's equity beta (using the PROXY's 5:2 gearing)
βa = 1.1 × 55 + 2(1 − 0.30) = 1.1 × 55 + 1.4 = 1.1 × 56.4 = 1.1 × 0.7813 = 0.859

Sense-check: the asset beta (0.859) must be lower than the equity beta (1.1), because we have stripped out financial risk.

Step 3 — Re-gear into Hubbard Co's own capital structure (80:20)
0.859 = βe × 8080 + 20(1 − 0.30) = βe × 8094 = βe × 0.8511 βe = 0.8590.8511 = 1.009
Step 4 — Apply CAPM
Ke = 4% + 1.009 × (10% − 4%) = 4% + 6.05% = 10.05%, say 10.1%

Conclusion to write: "A cost of equity of approximately 10.1% reflects both the systematic business risk of the balloon manufacturing industry and Hubbard Co's own level of financial risk, and is therefore a more appropriate discount rate for this project than Hubbard Co's existing cost of equity."

EXAMPLE 9.2 — RE-GEARING TO A DIFFERENT COMPANY'S STRUCTURE
XY Co and PQ Co are identical in every respect except capital structure. XY Co has a debt : equity ratio of 1 : 3 and an equity beta of 1.2. PQ Co has a debt : equity ratio of 2 : 3. Corporation tax is 30%. What is an appropriate equity beta for PQ Co?
Step 1 — De-gear XY Co (Ve = 3, Vd = 1)
βa = 1.2 × 33 + 1(0.70) = 1.2 × 33.7 = 1.2 × 0.8108 = 0.973
Step 2 — Re-gear to PQ Co (Ve = 3, Vd = 2)
0.973 = βe × 33 + 2(0.70) = βe × 34.4 = βe × 0.6818 βe = 0.9730.6818 = 1.43

PQ Co is more highly geared, so its shareholders bear more financial risk, so its equity beta (1.43) exceeds XY Co's (1.2). Identical business, higher risk to shareholders — this is exactly what financial risk means.

EXAMPLE 9.3 — AVERAGING MULTIPLE PROXIES
Two proxy companies operate in the target industry. Proxy A: equity beta 1.30, Ve:Vd = 7:3. Proxy B: equity beta 1.05, Ve:Vd = 4:1. Tax is 25%. What single asset beta should be used?
De-gear each proxy separately
Proxy A: βa = 1.30 × 77 + 3(0.75) = 1.30 × 79.25 = 1.30 × 0.7568 = 0.984 Proxy B: βa = 1.05 × 44 + 1(0.75) = 1.05 × 44.75 = 1.05 × 0.8421 = 0.884
Take a simple average before re-gearing
Average βa = (0.984 + 0.884) ÷ 2 = 0.934

Use a simple average, not a weighted one, and average the asset betas — never average the equity betas, since they reflect different gearing levels and so are not comparable.

Estimating the Cost of Debt HIGH

Syllabus E2(b) · Five types, each with its own method

Essential conventions — read this before anything else

  • Debt is always quoted in $100 nominal value blocks. A "9% loan note quoted at $104" means: $9 of interest per year, and the market will pay $104 to buy $100 of nominal value.
  • The coupon rate is a percentage of nominal value, not market value. It is fixed at issue and never changes. Market value changes daily.
  • Ex-interest and cum-interest work exactly like ex-div and cum-div for shares. If given a cum-interest price, deduct the interest about to be paid.
  • Two different costs exist and you must know which is wanted. The pre-tax cost Kd is the return required by the investor. The post-tax cost Kd(1 − T) is the cost to the company, after the tax relief on interest. WACC always uses the post-tax figure.
⚠
Which cost does the question want?

Phrases like "the return required by the debt holders", "the investors' required return", or "the yield" mean the pre-tax cost — do not apply (1 − T). Phrases like "the cost of debt to the company" or "for inclusion in the WACC" mean the post-tax cost. Getting this backwards will cost you the mark even when your method is perfect.

Type 1 — Irredeemable debt

Never repaid; interest continues forever. That makes it a perpetuity, so no discounting tables are needed.

FORMULA — irredeemable debt Pre-tax:  Kd = IMV      Post-tax:  Kd(1 − T) = I (1 − T)MV
I = annual interest per $100 nominal value (= the coupon rate)
MV = current ex-interest market value per $100 nominal value
T = corporation tax rate
EXAMPLE 10.1 — IRREDEEMABLE DEBT
A company has irredeemable loan notes with an 8% coupon rate, currently trading at $75 ex interest. The corporation tax rate is 30%. Calculate (a) the return required by investors and (b) the cost of the debt to the company.

Type 2 — Redeemable debt

Here the investor receives interest for a fixed period and then gets the principal back. The market value today equals the present value of all those future cash flows. So the cost of debt is the discount rate that makes the present value of the future cash flows equal to today's market price — in other words, it is the IRR of the debt's cash flows.

  1. Set out the cash flows: T0 = market value as an outflow; T1–Tn = interest (after tax, if the post-tax cost is wanted); Tn = redemption value.
  2. Discount at two trial rates — ideally one giving a positive NPV and one negative.
  3. Interpolate to find the IRR.
FORMULA — linear interpolation (IRR) IRR = L + NLNL − NH × (H − L)
L = lower discount rate  ·  H = higher discount rate
NL = NPV at the lower rate  ·  NH = NPV at the higher rate

Type 3 — Convertible debt

The investor has a choice at the redemption date: take the cash, or convert into a set number of ordinary shares. Since investors act to maximise their own wealth, they will take whichever is worth more. Your job is to work out which that is, and then treat it as the terminal cash flow in an ordinary IRR calculation.

  1. Calculate the conversion value at the conversion date: grow the current share price forward at the given growth rate, then multiply by the number of shares per loan note.
  2. Compare with the cash redemption value and select the higher. State your conclusion explicitly — there is a mark for the comparison and the reasoning.
  3. Find the IRR of: market value now (outflow), after-tax interest each year, and the higher value at Tn.
⚠
No tax on the terminal amount

Whether the investor takes cash or converts, there is no tax effect on the redemption or conversion amount — tax relief applies only to the interest payments. Do not apply (1 − T) to the terminal value.

The Weighted Average Cost of Capital HIGH

Syllabus E2(c) · The destination that everything above leads to

What the WACC is

A company is financed by several sources, each with its own cost. The WACC blends them into a single figure: the minimum return the company must earn on its investments to satisfy all its providers of finance simultaneously. Earn more than the WACC and value is created; earn less and value is destroyed. This is why it is used as the discount rate in NPV appraisal.

FORMULA — WACC (given on the exam formula sheet) WACC = VeVe + Vd Ke  +  VdVe + Vd Kd(1 − T)
Ve = market value of equity  ·  Vd = market value of debt
Ke = cost of equity  ·  Kd(1 − T) = post-tax cost of debt
Extra sources (e.g. preference shares) are simply added as further weighted terms.

The four steps

  1. Calculate the market value of each source of long-term finance
  2. Calculate the cost of each source
  3. Multiply each source's value by its cost
  4. Divide the total of (value × cost) by the total value

Average versus marginal cost of capital

Everything calculated so far is the company's average cost of capital (ACC) — a weighted average across its existing capital structure. The marginal cost of capital (MCC) is the cost of raising the next unit of finance.

The two diverge because a company raising ever-larger amounts eventually exhausts its cheapest sources and must turn to more expensive ones. The relationship is the standard average/marginal relationship from economics:

  • While the MCC is below the ACC, each new unit of finance is cheaper than the existing average, so the ACC falls.
  • Once the MCC rises above the ACC, each new unit is dearer than the average, so it pulls the ACC up — though the ACC rises more slowly than the MCC, because it is being dragged by an average.

In principle the MCC is the theoretically correct rate for appraising a new project, since it reflects the cost of the money actually being used. In practice the WACC is used, because a specific project is rarely funded by one identifiable tranche of finance.

When can the WACC be used as a discount rate?

The WACC is calculated from the company's current costs of equity and debt, reflecting its current business risk and current gearing. It is therefore only a valid discount rate for a new project when all three of the following hold:

  1. The project is small relative to the company, so it will not materially change the company's overall risk profile.
  2. The project has the same business risk as the company's existing operations — i.e. it is more of the same, not a diversification into a new industry.
  3. The project is financed so as to leave gearing unchanged, so the company's financial risk — and therefore its cost of equity — is not altered.
💡
What goes wrong if you use the WACC anyway

Using a single company-wide WACC on projects of differing risk causes systematic misallocation of capital. Applied to a high-risk project, the WACC understates the required return, so the NPV is overstated and the company accepts projects it should reject. Applied to a low-risk project, the WACC overstates the required return, so the company rejects projects that would have created value. Over time, the company drifts towards a portfolio of ever-riskier investments — the exact opposite of prudent financial management.

The remedy is a project-specific discount rate calculated using CAPM with a de-geared and re-geared beta (Section 9 above). That is the advantage of CAPM over WACC.

Assessing the impact of a financing choice

When a question asks you to assess the effect of raising debt versus equity, you are expected to calculate and interpret a set of ratios. Critically, several of these are not provided on the exam formula sheet — you must know them.

Financial gearing

Measures the proportion of the capital structure that is debt. Two acceptable versions exist — state which you are using.

Debt/Equity = DebtEquity × 100%    or    Debt/(Debt+Equity) = DebtDebt + Equity × 100%
Use market values where available; be consistent, and label your ratio clearly so the marker knows which version you used.

Interest cover (interest gearing)

Measures how comfortably operating profit covers the interest bill — the most direct measure of financial risk.

Interest cover = Profit before interest and tax (PBIT)Interest payable
A cover below about 3 times is generally regarded as risky; below 2 is a serious warning sign. Higher is safer.

Operational gearing

Measures how sensitive operating profit is to a change in sales, which depends on the proportion of fixed costs in the cost base. High fixed costs mean profit swings violently with volume — that is business risk, and it is quite separate from financial risk.

Operational gearing = ContributionPBIT
Contribution = Revenue − Variable costs. An alternative version uses Fixed costs ÷ PBIT; contribution ÷ PBIT is the more common in FM.

The value of a company is the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at its cost of capital:

Capital Structure Theories HIGH — frequent Section C discussion

Syllabus E4 · Traditional view, M&M, market imperfections, pecking order

The question all four theories are answering

Debt is cheaper than equity. So why doesn't every company simply replace all its equity with debt and cut its WACC to almost nothing? The answer is that increasing gearing increases the risk borne by shareholders, so Ke rises. The entire debate is about whether the rise in Ke more than offsets, exactly offsets, or fails to offset the benefit of the cheaper debt. Hold that question in your head and the four theories become easy to keep apart.

1. The traditional view

The traditional view — the intuitive, "common sense" position that went essentially unquestioned before 1958 — holds that there is an optimal capital structure.

  • At low gearing, adding cheap debt lowers the WACC. Ke rises a little as shareholders notice the extra financial risk, but not by enough to offset the benefit. WACC falls.
  • Kd stays broadly constant at low-to-moderate gearing, because lenders still feel well protected.
  • As gearing rises further, shareholders demand progressively larger increases in Ke. Eventually this outweighs the benefit of the cheap debt. WACC starts to rise.
  • At extreme gearing, Kd also rises sharply as lenders worry about the security of their loans and even the payment of interest. Both costs rise together and the WACC increases steeply.

The result is a U-shaped WACC curve. At its lowest point — the optimal capital structure — the overall return required by all investors is minimised, and therefore the combined market value of the company's debt and equity is maximised.

💡
The logic of the value link

If investors are receiving the same total dollar return but the percentage return they require has fallen, market pressure must push the value of the securities up. Minimising the WACC is therefore identical to maximising company value.

Criticism: the traditional view offers no way to identify the optimal point in advance — it simply asserts that one exists. That gap is what Modigliani and Miller set out to fill.

2. Modigliani & Miller, 1958 — no taxation

M&M argued the traditionalists were wrong. In a perfect capital market with no taxes, capital structure is irrelevant — the WACC is constant at every level of gearing, and so is the value of the company.

The argument
  • Investors are rational, so the return they require on equity is directly and linearly proportional to gearing (measured as Vd/Ve).
  • The increase in Ke exactly offsets the benefit of the cheaper debt.
  • Therefore the WACC remains unchanged at all gearing levels.

Their underlying reasoning is that a company's value depends on the future operating income generated by its assets — and how that income stream is split between debt holders and equity holders cannot change the size of the stream itself. M&M supported this with an arbitrage argument: if two otherwise identical companies with different gearing had different values, investors could buy the underpriced one and sell the overpriced one until the values converged. (The proof itself is outside the FM syllabus, but the word "arbitrage" is worth including.)

Assumptions — learn these, they carry marks
  • No taxation
  • Perfect capital markets — all investors have the same information and act rationally on it
  • No transaction costs
  • Debt is risk-free and freely available at the same cost to individuals and companies alike

Implication for the finance director: the choice of finance is irrelevant to shareholder wealth. Use any mix you like.

3. Modigliani & Miller, 1963 — with corporate taxation

The most damaging criticism of the 1958 model was the assumption of no tax. Since debt interest is tax-deductible and dividends are not, tax cannot be ignored. M&M revised the model, keeping all the other perfect-market assumptions.

  • Ke still rises as gearing increases — shareholders still bear increasing financial risk.
  • But the post-tax cost of debt Kd(1 − T) is now lower than before, because of the tax relief on interest.
  • Lower debt costs mean less volatility in shareholder returns for a given level of gearing, so Ke rises along a shallower gradient than in the no-tax model.
  • The rise in Ke therefore no longer fully offsets the benefit of the cheaper debt.

Conclusion: gearing up steadily reduces the WACC and steadily increases the market value of the company. Geared companies pay less tax than ungeared ones and are therefore worth more — the value difference being the present value of the tax shield on the debt.

⚠
The logical conclusion — and why it is obviously wrong

If every extra dollar of debt lowers the WACC, the optimal capital structure is 99.9% debt. Companies should borrow as much as they possibly can. Since no real company does this, the model must be missing something — and what it is missing is exactly the subject of the next section. Stating this conclusion and then immediately explaining why it fails in practice is what a strong exam answer looks like.

4. Market imperfections — why 99.9% gearing never happens

M&M's with-tax conclusion collapses once you drop the assumption of a perfect capital market. Real-world frictions push the WACC back up at high gearing:

ImperfectionEffect on the M&M conclusion
Bankruptcy and financial distress costsM&M ignore them entirely. In reality, as gearing rises, so does the probability of insolvency — bringing legal and administrative costs, assets sold at fire-sale prices, key staff leaving to avoid a failing company, customers and suppliers demanding worse terms, and management time consumed by short-term firefighting rather than value creation.
Agency costsLenders protect themselves with restrictive covenants — limits on further borrowing, on dividends, on asset disposals, and required minimum ratios. These constrain management's freedom to pursue value-creating opportunities, an economic cost that does not appear in the interest rate.
Tax exhaustionThe whole benefit of debt in the M&M model is tax relief on interest. A company whose interest bill exceeds its taxable profits cannot use all the relief, so additional debt brings no further tax benefit — while still adding risk.
Debt is not risk-freeM&M assume it is. In reality, at high gearing lenders demand progressively higher interest rates, so Kd itself rises.
Reduced borrowing capacityAssets already pledged as security cannot be pledged again, so further borrowing becomes harder and dearer regardless of the tax position.
Reluctance of shareholdersAt high gearing, shareholders may refuse to fund even positive-NPV projects, unwilling to "throw good money after bad" when most of the benefit would accrue to lenders.

Once these are recognised, the WACC curve resembles the traditional view once more: falling initially as the tax benefit of debt dominates, then rising as distress and agency costs take over. An optimal capital structure exists — but at a moderate, sensible level of gearing, not at 99.9%.

✅
The sentence to end a capital structure discussion with

"It can therefore be argued that the use of debt in a company's capital structure can reduce its WACC and increase its value, provided that gearing is kept to an acceptable level." This one sentence reconciles M&M-with-tax and the real world, and it is the conclusion examiners are looking for.

5. Pecking order theory

Pecking order theory is a different kind of theory. It does not attempt to identify a WACC-minimising capital structure at all. Instead it describes how managers actually behave when choosing finance, and explains real-world patterns the formal theories cannot.

Its driver is information asymmetry: managers know more about the company's true prospects than outside investors, so any external fundraising sends a signal — and issuing equity sends the worst one, because investors reason that management would only sell shares if it believed them to be overvalued.

  1. Retained earnings first — no issue costs, no external scrutiny, no signalling effect, immediately available.
  2. Then debt — external, but does not dilute ownership, has low issue costs relative to equity, and a willingness to commit to fixed interest payments is actually read as a positive signal of management confidence.
  3. New equity last — a genuine last resort, because of high issue costs, dilution of control, and the negative signal it sends, which typically depresses the share price on announcement.
💡
What pecking order explains that the other theories cannot

Highly profitable companies frequently carry low gearing — precisely the opposite of what M&M-with-tax predicts. Pecking order explains it perfectly: profitable companies generate abundant retained earnings, so they simply never reach step 2. Gearing is not a target they are aiming at; it is a residual outcome of how much internal cash they happened to have.

⚠
Watch the MCQ wording

Objective test questions may ask you to order specific instruments. Remember the ordering runs from internal to debt to equity. Preference shares and convertible debt sit between straight debt and ordinary shares — a statement like "pecking order theory indicates preference shares are preferred to convertible debt" is not a proposition of the theory and would be false.

The practical problems of high gearing

Beyond the theory, be ready to discuss the concrete dangers of a highly geared structure:

  • Increased risk of insolvency — interest must be paid regardless of profitability, so a downturn can be fatal rather than merely painful.
  • Reduced financial flexibility — little or no capacity to raise further debt, either to exploit an opportunity or to survive a shock.
  • Higher required returns from all investors — both lenders and shareholders price in the increased risk, ultimately raising the WACC and lowering company value.
  • Covenant breach risk — breaching a covenant can trigger immediate repayment of the entire loan, converting a manageable problem into a crisis.
  • Loss of managerial freedom — covenants and lender scrutiny constrain investment, dividend and disposal decisions.
  • Deterrent to further equity investment — new shareholders are reluctant to invest when much of any upside would accrue to existing lenders.
📝

Section C questions in this area almost always carry marks for a concluding recommendation. Don't simply present the theories and stop. QUESTION 04 WACC
A company has equity with a market value of $60m and a cost of equity of 15%, and debt with a market value of $40m and a post-tax cost of debt of 6%.
What is the company's WACC?

Correct answer: C — 11.4%

Total market value = $60m + $40m = $100m
WACC = (60/100 × 15%) + (40/100 × 6%) = 9.0% + 2.4% = 11.4%

Option B (10.5%) is the simple unweighted average of 15% and 6% — a very common slip. Option A (9.0%) includes only the equity term.

QUESTION 05 Rights issue
A company has 4 million shares in issue with a cum-rights price of $5.00. It announces a 1 for 4 rights issue at $3.50 per share.
What is the theoretical ex-rights price?

Correct answer: B — $4.70

New shares = 4,000,000 ÷ 4 = 1,000,000
Total value = (4,000,000 × $5.00) + (1,000,000 × $3.50) = $20,000,000 + $3,500,000 = $23,500,000
TERP = $23,500,000 ÷ 5,000,000 = $4.70

Option A ($4.25) is the simple average of $5.00 and $3.50, which ignores the fact that there are four old shares for every one new share. Always sense-check: the TERP must sit between the issue price and the cum-rights price, and closer to the cum-rights price when the ratio is 1-for-many.

QUESTION 08 Capital structure
Consider the implications of the different capital structure theories.
According to Modigliani & Miller's theory with corporate tax, as gearing increases, what happens to the cost of equity and the WACC?

Correct answer: C — Ke increases; WACC decreases

Shareholders still bear increasing financial risk as gearing rises, so Ke still increases. But because interest is tax-deductible, the after-tax cost of debt is lower, and the rise in Ke is no longer steep enough to offset it — so the WACC falls.

QUESTION 10 Growth rates
A company earns an accounting return on equity of 15% and pays out 40% of its earnings as dividends.

Correct answer: B — 9.0%

QUESTION 11 Gearing
A company reports revenue of $40m, variable costs of $16m, fixed costs of $16m (so PBIT is $8m), and interest payable of $2m.
What are the company's operational gearing and interest cover respectively?

Correct answer: A — 3.0 times and 4.0 times

Contribution = $40m − $16m = $24m
Operational gearing = Contribution ÷ PBIT = $24m ÷ $8m = 3.0 times
Interest cover = PBIT ÷ Interest = $8m ÷ $2m = 4.0 times

Interpretation: operational gearing of 3.0 means a 10% fall in sales would reduce PBIT by around 30% — substantial business risk. Interest cover of 4.0 times is adequate but not generous.
