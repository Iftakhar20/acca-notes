What is Investment Appraisal?

Investment appraisal (also called capital budgeting) is the process of evaluating whether a long-term investment is worth making. Companies spend money today — on machinery, buildings, technology — expecting future cash inflows. The question is: will those future benefits justify the cost?

As an FP&A professional at Super Health, you'll recognise this every time a department submits a business case for new medical equipment or an IT system — this is exactly what investment appraisal covers.

Identifying Relevant Cash Flows

Before calculating any appraisal figure, you must identify which cash flows to include. ACCA is very specific: only relevant (incremental) cash flows should be included.

✅ INCLUDE — Relevant Cash Flows

  • Incremental revenue — extra sales generated by the project
  • Incremental costs — extra costs caused by the project
  • Initial capital expenditure (Time 0)
  • Scrap / residual value at end of project life
  • Working capital — cash tied up in inventory/receivables (recovered at end)
  • Opportunity costs — value of the next best alternative given up
  • Tax effects (covered in D2)

❌ EXCLUDE — Irrelevant Cash Flows

  • Sunk costs — already spent, cannot be recovered (e.g., market research already paid for)
  • Non-cash items — depreciation is NOT a cash flow; do not include it
  • Absorbed overheads — only incremental (extra) overheads are relevant
  • Finance costs — interest is reflected in the discount rate, not as a cash flow
Example Super Health — New MRI Machine

Super Health is evaluating a new MRI scanner. Six months ago, they paid AED 50,000 for a feasibility study. The machine costs AED 2,000,000 today. Revenues of AED 600,000 p.a. are expected. Existing maintenance costs of AED 40,000 p.a. will rise to AED 90,000 p.a. if the machine is bought.

Payback Period

The payback period measures how long it takes to recover the initial investment from project cash flows. Simple, intuitive, and widely used — but has significant limitations.

Formula
Payback = Year before full recovery + (Remaining balance ÷ Cash flow in recovery year)
Example Project Alpha — Payback Calculation

Initial investment: AED 500,000. Cash flows as follows:

YearCash Flow (AED)Cumulative (AED)
0(500,000)(500,000)
1150,000(350,000)
2180,000(170,000)
3200,00030,000 ✅
4160,000190,000

Payback = 2 years + (170,000 ÷ 200,000) = 2.85 years ≈ 2 years 10 months

✅ Advantages of Payback

  • Simple and easy to understand
  • Useful measure of liquidity risk
  • Favours early cash flows — less exposure to uncertainty
  • Useful for businesses with cash flow constraints
  • Quick initial screening tool

❌ Disadvantages of Payback

  • Ignores cash flows after payback period
  • Ignores time value of money (TVM)
  • Does not measure profitability
  • No objective criteria — what is "acceptable"?
  • Does not maximise shareholder wealth

Discounted Payback Period

An improvement on payback: discount cash flows first, then calculate when discounted cumulative cash flows turn positive. This addresses the time value of money criticism but still ignores cash flows after the payback point.

Example Discounted Payback — 10% discount rate

Discounted Payback = 3 years + (64,770 ÷ 109,280) = 3.59 years — longer than simple payback (2.85 years) as expected, since discounted cash flows are smaller.

Return on Capital Employed (ROCE) / Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

ROCE uses accounting profit (not cash flows) and compares it to the capital invested. Also called ARR.

Formula — Two Versions (both valid in exam)
ROCE = (Average annual accounting profit / Initial investment) × 100% — OR — ROCE = (Average annual accounting profit / Average investment) × 100% Average Investment = (Initial investment + Scrap value) ÷ 2 Accounting Profit = Cash flow – Depreciation Annual Depreciation = (Initial cost – Scrap value) ÷ Project life
⚠️ Exam Tip
Always state which ROCE formula you are using in your exam answer. Both are acceptable but must be used consistently. The examiner will not penalise you for using either version.
Example ROCE Calculation

Machine cost: AED 400,000. Scrap value: AED 40,000. Life: 5 years. Annual cash inflows: AED 120,000.

  • Annual depreciation = (400,000 – 40,000) ÷ 5 = AED 72,000
  • Annual accounting profit = 120,000 – 72,000 = AED 48,000
  • ROCE (on initial) = 48,000 ÷ 400,000 = 12%
  • Average investment = (400,000 + 40,000) ÷ 2 = 220,000
  • ROCE (on average) = 48,000 ÷ 220,000 = 21.8%

If the company's target ROCE is 15%, the project would be rejected on initial investment basis but accepted on average investment basis — highlighting the importance of consistency.

✅ Advantages of ROCE

  • Easy to understand and calculate
  • Uses accounting figures familiar to managers
  • Can be compared to a target/hurdle rate
  • Considers the whole project life (unlike payback)

❌ Disadvantages of ROCE

  • Uses profit not cash flows
  • Ignores time value of money
  • Affected by choice of depreciation method
  • No agreed definition — initial or average investment?
★
The most important method in FM

Net Present Value (NPV)

NPV is the gold standard investment appraisal method. It directly measures the increase (or decrease) in shareholder wealth from undertaking a project. A positive NPV means the project creates value; a negative NPV destroys value.

The Time Value of Money

AED 1,000 today is worth more than AED 1,000 in three years. Why?

  • Inflation — money loses purchasing power over time
  • Risk — future cash flows are uncertain
  • Opportunity cost — money invested today could earn a return
Discount Factor Formula
Discount Factor (DF) = 1 ÷ (1 + r)ⁿ Present Value (PV) = Future Cash Flow × Discount Factor NPV = Sum of all PVs (including initial investment at T0)
💡 Using the ACCA Tables
In the exam, you are given discount factor tables. For example, at 10% for year 3, the discount factor is 0.751. Simply multiply the cash flow by this factor.

Annuity and Perpetuity

When cash flows are equal each year, use the Annuity Factor (AF) — also called the Present Value of an Annuity or Cumulative Discount Factor.

Annuity Formula (for equal annual cash flows)
AF = [1 – (1+r)⁻ⁿ] ÷ r PV of Annuity = Annual Cash Flow × AF
Perpetuity (cash flow continues forever)
PV of Perpetuity = Annual Cash Flow ÷ r

NPV — Decision Rule

  • ✅ NPV > 0: Accept — project adds value to the business
  • ❌ NPV < 0: Reject — project destroys value
  • ➡️ NPV = 0: Marginal — project earns exactly the required return
  • 🔀 Mutually exclusive projects: Choose the one with the highest positive NPV

✅ Advantages of NPV

  • Considers TVM
  • Uses cash flows, not accounting profit
  • Directly measures shareholder wealth added
  • Considers all cash flows over project life
  • Theoretically superior method
  • Can handle non-conventional cash flows

❌ Disadvantages of NPV

  • Harder to understand than payback/ROCE
  • Result is an absolute figure — hard to compare projects of different sizes
  • Relies on an accurate discount rate
  • Assumes cash flows are reinvested at cost of capital

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

The IRR is the discount rate at which NPV = 0. It represents the return earned by the project. The decision rule: accept if IRR exceeds the cost of capital (hurdle rate).

IRR Formula (Linear Interpolation)
IRR = L + [ NPV_L ÷ (NPV_L – NPV_H) ] × (H – L) Where: L = lower discount rate, H = higher discount rate NPV_L = NPV at lower rate (positive), NPV_H = NPV at higher rate (negative)
📝 Exam Technique
To calculate IRR: (1) Calculate NPV at one rate (should be positive — try the cost of capital). (2) Calculate NPV at a higher rate (should be negative). (3) Apply the interpolation formula. Choose two rates that give one positive and one negative NPV.
Example IRR Calculation — Project Gamma

Initial investment AED 100,000. Annual cash flows AED 30,000 for 5 years. Cost of capital: 12%.

Step 1: NPV at 12%: PV = 30,000 × 3.605 (AF 5yr @12%) = 108,150; NPV = 108,150 – 100,000 = +AED 8,150

Step 2: Try 18%: PV = 30,000 × 3.127 = 93,810; NPV = 93,810 – 100,000 = –AED 6,190

Step 3: IRR = 12 + [8,150 ÷ (8,150 + 6,190)] × (18 – 12)

= 12 + [8,150 ÷ 14,340] × 6 = 12 + 0.568 × 6 = 12 + 3.41 = 15.41%

Decision
IRR 15.41% > Cost of capital 12% → ACCEPT the project.

✅ Advantages of IRR

  • Considers TVM
  • Easy to understand as a percentage return
  • Can be compared directly to cost of capital
  • No need to know cost of capital to calculate

❌ Disadvantages of IRR

  • Can give multiple IRRs with non-conventional cash flows
  • Assumes reinvestment at IRR (often unrealistic)
  • Can give wrong ranking for mutually exclusive projects
  • Ignores scale of investment

NPV vs IRR — Key Comparison (D1h)

🏆 ACCA's View — NPV is Superior

When NPV and IRR give conflicting rankings for mutually exclusive projects, always follow the NPV decision. NPV directly measures value added; IRR can mislead when project sizes differ.

DCF vs Non-DCF — Superiority of DCF (D1g)

The ACCA syllabus asks you to discuss why DCF methods (NPV and IRR) are superior to non-DCF methods (Payback and ROCE):

  • DCF methods account for the time value of money — money today is worth more than money tomorrow
  • DCF methods use cash flows not accounting profits — cash is objective; profit is affected by accounting policies
  • DCF considers all cash flows over the entire project life — payback ignores flows after recovery
  • NPV directly measures shareholder wealth maximisation — the primary financial objective

Inflation — Two Approaches (D2a)

Inflation affects investment appraisal in two ways: it affects the cash flows themselves, and it affects the discount rate. ACCA requires you to know both approaches and when to use each.

Fisher Equation (linking real and nominal rates)
(1 + nominal rate) = (1 + real rate) × (1 + inflation rate) ∴ Real rate = [(1 + nominal) ÷ (1 + inflation)] – 1
ApproachCash FlowsDiscount RateWhen to Use
Nominal (Money Terms) Inflate to include inflation — "actual money received/paid" Nominal/money rate When different items inflate at different rates; with taxation (preferred in exam)
Real Terms Keep at today's prices — exclude inflation Real rate Only when ALL items inflate at the same (general) rate
⚠️ Critical Exam Point
Both approaches give the same NPV — they are equivalent. However, when cash flows inflate at different rates (e.g., revenues at 4%, costs at 2%), you MUST use the nominal approach. When taxation is involved, always use the nominal approach.
Example Inflation — Nominal vs Real

Project: Cash flow of AED 100,000 at today's prices, receivable in Year 1. General inflation: 5%. Real rate: 8%.

Nominal Approach:

  • Nominal cash flow Year 1 = 100,000 × 1.05 = AED 105,000
  • Nominal discount rate = (1.08 × 1.05) – 1 = 13.4%
  • DF Year 1 = 1 ÷ 1.134 = 0.882
  • PV = 105,000 × 0.882 = AED 92,610

Real Approach:

  • Cash flow stays at AED 100,000 (today's prices)
  • Real discount rate = 8%
  • DF Year 1 @ 8% = 0.926
  • PV = 100,000 × 0.926 = AED 92,600

Same answer (small rounding difference) — both approaches are valid ✅

Taxation in Investment Appraisal (D2b)

Tax is a cash flow! In ACCA FM, you deal with two types of tax cash flows:

1. Tax on Trading Cash Flows (Operating Cash Flows)

  • Tax is calculated on taxable profits (revenue – allowable costs)
  • Tax is typically paid one year in arrears (e.g., Year 1 profits taxed in Year 2)
  • Tax payments are cash outflows
  • Tax rate × taxable operating cash flow = tax payable

2. Tax-Allowable Depreciation (TADs) / Capital Allowances

The government allows companies to deduct a portion of capital expenditure against taxable profits each year. This generates a tax saving (cash inflow benefit).

Writing Down Allowance (WDA) — Reducing Balance Method
Year 1: WDA = Cost × Tax depreciation rate (e.g., 25%) Year 2: WDA = (Cost – Year 1 WDA) × rate Tax saving = WDA × tax rate Tax saving is received ONE year after the WDA period (one year in arrears)
Balancing Allowance / Charge (on disposal)
When asset is sold: Compare Tax Written Down Value (TWDV) to sale proceeds If TWDV > proceeds: Balancing Allowance (gives extra tax saving) If proceeds > TWDV: Balancing Charge (creates extra tax payment)
Example Tax and Capital Allowances — Full Worked Example

After-Tax Discount Rate (D2c)

The cost of capital used in after-tax DCF analysis should itself be an after-tax rate. For debt, the after-tax cost = pre-tax cost × (1 – tax rate), because interest is tax-deductible.

After-Tax Cost of Debt
Kd(after-tax) = Kd(pre-tax) × (1 – t) Example: 8% pre-tax, 25% tax rate → Kd(after-tax) = 8% × (1–0.25) = 6%

Adjusting for Risk and Uncertainty

D3(a) Risk vs Uncertainty D3(b) Sensitivity Analysis D3(c) Probability Analysis D3(d) Simulation, Adjusted Payback, Risk-adj. Rates

Risk vs Uncertainty (D3a)

ConceptDefinitionHow Measured
Risk Variability where probabilities can be assigned to possible outcomes Expected Value, Standard Deviation, Probability tables
Uncertainty Variability where probabilities cannot be assigned — the future is truly unknown Sensitivity Analysis, Simulation, Scenario Analysis
💡 Remember
Uncertainty increases with project life — the further into the future, the less certain outcomes become.

Sensitivity Analysis (D3b)

Sensitivity analysis asks: "By how much can a variable change before the project becomes unacceptable (NPV = 0)?" It identifies which variables the NPV is most sensitive to.

Sensitivity Formula
Sensitivity = NPV ÷ PV of the variable being tested × 100% The lower the %, the MORE sensitive (more critical) the variable is
Example Sensitivity Analysis — Revenue

A project has NPV = +AED 25,000. PV of revenues = AED 500,000.

Sensitivity of revenues = 25,000 ÷ 500,000 × 100% = 5%

This means revenue can fall by only 5% before NPV becomes zero. This variable is highly sensitive.

If PV of costs = AED 300,000:

Sensitivity of costs = 25,000 ÷ 300,000 × 100% = 8.3%

Costs are less sensitive than revenue — revenue is the more critical variable.

✅ Advantages

  • Simple to calculate and understand
  • Identifies critical variables
  • Focuses management attention
  • No probability estimates needed

❌ Disadvantages

  • Only changes one variable at a time — unrealistic
  • No probabilities — doesn't say how likely the change is
  • Does not give a clear accept/reject signal
  • Still subjective in interpretation

Probability Analysis / Expected Values (D3c)

When probabilities can be assigned, we calculate the Expected Net Present Value (ENPV).

Expected Value Formula
Expected Value (EV) = Σ (Probability × Outcome) ENPV = Σ (Probability × NPV of each scenario)
Example ENPV with Three Scenarios
ScenarioProbabilityNPV (AED)EV (AED)
Optimistic0.25200,00050,000
Most likely0.5080,00040,000
Pessimistic0.25(60,000)(15,000)
ENPV75,000

ENPV = +AED 75,000 → Accept (positive expected NPV).

⚠️ Limitation of EV
EV is a long-run average — if this is a one-off project, the outcome is either the optimistic, most likely, or pessimistic result, not the average. A risk-averse manager might focus on the worst case.

Other Risk Techniques (D3d)

Simulation (e.g., Monte Carlo)

A computer model assigns probability distributions to key variables and generates thousands of random scenarios. Produces a distribution of possible NPVs.

  • ✅ More realistic — many variables can change simultaneously
  • ❌ Expensive and complex; probabilities for distributions may be subjective

Adjusted Payback

Applying a shorter payback period requirement for riskier projects to compensate for uncertainty. Simple but crude — ignores cash flows after the reduced payback period.

Risk-Adjusted Discount Rates (RADR)

Apply a higher discount rate to higher-risk projects (e.g., cost of capital 10%, but use 15% for a high-risk project). This reduces the NPV of future cash flows more aggressively.

  • ✅ Easy to apply; adjusts for both time value and risk
  • ❌ Difficult to determine the appropriate risk premium objectively; applies increasing penalty to later cash flows even if risk is constant

Lease or Buy Decision (D4a)

Should a company lease an asset or borrow and buy it? This is a financing decision that evaluates the cheapest way to acquire the use of an asset.

💡 Approach
The decision to acquire the asset has already been made (positive NPV). The question is simply: which is the cheaper way to finance it — leasing or borrowing to buy?
Approach: Compare PV of costs under each option
Buy option: Include loan repayments (or asset cost), tax savings on capital allowances Lease option: Include lease payments, tax relief on lease payments Discount rate: After-tax cost of borrowing (since both options involve borrowing risk) Choose the option with the LOWER PV of costs (cheaper)

Asset Replacement Decisions (D4b)

When should you replace a machine? The key tool is the Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC) — also called Equivalent Annual Benefit (EAB). This converts NPV of costs into an annual figure, enabling fair comparison between assets with different lives.

Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC)
EAC = NPV of costs ÷ Annuity Factor for that period — Choose the asset with the LOWEST EAC (cheapest per year)
Equivalent Annual Benefit (EAB)
EAB = NPV ÷ Annuity Factor — Choose the project with the HIGHEST EAB
Example Asset Replacement — EAC

Two machines provide the same service. Discount rate: 10%.

ItemMachine A (3-year life)Machine B (5-year life)
Purchase costAED 90,000AED 120,000
Annual running costAED 25,000AED 18,000
PV of total costsAED 152,125AED 188,244
Annuity Factor @10%2.487 (3 years)3.791 (5 years)
EACAED 61,162AED 49,683
Decision
Machine B has a lower EAC (AED 49,683 vs AED 61,162) → Choose Machine B ✅

Without EAC, comparing NPVs of AED 152,125 vs AED 188,244 is misleading — Machine A appears cheaper, but it provides fewer years of service!

Optimal Replacement Cycle

When should you replace a machine with an identical one? Calculate the EAC for each possible cycle (replace every 1 year, 2 years, 3 years, etc.) and choose the cycle with the lowest EAC.

Capital Rationing (D4c)

Capital rationing occurs when a company has insufficient funds to invest in all positive NPV projects. The goal: maximise total NPV from the limited funds available.

TypeCauseSolution
Hard Rationing External restriction — capital markets limit borrowing PI / Profitability Index
Soft Rationing Internal management decision to limit investment budget PI / Linear Programming

Single-Period Capital Rationing

Profitability Index (PI) — for Divisible Projects
PI = NPV ÷ Initial Investment (or PV of investment) Rank projects by PI (highest first) and invest in order until funds run out For partial investment in divisible projects: scale NPV proportionally
Example Capital Rationing — Divisible Projects

Budget: AED 300,000. All projects are divisible.

⚠️ Non-Divisible Projects

When projects are non-divisible (cannot be partially undertaken), the PI approach may not work. Instead, enumerate all feasible combinations of projects within the budget and choose the combination with the highest total NPV.

Example: Budget AED 300,000; Projects: A (200k, NPV 40k), B (150k, NPV 45k), C (100k, NPV 20k). Test all combinations: A alone (200k→NPV 40k), B+C (250k→NPV 65k) ✅ best, A+C (300k→NPV 60k), B alone, C alone.

Why Capital Rationing Exists (Discussion Points)

  • Capital markets may be imperfect — information asymmetry makes external finance expensive
  • Management may be risk-averse and set arbitrary limits
  • Desire to maintain certain financial ratios (debt/equity, interest coverage)
  • New share issues may be perceived negatively by existing shareholders (dilution)

Top Exam Tips & Common Mistakes

🚫 Common Mistakes

  • Including depreciation as a cash flow (it's not!)
  • Including sunk costs (already spent = irrelevant)
  • Including finance costs (interest) in cash flows — it's in the discount rate
  • Forgetting working capital outflow at start and recovery at end
  • Forgetting tax is paid one year in arrears
  • Using nominal cash flows with a real discount rate (or vice versa)
  • In ROCE: using cash flows instead of accounting profit

✅ Exam Scoring Tips

  • Always state the decision (accept/reject) — easy mark
  • Show workings clearly in separate columns
  • For IRR, pick rates that give one + and one – NPV
  • For sensitivity: lower % = more critical
  • Use ACCA tables for discount factors — always provided
  • State which ROCE formula you're using (initial or average)
  • For capital rationing: always use PI for divisible; enumerate for non-divisible

Key Relationships to Memorise

  • NPV > 0 → IRR > cost of capital → Accept
  • NPV < 0 → IRR < cost of capital → Reject
  • Real rate: (1+n) = (1+r)(1+i) — Fisher equation
  • Sensitivity = NPV ÷ PV of variable — lower % = more sensitive
  • EAC = NPV of costs ÷ Annuity Factor — choose lowest
  • PI = NPV ÷ Investment — rank for divisible capital rationing
  • Discounted payback period > Simple payback period (always)
