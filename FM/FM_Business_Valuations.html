Why We Value, and What We Need to Do It MEDIUM

Syllabus F1 · The framing that every Section F answer should start from

Reasons for valuing a business or financial asset

A valuation is needed whenever shares change hands and there is no reliable market price to rely on:

  • Takeovers and mergers — to establish the terms of the deal (by far the most commonly examined context)
  • Buy-and-hold investment decisions — to decide whether a share is under- or over-priced
  • Flotation — to set an issue price for a company entering the stock market
  • Directors' and employees' shareholdings — where the articles require a retiring director to sell their shares back
  • Fiscal purposes — capital gains tax and inheritance tax computations
  • Legal settlements — divorce, partnership dissolution, disputes between shareholders
⚠
A common MCQ distractor

Valuation is not required for corporate taxation purposes — corporation tax is charged on profits, not on the value of the company. Watch for this in objective test questions listing "reasons for valuation".

Market capitalisation — and why it isn't the answer

Market capitalisation = Current share price × Number of shares in issue

For a listed company this seems like the obvious valuation. But it has real limitations:

  • Share prices move constantly with supply and demand, so the market capitalisation is a moving target
  • It does not represent the price at which the whole company could actually be bought — acquirers routinely pay a substantial premium over the pre-bid price to secure control
  • It simply does not exist for an unlisted company — which is exactly the situation most exam questions describe

Information requirements and their limitations

Information neededLimitation
Published financial statementsHistoric, prepared on a historical cost basis, and subject to accounting policy choices. Book values often bear little relation to current values. For an unlisted company they may be abbreviated and unaudited.
Asset valuationsProfessional valuations are costly and quickly go out of date. Intangibles — brands, know-how, customer relationships — are often not recognised at all.
Forecast earnings and cash flowsEstimates by definition, and normally prepared by the party with an interest in the outcome. Highly sensitive to assumptions about growth.
Comparable company data (e.g. P/E ratios)No two companies are truly comparable in size, growth prospects, gearing and risk. Listed comparators are not directly applicable to unlisted targets.
Cost of capitalFor an unlisted company there is no share price and no beta, so the discount rate must be estimated from proxies — introducing significant error.
📝
The framing sentence that earns marks in every Section F discussion

"Valuation is an art, not a science." Different methods give widely differing results; each party has an incentive to argue for a high or a low figure; and the final price is ultimately a matter of negotiation between buyer and seller. Examiners look for evidence that you understand a valuation is a range and a bargaining position, not a single correct number. Build this into your conclusion every time.

The natural structure: a net realisable value basis gives the seller's minimum price (they would do better breaking the company up than accepting less); a replacement cost basis gives the buyer's maximum price (they could recreate the business from scratch for that). The deal is struck somewhere in between.

Asset-Based Valuation Models HIGH

Syllabus F2(a) · Three bases, each answering a different question

The common formula

All three asset bases work identically. Only the values you put in change.

FORMULA — asset-based value of equity Value of equity = Total assets − Total liabilities Value per share = Value of equityNumber of ordinary shares
Deduct all liabilities, including preference share capital and any redemption premium on loan notes.
Intangible assets and goodwill are normally excluded — they cannot be sold or replaced separately.
BasisAssets valued at…What it tells you
Net book value
(SOFP basis)		Carrying amount straight from the statement of financial positionThe easiest to calculate but the least useful — based on historic cost less accumulated depreciation, which reflects accounting policy rather than value.
Net realisable value
(NRV / break-up basis)		Amount each asset could be sold for, less selling costsThe minimum price a seller should accept — below this they would be better off liquidating the company and selling the assets piecemeal.
Net replacement costCost of buying an equivalent asset todayThe maximum price a buyer should pay — above this they could simply build an equivalent business from scratch.
💡
Deprival value

Occasionally examined as a definition: deprival value is the lower of replacement cost and recoverable amount — the loss the owner would suffer if deprived of the asset. It is not simply replacement cost; an MCQ may test exactly that distinction.

Worked example

Strengths, weaknesses, and when to use it

StrengthsWeaknesses
Objective and relatively easy to calculateIgnores future earnings and cash flows entirely — the main reason anyone buys a business
Provides a useful floor value (NRV) and ceiling value (replacement cost) to frame negotiationsExcludes intangibles — brands, skilled workforce, customer relationships, know-how — which for many businesses are the value
Highly relevant for asset-stripping acquisitions and for property or investment companiesNBV is based on historic cost and accounting policy, so is largely meaningless as a value

Most appropriate for: asset-rich businesses (property, investment trusts, shipping), companies being broken up, and as a supporting cross-check alongside an earnings- or cash flow-based valuation.

💡
Goodwill is the gap between the two approaches

Goodwill can only be measured as income-based value less tangible asset value. That means a pure asset-based valuation of a going concern is never really complete — you always have to consider an earnings-based value as well to know what the goodwill is worth. Saying this explicitly is a strong discussion point.

Income / Earnings-Based Valuation HIGH

Syllabus F2(b) · P/E ratio and earnings yield — the most commonly examined models

When to use an earnings-based method

Earnings-based methods are appropriate for valuing a majority (controlling) shareholding. The reason is important and worth stating in an answer: a majority shareholder controls the company's dividend policy, so they are not restricted to the dividends currently being paid — they are buying the whole earnings stream and can decide what to do with it. A minority shareholder cannot influence dividend policy, so a dividend-based model suits them better.

The P/E ratio method

FORMULA — P/E ratio valuation P/E ratio = Market price per shareEarnings per share Value of company = Total earnings × P/E ratio Value per share = EPS × P/E ratio
Earnings = profit after tax and after preference dividends — i.e. earnings attributable to ordinary shareholders
The P/E ratio used is that of a comparable quoted company (or the industry average), suitably adjusted.
EXAMPLE 3.1 — P/E RATIO VALUATION
An unquoted company has issued ordinary share capital of 400,000 shares of 25c. Extract from the statement of profit or loss:

Profit before income taxes$260,000
Corporation tax($120,000)
Profit for the year$140,000
Preference dividend($20,000)
Ordinary dividend($36,000)
Retained profit$84,000

The P/E ratio applicable to a similar business, suitable for an unquoted company, is 12.5.

Value a holding of 200,000 shares.
Step 1 — Earnings attributable to ordinary shareholders
Profit for the year $140,000 − preference dividend $20,000 = $120,000

The ordinary dividend is not deducted — it is a distribution of earnings, not a cost. Only the preference dividend comes off, because it belongs to a different class of shareholder.

Step 2 — EPS
EPS = $120,000400,000 shares = 30c

Note the 25c nominal value is a distractor — EPS uses the number of shares, not their nominal value.

Step 3 — Apply the P/E ratio
Value per share = $0.30 × 12.5 = $3.75 Value of 200,000 shares = 200,000 × $3.75 = $750,000

Equivalently: total earnings $120,000 × 12.5 = $1,500,000 for the whole company; the 50% holding is worth half of that.

Choosing and adjusting the P/E ratio

The starting point is a quoted company of comparable size in the same industry. But a raw comparable P/E almost always needs adjusting, and the marks are in explaining why, not in the size of the adjustment.

Reason to adjustDirection and rationale
Target is unquotedReduce — shares in an unquoted company are far less marketable, so investors require a higher return and will pay a lower multiple. A reduction of around one third is a common convention.
Different growth prospectsReduce if the target's growth is lower than the comparator's, increase if higher. Growth is the single biggest driver of P/E differences.
Different risk / gearingReduce if the target is riskier or more highly geared, since investors demand a higher return.
Different sizeReduce if the target is much smaller — smaller companies are generally regarded as riskier and less liquid.

A workable convention in the exam is to adjust by roughly 10% for each reason identified, but state clearly what you are adjusting for and why. The examiner cares about the reasoning.

⚠
Interpreting a high or low P/E ratio

A high P/E may mean: a genuine growth stock; a stock whose last reported earnings were exceptionally low (so the ratio is distorted); a share price inflated by a pending takeover bid; or a high-security share where investors accept low income for capital growth and safety.

A low P/E may mean: losses or falling profits are expected; or the share price has been temporarily depressed by a one-off event such as a strike. Never assume a low P/E means "cheap".

Problems with the P/E method

  • Finding a genuinely similar company with similar growth prospects is difficult — most companies are not directly comparable
  • Reported earnings are based on historical cost accounts and different accounting policies, which undermines comparison between two companies
  • The P/E is based on a single year's reported earnings, whereas a share price reflects expectations of future earnings
  • Estimating maintainable earnings for the target is difficult — you may need to strip out one-off items (a debt write-off, an exceptional gain) or normalise abnormal director remuneration

The earnings yield method

The earnings yield is simply the inverse of the P/E ratio, so it produces the same answer when applied consistently. Its advantage is that it can easily incorporate earnings growth.

FORMULA — earnings yield Earnings yield = EPSMarket price per share = 1P/E ratio Value of company = Earnings × 1Earnings yield With growth:  Value = Earnings × (1 + g)Earnings yield − g
The growth version is the dividend growth model with earnings substituted for dividends and earnings yield substituted for the cost of equity.
EXAMPLE 3.2 — EARNINGS YIELD, WITH AND WITHOUT GROWTH
Company A has earnings of $300,000, growing at 3% per year. A similar listed company has an earnings yield of 12.5%.
Company B has earnings of $420,500 with no growth expected. A similar listed company has a P/E ratio of 7.

Estimate the value of each company.
Company A — with growth
Value = $300,000 × 1.030.125 − 0.03 = $309,0000.095 = $3,252,632
Company B — no growth
Value = $420,500 × 7 = $2,943,500

Equivalently, earnings yield = 1 ÷ 7 = 14.29%, and $420,500 ÷ 0.1429 = $2,943,500. Same answer either way — which is exactly the point about the two methods being equivalent.

EXAMPLE 3.3 — CONVERTING BETWEEN P/E AND EARNINGS YIELD
A company has earnings of $150 million. A comparable listed company trades on a P/E ratio of 12.5. Value the company using the earnings yield method.
Solution
Earnings yield = 1 ÷ 12.5 = 0.08 (8%) Value = $150m × (1 ÷ 0.08) = $1,875 million

Cash Flow-Based Valuation HIGH

Syllabus F2(c) · The dividend valuation model and the discounted cash flow basis

Method 1 — The dividend valuation model (DVM)

You met this model in the cost of capital chapter. Here it is used in the other direction: instead of solving for Ke, we know Ke and solve for the price.

FORMULA — DVM (given on the exam formula sheet) P0 = Dre   (constant dividend)     P0 = D0(1 + g)re − g   (constant growth)
re = shareholders' required return (the cost of equity)
Use dividend per share to get a share price, or total dividends to get the whole company value
The model produces an ex-div price — add back D0 if a cum-div value is required
💡
Who is this valuation for?

The DVM is best suited to valuing a minority shareholding. A minority shareholder cannot influence dividend policy, so the dividends actually paid are genuinely all they will receive. For a controlling stake the DVM understates value, because a majority shareholder controls the earnings, cash flows and assets — not just the dividend stream. Say this whenever you use the DVM in a scenario.

EXAMPLE 4.1 — DVM WITH CAPM SUPPLYING THE DISCOUNT RATE
A company has 4 million ordinary shares of 50c nominal in issue. The current dividend per share, just paid, is 24c; the dividend four years ago was 15.25c. The company's equity beta is 0.8. The current market return is 15% and the risk-free rate is 8%.

Find the market capitalisation of the company.
Step 1 — Dividend growth rate (four years of growth)
g = ( 2415.25 )1/4 − 1 = (1.5738)0.25 − 1 = 12.0%
Step 2 — Cost of equity via CAPM
Ke = 8% + 0.8 × (15% − 8%) = 8% + 5.6% = 13.6%
Step 3 — Apply the DVM
P0 = 24c × 1.120.136 − 0.12 = 26.88c0.016 = 1,680c = $16.80
Market capitalisation = 4,000,000 × $16.80 = $67.2 million
⚠
Notice how fragile this answer is

The denominator here is only 0.016 — g (12.0%) is very close to Ke (13.6%). If the growth rate were 12.5% instead of 12.0%, the value would jump from $16.80 to roughly $24.50. This extreme sensitivity when g approaches Ke is the DVM's most serious practical weakness, and pointing it out in your answer demonstrates real understanding. Also note the model breaks down entirely if g ≥ Ke, giving a negative or infinite value.

Strengths and weaknesses of the DVM
StrengthsWeaknesses
Theoretically sound — value is the PV of the cash the investor actually receivesDifficult to estimate a future growth rate; assumes it is constant forever, which is untrue for most companies
Well suited to valuing a non-controlling (minority) interestHighly sensitive to its assumptions, especially when g is close to re
Uses observable data where the company has a dividend recordRequires g < re to work at all
Offers few advantages over earnings methods when valuing a controlling interest

Method 2 — Discounted cash flow (DCF) basis

Used when acquiring a majority shareholding, because a buyer of a business is really buying a stream of future operating cash flows. The maximum value of the business is the present value of those cash flows.

  1. Identify the relevant free cash flows — the cash the business generates after tax and after the reinvestment needed to sustain it.
  2. Select a discount rate reflecting the systematic risk of those cash flows (the acquirer's WACC, or a project-specific rate if the risk differs).
  3. Discount the cash flows to a present value — this gives the value of the whole entity.
  4. Deduct the market value of debt to arrive at the value of equity.
⚠
Entity value versus equity value

If you discount cash flows available to all providers of finance (i.e. before interest) at the WACC, you get the value of the whole entity. To get the value of the equity, you must then deduct the value of the debt. Forgetting this final step is one of the most common errors in DCF valuation questions.

EXAMPLE 4.2 — DCF VALUATION OF EQUITY
A target company is forecast to generate free cash flows of $40m, $50m and $210m in years 1, 2 and 3 respectively (year 3 includes a terminal value). The appropriate discount rate is 12%. The company has debt with a market value of $100m.

Calculate the value of the equity.
Solution

1Free cash flow40.000.89335.72

Strengths and weaknesses of DCF
StrengthsWeaknesses
Theoretically the best method — it directly values what the buyer is acquiringRelies on estimates of both future cash flows and the discount rate, which may be unavailable or unreliable
Explicitly captures growth prospects, so is especially suitable for high-growth companiesDifficult to choose an appropriate time horizon, and difficult to value the company's worth beyond it (the terminal value often dominates the answer)

The Valuation of Debt and Other Financial Assets HIGH

Syllabus F3 · The cost of capital formulae, rearranged

The single governing principle

Every debt valuation in FM is the same idea: the market value of a financial instrument is the present value of its future cash flows, discounted at the return investors require. The formulae from Section E are simply rearranged.

⚠
Use the investors' PRE-TAX required return

When valuing debt you are pricing it from the investor's perspective, and investors receive the full gross interest. So discount at the pre-tax required return and use gross interest — no (1 − T) anywhere. The post-tax figure is only used when calculating the company's cost of debt for the WACC. This is the single most common error in this topic.

Irredeemable debt and preference shares

Both are perpetuities, so both use the same formula:

FORMULAE — perpetual instruments Irredeemable debt:  MV = Ir       Preference shares:  P0 = DKp
I = annual interest per $100 nominal  ·  r = debt holders' required return
D = fixed annual preference dividend  ·  Kp = preference shareholders' required return
EXAMPLE 5.1 — IRREDEEMABLE DEBT AND PREFERENCE SHARES
(a) A company has issued irredeemable loan notes with a coupon rate of 7%. Investors require a return of 4%. What is the market value?
(b) A firm has 12% preference shares of $1 nominal value. Preference shareholders require a return of 14%. What is the value of a preference share?
Solution
(a) MV = $70.04 = $175 per $100 nominal (b) P0 = 12c0.14 = 85.71c

In (a), the loan notes trade far above par because the 7% coupon is much better than the 4% currently available in the market — investors bid the price up until the effective yield falls to 4%. In (b), the required return exceeds the coupon, so the share trades below its $1 nominal value. This inverse relationship between required return and market value is worth internalising.

Redeemable debt

METHOD — redeemable debt MV = PV of future interest payments + PV of the redemption amount, both discounted at the debt holders' required return
EXAMPLE 5.2 — REDEEMABLE DEBT

Convertible debt

A convertible gives the investor a choice at redemption, so it is worth at least as much as plain debt, plus something for the option to convert. Three figures may be asked for:

TermDefinition
Market value
(formula value)		The higher of its value as debt and its converted value. Calculated as the PV of future interest plus the PV of whichever terminal amount investors will choose.
Floor valueThe value the instrument would have without the conversion option — i.e. PV of future interest plus PV of the cash redemption value. It is a floor because the conversion option can only add value, never subtract it.
Conversion premiumMarket value − current conversion value (today's share price × conversion ratio). It measures how much extra investors are paying above the immediate share value, reflecting expected future share price growth.

The Efficient Market Hypothesis MEDIUM-HIGH

Syllabus F4(a) · Reliably tested in objective test questions

What "efficient" means

A capital market is efficient when share prices fully and quickly reflect all available information. In an efficient market, prices always represent the best available estimate of a share's true value, so no investor can consistently make abnormal returns — returns above those justified by the risk taken.

The three forms differ only in what information is meant by "all available information".

FormPrices reflect…Implication
Weak formAll information about past share price movementsFuture price movements cannot be predicted from historic patterns — prices follow a "random walk". Technical analysis (chartism) is worthless.
Semi-strong formPast prices plus all publicly available information (financial statements, announcements, press coverage)Prices adjust quickly and accurately as new public information is released. Fundamental analysis of public data cannot produce abnormal gains — only non-public (insider) information could.
Strong formAll information — public and private/insiderNo investor can ever earn abnormal returns, even with inside information. Generally regarded as not holding in practice, which is why insider dealing is illegal and profitable.
💡
Where real markets sit

Empirical evidence suggests major stock markets are semi-strong form efficient at best. This is the standard assumption in FM, and it matters elsewhere in the syllabus — it is one of the criticisms levelled at CAPM and at Modigliani & Miller, both of which assume perfect (i.e. strong-form) markets.

⚠
Typical MCQ traps
  • "If the market is semi-strong efficient, newly-released insider information will be reflected in the price." False — semi-strong efficiency covers publicly available information only. Once insider information becomes public it will be reflected, but not while it remains private.
  • An investor who studies past price charts expecting to beat the market must believe the market is not even weak form efficient — because if it were, past price data would already be in the price.
  • Under semi-strong efficiency, studying published accounts cannot produce consistent gains; only non-public information could.

Practical Considerations in Valuation MEDIUM

Syllabus F4(b), (c) · Where the discussion marks are

Factors affecting the value reached

FactorEffect on the valuation
Marketability and liquidityShares in an unlisted company are difficult to sell — there is no market, finding a buyer takes time, and the price is uncertain. Investors therefore require a higher return, so a discount is applied (commonly around one third off a comparable listed P/E ratio). Even among listed companies, thinly traded small-company shares attract a liquidity discount.
Size of the stakeA control premium is paid for a stake conferring control, because the buyer gains power over dividends, earnings, assets and strategy. Conversely a minority discount applies to a small holding with no influence. Always identify the size of the stake before choosing a method.
Availability and quality of informationListed companies publish extensively and are widely analysed. Unlisted companies may file only abbreviated accounts, so forecasts must be built on much weaker foundations — widening the range of plausible values.
Market imperfections and pricing anomaliesReal markets do not behave as the EMH predicts. Examples: overreaction to unexpected news; low trading volumes in small-company shares preventing an efficient equilibrium price; calendar effects; and momentum. These mean an observed market price may not be a reliable guide to fundamental value.
Market capitalisationAvailable only for listed companies, moves constantly, and is routinely exceeded by the price actually paid in a takeover.

Investor speculation and behavioural finance LOW

Speculation is buying and selling in the hope of short-term price movements rather than long-term fundamental value. Heavy speculative activity can push prices away from fundamentals and increase volatility.

Behavioural finance challenges the EMH's core assumption that investors are rational. It argues that predictable psychological biases lead to systematic mispricing:

  • Herding — following the crowd rather than one's own analysis, amplifying booms and crashes
  • Overconfidence — overestimating one's own judgement and trading too frequently
  • Anchoring — fixating on an irrelevant reference point, such as the price originally paid for a share
  • Loss aversion — feeling losses more keenly than equivalent gains, so holding losing shares too long and selling winners too early
  • Overreaction and under-reaction — prices moving too far, or too slowly, in response to news

The practical significance is that if investors are not rational, market prices may persistently deviate from fundamental value — undermining both the EMH and the use of a market price as a valuation benchmark.

Choosing a Method & Writing the Conclusion HIGH

Where Section C marks are won or lost

Matching method to circumstance

MethodBest suited toKey limitation
Net realisable valueSetting the seller's minimum price; asset-rich or loss-making companiesIgnores earnings, cash flows and intangibles
Replacement costSetting the buyer's maximum priceGoodwill cannot be measured on this basis alone
P/E ratio / earnings yieldA controlling stake, where a listed comparator existsFinding a truly comparable company; historic and accounting-policy-dependent earnings
DVMA minority stake in a mature, dividend-paying companyConstant growth assumption; extreme sensitivity when g nears Ke; unusable for non-dividend payers
DCFA controlling stake, especially in a high-growth company; captures synergiesForecast reliability, time horizon and terminal value; assumes constant discount rate

A reusable structure for the discussion requirement

  1. Perspective — is this valuation for the buyer or the seller? Each will argue for a different figure. NRV sets the seller's floor; replacement cost sets the buyer's ceiling.
  2. Size of stake — a controlling stake justifies earnings- or cash flow-based methods and a control premium; a minority stake points to the DVM and a marketability discount.
  3. Nature of the company — an asset-based value has little relevance to a company whose worth lies in intangibles or growth prospects. A DCF value is particularly relevant where significant growth is expected.
  4. Quality of the inputs — comment on how reliable the earnings figures, growth rates and discount rate actually are, and note the sensitivity of your answer to them.
  5. Conclusion — give a range, state which method you consider most appropriate and why, and note that the final price will be a matter for negotiation. Valuation is an art, not a science.
📝
Don't just calculate

A Section C valuation question typically awards roughly half its marks for calculations and half for discussion. Candidates who produce four immaculate valuations and then stop, without saying which is most appropriate or acknowledging the subjectivity, routinely lose half the available marks. Always finish with a reasoned recommendation.

Test Yourself — 10 Questions

Objective-test style, covering the whole of Section F.

QUESTION 01 P/E method
A company has profit after tax of $500,000, preference dividends of $50,000, and 900,000 ordinary shares in issue. A comparable listed company trades on a P/E ratio of 14.
What is the value per ordinary share?

Correct answer: A — $7.00

Earnings attributable to ordinary shareholders = $500,000 − $50,000 = $450,000
EPS = $450,000 ÷ 900,000 = $0.50
Value per share = $0.50 × 14 = $7.00

Option B ($7.78) forgets to deduct the preference dividend. The P/E ratio is applied to earnings available to ordinary shareholders only.

QUESTION 02 Earnings yield
A company has earnings of $80 million. A comparable listed company trades on a P/E ratio of 10.
Using the earnings yield method, what is the value of the company?

Correct answer: C — $800 million

Earnings yield = 1 ÷ P/E = 1 ÷ 10 = 0.10 (10%)
Value = Earnings × (1 ÷ earnings yield) = $80m × 10 = $800 million

This confirms the two methods are equivalent: $80m × P/E of 10 gives the same $800m. Option A ($8m) divides by the P/E instead of multiplying.

QUESTION 03 Asset basis
A company has non-current assets with a carrying value of $800,000 (market value $950,000), net current assets of $150,000, intangible goodwill of $200,000, and loan notes of $300,000 redeemable at a 5% premium.
What is the net realisable value of the equity?

Correct answer: A — $785,000

Non-current assets at market value $950,000 + net current assets $150,000 = $1,100,000
Less loan notes at redemption value: $300,000 × 1.05 = ($315,000)
Net realisable value = $785,000

Goodwill is excluded — intangibles cannot be realised separately. Option C ($985,000) wrongly includes the goodwill. Option B deducts the loan notes at nominal value, ignoring the redemption premium.

QUESTION 04 DVM
A company has just paid a dividend of 20c per share. Dividends are expected to grow at 6% per year and shareholders require a return of 14%.
What is the ex-div market value of a share?

Correct answer: C — $2.65

P0 = D0(1 + g) ÷ (re − g) = (20 × 1.06) ÷ (0.14 − 0.06) = 21.2 ÷ 0.08 = 265c = $2.65

Option B ($2.50) omits the (1 + g) uplift, using 20 ÷ 0.08. Remember D0 is the dividend just paid and must be grown forward one year.

QUESTION 05 Valuing debt
A company has irredeemable loan notes in issue with a coupon rate of 5%. Investors currently require a return of 8%. Corporation tax is 25%.
What is the market value per $100 nominal?

Correct answer: B — $62.50

MV = I ÷ r = $5 ÷ 0.08 = $62.50

The tax rate is a distractor. When valuing debt you take the investor's perspective, and investors receive the full gross interest — so there is no (1 − T) adjustment. Option A ($46.88) wrongly applies the tax shield: [$5 × 0.75] ÷ 0.08.

QUESTION 06 Convertibles
A convertible loan note is currently worth $115. It can be converted into 25 ordinary shares. The current share price is $3.80.
What is the conversion premium per loan note?

Correct answer: B — $20.00

Current conversion value = 25 shares × $3.80 = $95.00
Conversion premium = market value − current conversion value = $115.00 − $95.00 = $20.00

Per share this is $20.00 ÷ 25 = $0.80 (option C — correct only if the question had asked for the premium per share). Read the requirement carefully.

QUESTION 07 EMH
Consider the three forms of the efficient market hypothesis.
If a capital market is semi-strong form efficient, which of the following is correct?

Correct answer: B

Semi-strong efficiency means all publicly available information is already in the price, so analysing public data such as published accounts cannot produce consistent gains. Only non-public information could.

QUESTION 08 Method selection
An investor is valuing a 5% shareholding in a mature, listed company with a long record of stable dividend payments.
Which valuation method is most appropriate?

Correct answer: C — dividend valuation model

A 5% holding is a minority stake. The investor cannot influence dividend policy, earnings or assets, so the dividends actually paid are genuinely all they will receive. A mature company with a stable dividend record also fits the DVM's constant-growth assumption well.

Options A, B and D all value the whole business — appropriate for a controlling stake, but not for a passive 5% holder who cannot access those assets or cash flows.

QUESTION 09 DCF
A target company's free cash flows have a present value of $340 million. It has debt with a market value of $85 million and cash of $20 million (already included in the cash flow forecast).
What is the value of the equity?

Correct answer: B — $255 million

The PV of free cash flows gives the value of the entity. To reach the value of the equity, deduct the market value of debt: $340m − $85m = $255 million.

The cash of $20m is a distractor — the question states it is already reflected in the forecast cash flows, so adding it again would double-count. Option C forgets to deduct debt at all, the most common error in DCF valuation.

QUESTION 10 Practical issues
An acquirer is valuing an unlisted target company using a P/E ratio drawn from a similar listed company.
Which adjustment to the listed company's P/E ratio would normally be appropriate?

Correct answer: C

Shares in an unlisted company are much harder to sell — there is no ready market, so investors bear liquidity risk and demand a higher return, which translates into a lower multiple. A reduction of around one third is a common convention, though the reasoning matters far more than the exact percentage.

Other legitimate reasons to adjust include differences in growth prospects, risk, gearing and size — but always state why you are adjusting.
