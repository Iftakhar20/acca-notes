1. Why NPV, and what the syllabus demands

Definition

Net present value is the sum of the discounted future cash flows of a project, less the initial investment. It measures the absolute increase in shareholder wealth in today's AED that the project is expected to deliver.

NPV = Σ [ CFt ÷ (1 + i)t ] where CFt is the net relevant cash flow at time t, and i is the cost of capital

Decision rule: accept if NPV > 0; reject if NPV < 0. Where projects are mutually exclusive, choose the highest positive NPV.

Why NPV is theoretically superior

  • It uses cash flows, not accounting profits, which are subject to policy choices.
  • It accounts for the time value of money — a dirham today is worth more than a dirham next year because of the opportunity to earn a return, inflation, and risk.
  • It considers all cash flows over the whole project life (unlike payback).
  • It gives an absolute measure of wealth added, which is directly consistent with the objective of maximising shareholder wealth.
  • Risk can be reflected through a risk-adjusted discount rate.

Drawbacks you must be able to discuss

  • Estimating future cash flows accurately is difficult, especially far out.
  • The cost of capital is itself an estimate and may change over the project life.
  • Non-financial managers may find it less intuitive than payback or ROCE.
  • It assumes cash flows arise at year ends and that intermediate cash can be reinvested at the cost of capital.

Super Health connection

In an FP&A role in a public healthcare body, NPV appraisal of a new imaging suite or day-surgery unit is exactly the D1/D2 skill set. Note however that a not-for-profit or public sector body (Syllabus A4) will often supplement NPV with value for money criteria — economy, efficiency, effectiveness — because many benefits are non-financial. Examiners love a sentence linking these two areas.

What the Sep 26–Jun 27 syllabus actually says

2. Relevant cash flows

Before any discounting, you must decide what goes in the table. A relevant cash flow is one that is:

The three tests

  1. Future — it has not yet happened.
  2. Incremental — it arises only because the project goes ahead (the difference between "with" and "without").
  3. Cash — it involves an actual movement of money.

Include

  • Initial capital expenditure and any subsequent replacement capex.
  • Incremental revenues and incremental cash operating costs.
  • Opportunity costs — the benefit forgone from the next best use of a resource (e.g. rental income lost on a floor of a clinic that will now house the project).
  • Tax payments and tax savings.
  • Working capital movements.
  • Scrap / disposal proceeds.

Exclude

  • Depreciation — a book entry, not cash. (Tax-allowable depreciation is different: the tax saving it generates is cash.)
  • Sunk costs — already incurred, e.g. a feasibility study already paid for.
  • Committed costs — will be incurred regardless.
  • Apportioned fixed overheads — only genuinely incremental fixed costs are relevant.
  • Interest payments and loan repayments — the cost of finance is already reflected in the discount rate. Including interest double-counts it.

Classic examiner trap

A question gives you "profit" figures that are stated after charging depreciation and apportioned head office overhead, and includes interest. You must add back depreciation, add back apportioned overhead, add back interest to get to the cash flow. Read the note under the table — it is always there.

3. Discounting mechanics

You will be given discount tables in the exam, but the formulae matter for awkward rates (e.g. 10.24%) and for perpetuities, which are not in the tables.

Single sum

PV = FV × (1 + r)−n

Annuity (equal cash flow, years 1 to n)

Annuity factor = [1 − (1 + r)−n] ÷ r

Perpetuity (equal cash flow forever, from year 1)

PV = Cash flow ÷ r   |   Growing perpetuity: PV = CF1 ÷ (r − g)

Timing rules — memorise these

  • Ordinary annuity: first flow at T1. Use the annuity factor as given.
  • Advanced annuity (first flow at T0): use the annuity factor for (n−1) years and add 1.
  • Delayed annuity (first flow at T3, say): use AF for the full span, then subtract the AF for the years with no flow. Alternatively, discount the annuity's PV back by one extra year than you'd instinctively think.
  • Advanced perpetuity (first flow at T0): PV = CF/r + CF.
  • Delayed perpetuity (first flow at T4): PV = (CF/r) × DF for year 3.
EXAMPLE 3

Four timing variants at 10%

Table factors: 10% AF 2yr = 1.736; 4yr = 3.170; 7yr = 4.868. DF: yr2 = 0.826; yr3 = 0.751.

(a) Ordinary annuity. AED 200,000 per year, T1–T4.
PV = 200,000 × 3.170 = AED 634,000

(b) Advanced annuity. AED 100,000 per year for 5 years, first payment today.
AF = 3.170 (4 yr) + 1 = 4.170 → PV = 100,000 × 4.170 = AED 417,000

(c) Delayed annuity. AED 200,000 per year, T3 to T7.
AF = 4.868 (7 yr) − 1.736 (2 yr) = 3.132 → PV = 200,000 × 3.132 = AED 626,400

(d) Delayed perpetuity. AED 250,000 per year forever, first receipt at T4, rate 8%.
PV at T3 = 250,000 ÷ 0.08 = AED 3,125,000
PV at T0 = 3,125,000 × 0.794 (8% yr 3 DF) = AED 2,481,250

EXAMPLE 4

Growing perpetuity

A pharmacy licensing arrangement generates AED 600,000 at T1, growing at 3% per annum indefinitely. Cost of capital 11%.

PV = 600,000 ÷ (0.11 − 0.03) = 600,000 ÷ 0.08 = AED 7,500,000

Note: the numerator is the year 1 flow, not the year 0 flow. If told the flow "has just been" AED 600,000 and grows at 3%, the numerator becomes 600,000 × 1.03 = 618,000, giving AED 7,725,000.

Test yourself: AED 50,000 per year in perpetuity, first receipt today, discount rate 10%. PV?

50,000 ÷ 0.10 = 500,000, plus the immediate 50,000 = AED 550,000.

Exam tip — always write a conclusion

One or two marks in every Section C NPV question are for a comment. Say (i) whether NPV is positive/negative, (ii) therefore accept/reject, (iii) at least one caveat about the reliability of the forecasts or the discount rate. If the NPV is small relative to the investment, sensitise it — say the conclusion is marginal and small forecasting errors could reverse the decision.

5. Inflation: real vs money (nominal)

This is the single biggest source of confusion in D2. Get the vocabulary straight first.

Four terms

  • Money (nominal) cash flow — the actual number of dirhams that will change hands, i.e. including inflation.
  • Real cash flow — expressed in today's purchasing power, i.e. excluding inflation ("current prices", "today's terms").
  • Money (nominal) rate — the return that includes compensation for inflation. Market rates you observe are money rates.
  • Real rate — the return excluding inflation.

The Fisher equation

(1 + i) = (1 + r) × (1 + h) i = money rate  ·  r = real rate  ·  h = general rate of inflation

Trap: h is the GENERAL inflation rate

Never use a specific inflation rate (e.g. "labour costs inflate at 6%") in the Fisher equation. Only the general rate of inflation converts between real and money rates. Specific rates are used to inflate individual cash flows.

The golden rule — match the flow to the rate

ApproachCash flowsDiscount rate
Money / nominalInflated to actual money amountsMoney (nominal) rate
RealToday's prices, uninflatedReal rate

Both give the same NPV — but only when every cash flow inflates at the same, general, rate. Mixing them (real flows at a money rate, or vice versa) is an automatic loss of most of the marks.

When must you use the money method?

Use the money method whenever any of these appear — which in practice is almost every Section C question:

  • Different cash flows inflate at different specific rates
  • Tax is involved (tax-allowable depreciation is based on the actual money cost of the asset and does not inflate)
  • Working capital is based on inflated sales
EXAMPLE 6

Real and money give the same answer

A project costs AED 1,200,000 at T0 and generates a contribution of AED 500,000 per year in current terms for three years. General inflation is 4% and it is the only inflation affecting the project. The real cost of capital is 6%.

Money rate: (1 + i) = 1.06 × 1.04 = 1.1024 → i = 10.24%

Why you could not use the real method here

Revenue and costs inflate at different rates, and neither equals the general rate of 5%. There is no single set of "real" cash flows to discount. Attempting a real appraisal would require deflating each money flow by 5% first — extra work for the same answer.

The real-terms approach when tax is present (the "Pinks" shortcut)

Occasionally a question asks for a real-terms NPV in a question that also has tax — typically for 4 marks. A strictly correct real appraisal from scratch is very long. The accepted approach is:

  1. Take the pre-tax money (nominal) net cash flows you have already calculated.
  2. Deflate them by the general inflation rate: real flow = money flow ÷ (1 + h)n.
  3. Apply tax to the deflated flows.
  4. Discount at the real cost of capital.

6. Taxation in DCF

Tax has two separate effects in an FM NPV. Set them out as two separate rows; do not net them.

The two tax effects

  1. Tax on operating cash flows — an outflow, calculated as tax rate × net taxable operating cash flow.
  2. Tax saved on tax-allowable depreciation (TAD / capital allowances) — an inflow, calculated as tax rate × TAD.

Timing of tax

The question will say one of two things. Read it carefully — it is worth several marks.

  • "Tax is payable in the same year in which the profits arise" → tax cash flow at Tn, same column.
  • "Tax is payable one year in arrears" (most common) → tax cash flow at Tn+1. The table therefore extends one year beyond the end of the project.

Trap: forgetting the extra column

With a one-year delay on a 4-year project, your table runs T0 to T5. The final column usually contains only tax flows and is a net outflow. Candidates routinely stop at T4 and lose 3–4 marks.

Before- and after-tax discount rates (D2c)

After-tax cost of debt = Kd × (1 − T)

Because interest is tax-deductible, the company's effective cost of debt is reduced. Where a question gives a pre-tax cost of capital and asks for an after-tax appraisal, or in a lease vs buy decision, you use the after-tax cost of borrowing. In a standard NPV question the WACC given is already post-tax; do not adjust it again.

7. Tax-allowable depreciation (WDA / capital allowances)

This is the area you flagged, so we will go slowly. Accounting depreciation is never a cash flow. But tax authorities allow a deduction against taxable profit for capital spending, called tax-allowable depreciation (TAD), also known as writing-down allowances (WDA) or capital allowances. The tax saved is a real cash inflow.

Tax saving in a year = TAD for that year × tax rate

The reducing balance method (the FM default)

  1. Start with the cost of the asset.
  2. Each year, claim a fixed percentage (usually 25%) of the written-down value (WDV) brought forward.
  3. Deduct the claim to get the new WDV carried forward.
  4. In the final year, do not claim the normal percentage. Instead compare WDV with disposal proceeds:

Final year: balancing allowance or balancing charge

  • If WDV > proceeds → the company under-claimed → balancing allowance (extra deduction) → tax saving (inflow).
  • If proceeds > WDV → the company over-claimed → balancing charge (taxable amount) → tax cost (outflow).

Sense check that always works: total TAD claimed over the life must equal cost − disposal proceeds. If it doesn't, you have made an error.

Timing convention

Unless told otherwise, assume the asset is bought on the first day of an accounting period (T0), so the first TAD is claimed at T1. Then apply the tax delay (if any) on top. So with a 1-year delay, the first tax saving is received at T2.

Because TAD is calculated on the historic money cost actually paid for the asset. That cost is already a money figure. 8. Working capital

New projects usually tie up cash in inventory and receivables, net of payables. That investment is a real cash outflow even though it is not a cost.

The four rules

  1. Initial investment in working capital is an outflow at the start (usually T0).
  2. An increase in the requirement is an incremental outflow.
  3. A decrease in the requirement is an incremental inflow.
  4. At the end of the project, the whole cumulative balance is released as an inflow.

Two-step method

Step 1: Calculate the absolute working capital balance required at each point in time.
Step 2: Calculate the incremental movement between each point — that is what goes in the NPV table.

Traps

  • "In place at the start of each year." Working capital for year 1 sales is required at T0, for year 2 sales at T1, and so on. It is a one-year lead.
  • If instead the question says working capital is required at the end of each year, the year-1 balance sits at T1.
  • Working capital movements are not taxed — they are balance sheet movements, not profits.
  • Where inflation is present, base working capital on the inflated (money) sales figure, unless told otherwise.
  • The release at the end is the total cumulative balance, not the last year's increment.

EXAMPLE 17 — THE BIG ONE

Full NPV with tax, TAD, specific inflation and working capital

Scenario. Super Health is appraising the Al Barsha Day Surgery Unit.

  • Equipment and fit-out cost AED 8,000,000, payable now. Expected disposal proceeds at the end of year 3: AED 2,000,000.
  • Procedure volumes: year 1 4,000, year 2 5,000, year 3 4,500.
  • Price per procedure AED 2,000 and variable cost per procedure AED 800, both in current terms, both inflating at 5% per year.
  • Incremental fixed cash costs AED 1,000,000 per year in current terms, inflating at 4% per year.
  • Working capital equal to 10% of the year's sales revenue, required at the start of each year, fully recovered at the end.
  • Tax at 20%, payable one year in arrears. Tax-allowable depreciation at 25% reducing balance.

10. Sensitivity analysis on your NPV

Syllabus D3(b), but it attaches directly to NPV questions and is examined constantly alongside them.

Sensitivity (%) = NPV ÷ PV of the cash flow under consideration × 100

The answer is the percentage by which that variable can change adversely before the NPV falls to zero. The lower the percentage, the more sensitive — and therefore the more critical — the variable.

Trap

The denominator must be the post-tax PV of the flow if tax is present. If the sales price falls, taxable profit falls too, so the tax saving partially cushions the loss. Forgetting this overstates the sensitivity.

EXAMPLE 18

Sensitivity to the initial investment

Using Example 17: NPV = AED 4,017,391; PV of the initial equipment cost = AED 8,000,000 (it is at T0, so no discounting).

Sensitivity = 4,017,391 ÷ 8,000,000 = 50.2%

The equipment cost could overrun by more than half before the project became unattractive — not a critical variable.

EXAMPLE 19

Sensitivity to selling price (post-tax)

EXAMPLE 20

Sensitivity of the discount rate

The sensitivity of the cost of capital is not computed with the formula above. Instead it is the difference between the IRR and the current cost of capital.

If a project's IRR is 18% and the cost of capital is 10%, the discount rate could rise by 8 percentage points, or 80% in relative terms, before the project became unacceptable.

11. NPV vs IRR, payback and ROCE

IRR

The discount rate at which the NPV of a project is zero. Accept if IRR > cost of capital.

IRR ≈ L + [ NL ÷ (NL − NH) ] × (H − L) L = lower rate, H = higher rate, NL and NH = the NPVs at those rates (with signs)
EXAMPLE 21

Interpolating an IRR

NPV at 10% = +AED 42,000. NPV at 20% = −AED 22,000.

IRR = 10 + [42,000 ÷ (42,000 − (−22,000))] × (20 − 10) = 10 + (42,000/64,000) × 10 = 10 + 6.6 = 16.6%

Watch the signs. If you treat the −22,000 as positive you get 31.0%, a common wrong answer. If you swap the two NPVs you get 13.4%.

NPV vs IRR — the discussion marks

PointNPVIRR

Conclusion to write: NPV is theoretically superior because it directly measures the change in shareholder wealth, but IRR is popular because a percentage return is easily compared with a target rate and does not require the cost of capital to be known precisely in advance.

Payback and discounted payback

Payback = time to recover the initial investment from cumulative net cash flows. Simple, and useful as a liquidity/risk screen, but ignores the time value of money and all cash flows after payback. Discounted payback fixes the first flaw by using discounted cash flows, but still ignores post-payback flows and gives a longer (more conservative) period.

ROCE / ARR

ROCE = Average annual accounting profit ÷ Average investment × 100 Average investment = (initial cost + scrap value) ÷ 2
EXAMPLE 23

ROCE

Profits after depreciation over four years: AED 39,600, 19,600, 22,400, 32,400. Initial investment AED 110,400; scrap value AED 9,600.

Average profit = (39,600 + 19,600 + 22,400 + 32,400) ÷ 4 = AED 28,500
Average investment = (110,400 + 9,600) ÷ 2 = AED 60,000
ROCE = 28,500 ÷ 60,000 = 47.5%

Using the initial rather than average investment gives 25.8% — a frequent wrong answer. Read the question for which basis is required.

12. Examiner traps & exam technique

The twelve most-penalised errors

  1. Discounting real cash flows at a money rate (or vice versa).
  2. Using a specific inflation rate in the Fisher equation instead of the general rate.
  3. Forgetting the extra year column when tax is one year in arrears.
  4. Inflating tax-allowable depreciation — it is based on the fixed money cost.
  5. Claiming the normal 25% in the final year instead of computing the balancing figure.
  6. Treating a balancing charge as a saving rather than a tax cost.
  7. Omitting the disposal proceeds from the main cash flow table (they appear in the TAD working and in the main table, for different reasons).
  8. Failing to release working capital at the end, or releasing only the last increment.
  9. Taxing working capital movements.
  10. Including depreciation or interest as cash flows.
  11. Placing working capital at T1 when the question says it is required at the start of year 1 (= T0).
  12. Producing a beautiful table and no conclusion — throwing away 2 easy marks.

Technique in the CBE

  • Build the table in the spreadsheet tool with formulae, not hard-coded numbers — it makes corrections fast and earns method marks even if one input is wrong.
  • Use =NPV(rate, range) for T1 onwards, then add the T0 flow separately (Excel's NPV function assumes the first value is at T1).
  • Label your workings (W1, W2…) and cross-reference them in the main table. Markers award marks for workings even when the final NPV is wrong.
  • Own-figure rule applies: an error early on does not cascade into lost marks later if your method is right and visible.
  • Time budget: 20 marks = 36 minutes. Roughly 6 minutes reading and planning, 20 minutes on the table and workings, 10 minutes on the discussion parts. Do the discussion parts. They are the easiest marks in the question and are usually attempted last and badly.

13. MCQ practice bank

Q1. The real cost of capital is 7% and general inflation is 5%. Specific inflation on labour is 8%. What money cost of capital should be used?

  1. 12.0%
  2. 12.35%
  3. 15.56%
  4. 11.0%
Show answer

B — 12.35%. 1.07 × 1.05 = 1.1235. Option A is the incorrect additive approximation; C wrongly uses the specific labour rate.

Q2. A machine costs AED 1,200,000. TAD is 25% reducing balance, tax 20% payable in the same year. The machine is sold at the end of year 2 for AED 800,000. What is the tax cash flow arising from TAD in year 2?

  1. Saving of AED 60,000
  2. Saving of AED 20,000
  3. Cost of AED 20,000
  4. Saving of AED 45,000
Show answer

B — saving of AED 20,000. T1 TAD = 300,000 → WDV 900,000. At T2, WDV 900,000 exceeds proceeds 800,000, so there is a balancing allowance of 100,000 → tax saving 100,000 × 20% = AED 20,000.

Check: total TAD 300,000 + 100,000 = 400,000 = 1,200,000 − 800,000 ✓

Q3. Which of the following is NOT a relevant cash flow in an NPV appraisal?

  1. Rental income forgone on a building used by the project
  2. Interest payable on the loan raised to fund the project
  3. Redundancy costs arising because of the project
  4. Additional inventory required at the start of the project
Show answer

B. The cost of finance is captured in the discount rate; including interest double-counts it. A is an opportunity cost, C is an incremental future cash cost, D is a working capital outflow.

Q4. A project has a money cost of capital of 21% and general inflation is 8%. What is the real cost of capital?

  1. 13.0%
  2. 12.0%
  3. 11.6%
  4. 13.5%
Show answer

B — 12%. 1.21 ÷ 1.08 = 1.12. Option A is the additive approximation.

Q5. Working capital of 10% of sales is required at the start of each year. Sales are AED 500,000 (yr 1), AED 700,000 (yr 2) and AED 400,000 (yr 3). What is the working capital cash flow at T2?

  1. Outflow AED 40,000
  2. Inflow AED 30,000
  3. Outflow AED 70,000
  4. Inflow AED 110,000
Show answer

B — inflow of AED 30,000. Balances required: T0 = 50,000; T1 = 70,000; T2 = 40,000. The movement at T2 is 70,000 − 40,000 = a reduction of 30,000, hence an inflow. The full 40,000 is then released at T3.

Q6. An investment has an NPV of AED 480,000. The present value of the post-tax contribution is AED 3,200,000 and the initial investment is AED 2,000,000. What is the sensitivity of the NPV to a change in contribution?

  1. 15%
  2. 24%
  3. 6.7%
  4. 10%
Show answer

A — 15%. 480,000 ÷ 3,200,000 = 15%. Option B (24%) uses the initial investment as denominator, which is the sensitivity of the investment, not of contribution.

Q7. If cash inflows and outflows after time 0 all inflate, but the time 0 outflow does not, what happens to the payback period and the IRR?

  1. Both increase
  2. Both decrease
  3. Payback decreases; IRR increases
  4. Payback increases; IRR decreases
Show answer

C. The T0 outflow is unaffected while later inflows grow in money terms, so the investment is recovered sooner (shorter payback) and the return implied by the cash flow pattern is higher (higher IRR).

Q8. A project's cash flows change sign twice. Which statement is correct?

  1. The project has no IRR
  2. The project has exactly one IRR
  3. The project may have up to two IRRs
  4. The NPV will always be negative
Show answer

C. A project can have up to as many IRRs as there are sign changes in its cash flows. This multiple-IRR problem is one of the standard arguments for the superiority of NPV.

Q9. Equipment costing AED 5,000,000 is bought at T0. TAD is 25% reducing balance and tax is payable one year in arrears. When is the FIRST tax saving from TAD received?

  1. T0
  2. T1
  3. T2
  4. T3
Show answer

C — T2. The asset is assumed bought on the first day of an accounting period, so the first TAD is claimed at T1; the one-year delay pushes the cash saving to T2.

Q10. Which of the following would you NOT inflate before including in a money-terms NPV?

  1. Sales revenue given in current prices
  2. Variable costs given in today's terms
  3. Tax-allowable depreciation on an asset bought at T0
  4. Fixed cash overheads given in current prices
Show answer

C. TAD is computed on the actual money cost paid at T0, which is already a money figure and receives no further inflation.

Q11. AED 60,000 is receivable annually in perpetuity, with the first receipt in four years' time. The cost of capital is 12%. What is the present value? (12% DF: yr3 = 0.712, yr4 = 0.636)

  1. AED 500,000
  2. AED 356,000
  3. AED 318,000
  4. AED 560,000
Show answer

B — AED 356,000. PV at T3 = 60,000 ÷ 0.12 = 500,000. Discount back three years: 500,000 × 0.712 = AED 356,000. Option C incorrectly discounts four years.

Q12. Which statement about the real-terms approach to investment appraisal is correct?

  1. It always gives a different NPV from the money approach
  2. It is preferable where different cash flows inflate at different rates
  3. It gives the same NPV as the money approach where all flows inflate at the general rate
  4. It uses money cash flows discounted at the real rate
Show answer

C. The two approaches are mathematically equivalent when a single general inflation rate applies to everything. Where specific rates differ, or where tax or working capital is present, the money method is the practical choice.

One-page recall list

  • (1 + i) = (1 + r)(1 + h), where h is general inflation
  • Money flows ↔ money rate; real flows ↔ real rate. Never mix.
  • Tax has two rows: tax on operating flows (out), tax saved on TAD (in)
  • Total TAD over the life = cost − disposal proceeds
  • WDV > proceeds → balancing allowance (saving); proceeds > WDV → balancing charge (cost)
  • Tax one year in arrears → the table has one extra column
  • Working capital: incremental movements only; released in full at the end; never taxed
  • Sensitivity = NPV ÷ PV of the flow; lower % = more critical
  • Always conclude, always caveat, always sensitise a marginal result
