Time Value of Money & Discounting

1.1 Core Concept

A dollar today is worth more than a dollar tomorrow. Why? Because money can be invested to earn interest (opportunity cost). This is the foundation of all investment appraisal.

Key Formulas

Future Value (FV): FV = PV x (1 + r)^n

Present Value (PV): PV = FV / (1 + r)^n or PV = FV x (1 + r)^-n

Where r = discount rate (cost of capital), n = number of periods

1.2 Discount Factors

A discount factor is simply (1 + r)^-n. Exam tables usually provide these, but know how to calculate them!

Example: Basic Discounting

You expect to receive $10,000 in 4 years. Your cost of capital is 8% p.a. What is the present value?

Solution:

PV = $10,000 x (1.08)^-4 = $10,000 x 0.735 = $7,350

Or using tables: $10,000 x DF(8%, 4y) = $10,000 x 0.735 = $7,350

Exam Tip

Always check whether cash flows occur at the start (Time 0) or end (Time 1 onwards) of each year. Time 0 = NOW. Time 1 = end of Year 1.

Net Present Value (NPV)

2.1 What is NPV?

NPV is the sum of all discounted future cash flows minus the initial investment. It tells you how much value a project adds (or destroys) in today's money.

NPV Formula

NPV = Sum [CFt / (1 + r)^t] - Initial Investment

If NPV > 0 -- Accept (adds shareholder wealth)

If NPV < 0 -- Reject (destroys shareholder wealth)

If NPV = 0 -- Indifferent (returns exactly cost of capital)

2.2 Step-by-Step NPV Calculation

Identify all cash flows -- initial outlay, annual operating cash flows, scrap value, working capital changes, tax flows.
Determine the timing -- when does each cash flow occur? (Time 0, 1, 2, 3...)
Apply the correct discount factor -- use the company's cost of capital (WACC or project-specific).
Calculate PV of each cash flow -- multiply each cash flow by its discount factor.
Sum all PVs and subtract initial investment -- this gives NPV.
Decision: Accept if NPV is positive.

A company is considering a project with the following details:

  • Initial investment: $50,000 (Time 0)

  • Scrap value at end of Year 4: $5,000

  • Considers ALL cash flows over the project's life
  • Accounts for time value of money
  • Uses an objective market-based discount rate
  • Directly measures the increase in shareholder wealth
  • Consistent with the goal of wealth maximization

Internal Rate of Return (IRR)

3.1 What is IRR?

IRR is the discount rate at which NPV = 0. It represents the project's inherent rate of return. If IRR > cost of capital, the project creates value.

IRR Formula (Interpolation)

IRR = L + [NPV_L / (NPV_L - NPV_H)] x (H - L)

Where L = lower rate (gives positive NPV), H = higher rate (gives negative NPV)

3.2 How to Calculate IRR

Calculate NPV at your cost of capital (e.g., 10%).
If NPV is positive, pick a higher rate (e.g., 15%) and recalculate NPV.
If NPV is now negative, you have two rates bracketing zero.
Use the interpolation formula to estimate IRR.
Decision: Accept if IRR > Cost of Capital.
Example: IRR Calculation

Initial investment: $40,000. Annual cash inflow: $12,000 for 5 years. Cost of capital = 10%.

Step 1: NPV at 10%

PV of inflows = $12,000 x 3.791 (Annuity factor, 5y, 10%) = $45,492

NPV = $45,492 - $40,000 = +$5,492 Positive

Step 2: NPV at 15%

PV of inflows = $12,000 x 3.352 (Annuity factor, 5y, 15%) = $40,224

NPV = $40,224 - $40,000 = +$224 Still positive -- try higher!

Step 3: NPV at 16%

PV of inflows = $12,000 x 3.274 = $39,288

NPV = $39,288 - $40,000 = -$712 Negative!

Step 4: Interpolate between 15% and 16%

IRR = 15 + [224 / (224 - (-712))] x (16 - 15)

IRR = 15 + [224 / 936] x 1 = 15 + 0.24 = 15.24%

Decision: IRR (15.24%) > Cost of Capital (10%). Accept the project.

3.3 NPV vs IRR -- Conflicts & Resolution

ScenarioNPV SaysIRR SaysWhich to Follow?
Conventional project, independentAcceptAcceptEither
Mutually exclusive projectsAccept higher NPVMay differNPV wins!
Non-conventional cash flowsOne NPVMultiple IRRsNPV wins!

IRR Problems
  • Multiple IRRs: When cash flows change sign more than once, there can be multiple IRRs. NPV is always reliable.
  • Reinvestment assumption: IRR assumes reinvestment at IRR itself (unrealistic). NPV assumes reinvestment at cost of capital (more realistic).
  • Scale ignored: A small project with 50% IRR may add less value than a large project with 15% IRR.

Payback Period & Discounted Payback

4.1 Payback Period

The time taken for cumulative cash inflows to equal the initial investment. Simple, but ignores time value of money and cash flows after payback.

Formula (for even cash flows)

Payback = Initial Investment / Annual Cash Inflow

Example: Payback Period

Initial investment: $50,000. Cash inflows: Year 1 = $20,000, Year 2 = $25,000, Year 3 = $30,000.

Solution:
YearCash Flow ($)Cumulative ($)
0(50,000)(50,000)
120,000(30,000)
225,000(5,000)
330,00025,000

At end of Year 2, still need $5,000. Year 3 provides $30,000.

Fraction of Year 3 needed = $5,000 / $30,000 = 0.17 years

Payback = 2.17 years (or 2 years 2 months)

4.2 Discounted Payback Period

Same concept, but uses discounted cash flows. More realistic because it accounts for time value of money.

Example: Discounted Payback

Same project as above. Cost of capital = 10%.

Solution:
YearCash Flow ($)DF @ 10%PV ($)Cumulative PV ($)
0(50,000)1.000(50,000)(50,000)
120,0000.90918,180(31,820)
225,0000.82620,650(11,170)
330,0000.75122,53011,360

At end of Year 2, still need $11,170. Year 3 provides $22,530 (discounted).

Fraction = $11,170 / $22,530 = 0.50 years

Discounted Payback = 2.50 years

Note: Discounted payback is ALWAYS longer than simple payback!

Pros & Cons of Payback
Advantages
  • Simple to calculate & understand
  • Useful for liquidity-focused firms
  • Good for risky/uncertain environments
  • Emphasizes earlier cash flows
Disadvantages
  • Ignores time value of money (unless discounted)
  • Ignores cash flows AFTER payback
  • No objective decision rule (what is "acceptable"?)
  • Does not measure profitability

ROCE / Accounting Rate of Return (ARR)

5.1 Definition

ROCE (Return on Capital Employed) or ARR measures profitability using accounting profits, not cash flows. It is a non-discounting method.

ROCE Formula

ROCE = (Average Annual Profit / Average Investment) x 100%

Where:

  • Average Annual Profit = Total profit over project life / Number of years
  • Average Investment = (Initial Investment + Scrap Value) / 2

Alternative: Some exams use Initial Investment as denominator -- READ THE QUESTION!

Example: ROCE Calculation

A machine costs $80,000 and has a 4-year life with no scrap value. Annual profits are:

  • Year 1: $15,000
  • Year 2: $20,000
  • Year 3: $25,000
  • Year 4: $10,000
Solution:

Total profit = $15,000 + $20,000 + $25,000 + $10,000 = $70,000

Average annual profit = $70,000 / 4 = $17,500

Average investment = ($80,000 + $0) / 2 = $40,000

ROCE = ($17,500 / $40,000) x 100 = 43.75%

Decision: Compare against target ROCE (e.g., 20%). 43.75% > 20%, so Accept.

Why ROCE is Inferior to NPV
  • Uses accounting profit (subjective, affected by depreciation policy) not cash flow
  • Ignores time value of money
  • Uses arbitrary target rate (not market-based)
  • Average investment method is mathematically questionable

Annuities & Perpetuities

6.1 Annuities

An annuity is a series of EQUAL cash flows for a FIXED number of periods. Instead of discounting each flow separately, use the annuity factor (cumulative discount factor).

Annuity Formulas

PV of Annuity: PV = C x [(1 - (1+r)^-n) / r]

Annuity Factor: AF = [1 - (1+r)^-n] / r

Then: PV = Cash Flow x AF

Example: PV of an Annuity

What is the PV of receiving $5,000 per year for 6 years at 8%?

Solution:

Annuity Factor (8%, 6y) = [1 - (1.08)^-6] / 0.08 = [1 - 0.630] / 0.08 = 4.623

PV = $5,000 x 4.623 = $23,115

From tables: AF(8%, 6) = 4.623

6.2 Delayed Annuities (Deferred Annuities)

When cash flows do not start immediately. Calculate PV at the period before the first cash flow, then discount back to Time 0.

Example: Delayed Annuity

A project generates $8,000 per year from Year 3 to Year 7 (5 years). Cost of capital = 10%.

Solution:

Step 1: PV at end of Year 2 (one period before first flow):

PV_2 = $8,000 x AF(10%, 5) = $8,000 x 3.791 = $30,328

Step 2: Discount back to Time 0:

PV_0 = $30,328 x DF(10%, 2) = $30,328 x 0.826 = $25,051

6.3 Perpetuities

A perpetuity is an infinite series of equal cash flows. No tables needed -- just one simple formula!

Perpetuity Formula

PV = C / r

Where C = annual cash flow, r = discount rate

Example: Perpetuity

An investment pays $4,000 per year forever. Cost of capital = 8%.

Solution:

PV = $4,000 / 0.08 = $50,000

6.4 Growing Perpetuity

Cash flows grow at a constant rate g forever.

Growing Perpetuity Formula

PV = C_1 / (r - g)

Where C_1 = cash flow in Year 1, g = growth rate (must be < r)

Example: Growing Perpetuity

A project generates $10,000 in Year 1, growing at 3% p.a. forever. Cost of capital = 10%.

Solution:

PV = $10,000 / (0.10 - 0.03) = $10,000 / 0.07 = $142,857

Annuity Factor Shortcut

AF for years 3-7 = AF(0-7) - AF(0-2). This is useful for "annuity in the middle" questions!

Example: AF(10%, years 3-7) = 4.868 - 1.736 = 3.132

Taxation in Investment Appraisal

7.1 Key Principles

  • Tax is a real cash outflow -- include it in NPV!
  • Tax is usually paid one year in arrears (e.g., Year 1 profit -- tax paid in Year 2)
  • Tax reduces operating cash flows
  • Tax-allowable depreciation (TAD) / Capital Allowances provide a tax shield (tax saving)

7.2 Tax on Operating Cash Flows

If a question gives you operating cash flows before tax, calculate tax at the given rate and delay by 1 year.

7.3 Tax-Allowable Depreciation (TAD) / Capital Allowances

TAD is not a cash flow itself, but it reduces taxable profit, creating a tax saving (tax shield).

TAD Tax Shield Formula

Tax Saving = TAD x Tax Rate

This tax saving is a cash inflow (reduction in tax paid).

7.4 Common TAD Methods

MethodDescriptionExam Frequency
Straight-line(Cost - Scrap) / LifeVery Common
Reducing balance (e.g., 25%)Apply % to written down value each yearVery Common
100% first year allowanceFull deduction in Year 1Occasional
Example: NPV with Tax & TAD (Straight-Line)

Machine cost: $100,000. Life: 4 years. Scrap: $20,000. Annual pre-tax cash flow: $40,000. Tax rate: 30% (1 year in arrears). Cost of capital: 10%. TAD on straight-line basis.

Step 1: Calculate TAD & Tax Shield

TAD per year = ($100,000 - $20,000) / 4 = $20,000

Tax saving on TAD = $20,000 x 30% = $6,000 per year

Step 2: Calculate Tax on Operating Cash Flows

Taxable profit = $40,000 - $20,000 = $20,000

  • If given profit after tax, add back non-cash items (depreciation) to get operating cash flow, then calculate tax separately.

Working Capital in NPV

8.1 Key Principles

  • Working capital (WC) investment is a cash outflow at the start and when it increases
  • WC is released at the end of the project (cash inflow)
  • WC is usually expressed as a % of sales/revenue
  • Only the incremental change in WC is a cash flow!
Working Capital Cash Flows

Increase in WC = Cash OUTFLOW (investment)

Decrease in WC = Cash INFLOW (release)

Total WC released at end = Full recovery of all cumulative WC invested

Example: Working Capital in NPV

A 3-year project requires WC equal to 10% of annual sales:

  • Year 1 Sales: $200,000
  • Year 2 Sales: $300,000
  • Year 3 Sales: $250,000

Cost of capital = 10%

Step 1: Calculate WC Required Each Year
YearSales ($)WC Required ($)Change in WC ($)Cash Flow ($)Timing
020,000 (10% of Y1)+20,000(20,000)Time 0
1200,00030,000 (10% of Y2)+10,000(10,000)End Y1
2300,00025,000 (10% of Y3)-5,000+5,000End Y2
3250,0000 (project ends)-25,000+25,000End Y3
Step 2: Check Total

Total WC invested = $20,000 + $10,000 = $30,000

Total WC released = $5,000 + $25,000 = $30,000

These cash flows are then included in the NPV calculation and discounted at 10%.

WC Memory Trick

"WC is like a loan to the project" -- you put money in at the start, and you get it all back when the project finishes. Only the changes between years matter for cash flows!

Inflation & Real vs Nominal Rates

9.1 The Fisher Equation

The relationship between real and nominal (money) rates:

Fisher Equation

(1 + m) = (1 + r) x (1 + i)

Or: m = r + i + (r x i)

Where m = money/nominal rate, r = real rate, i = inflation rate

9.2 Two Approaches to NPV with Inflation

ApproachCash FlowsDiscount RateRule
Nominal (Money) ApproachInflate cash flows to include inflationNominal (money) cost of capitalMost common in exams
Real ApproachKeep cash flows at current prices (real)Real cost of capitalOnly if all cash flows inflate at same rate
Golden Rule

NEVER mix real cash flows with nominal discount rates, or nominal cash flows with real discount rates!

Match nominal with nominal, or real with real.

Example: Real vs Nominal NPV

Real cost of capital = 8%. Inflation = 5%. Annual real cash flow = $10,000 for 3 years.

Method 1: Nominal Approach

Nominal rate = 1.08 x 1.05 - 1 = 13.4%

Method 2: Real Approach

Use real cash flows ($10,000) and real rate (8%):

PV = $10,000 x AF(8%, 3) = $10,000 x 2.577 = $25,770

(Small difference due to rounding -- both valid!)

9.3 Different Inflation Rates

If different cash flows inflate at different rates (e.g., sales at 5%, costs at 3%), you MUST use the nominal approach. Inflate each cash flow separately, then discount at the nominal cost of capital.

Example: Different Inflation Rates

Real cost of capital = 10%. General inflation = 4%.

Sales inflate at 6%, costs inflate at 3%. Current prices:

  • Year 1 Sales: $50,000 | Costs: $30,000
  • Year 2 Sales: $60,000 | Costs: $35,000
Solution:

Nominal rate = 1.10 x 1.04 - 1 = 14.4%

Lease vs Buy Decision

10.1 Types of Leases

TypeDescriptionOwnershipTax Treatment
Finance LeaseEffectively buying asset over time; risks/rewards transferredLessee (eventually)Lessee claims TAD + interest tax relief
Operating LeaseShort-term rental; lessor retains risks/rewardsLessorLessor claims TAD; lease payments are tax-deductible expense for lessee

10.2 Approach: Compare NPV of Costs

Calculate the PV of costs for both options and choose the LOWER cost (or higher NPV if framed as savings).

10.3 Buying Option Cash Flows

  • Initial purchase cost (Time 0 outflow)
  • Scrap value (inflow at end)
  • TAD tax shields (inflows, delayed one year)
  • Any maintenance costs (outflows)

10.4 Leasing Option Cash Flows

  • Lease payments (outflows)
  • Tax relief on lease payments (inflows, delayed one year)
  • No scrap value (lessor keeps it)
  • No TAD (lessor claims it)
Example: Lease vs Buy

A company needs a machine costing $100,000. It can either buy or lease over 4 years.

Buy option: Scrap value $20,000. TAD at 25% reducing balance. Tax 30% (1 year in arrears). Cost of capital 10%.

Lease option: $28,000 payable at START of each year for 4 years. Tax relief on payments at 30% (1 year in arrears).

  • With operating leases, the full payment is tax-deductible.

Asset Replacement Decisions

11.1 The Problem

Should we replace an asset now, or keep it for another year? When should we replace it? We use the Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC) method.

11.2 Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC)

EAC converts the PV of costs over different lifespans into an annual equivalent figure, allowing fair comparison.

EAC Formula

EAC = PV of Costs / Annuity Factor (r, n)

Where n = replacement cycle (e.g., replace every 2 years, 3 years, etc.)

Choose the replacement cycle with the LOWEST EAC.

11.3 Steps for Asset Replacement

Calculate the PV of costs for each possible replacement cycle (e.g., 1 year, 2 years, 3 years).
Include: purchase cost, running costs, scrap value, and any tax effects.
Divide each PV of costs by the relevant annuity factor to get EAC.
Choose the cycle with the lowest EAC.

Capital Rationing

12.1 What is Capital Rationing?

When a company has limited funds available for investment and cannot undertake all positive-NPV projects. We must choose the best combination of projects within the budget.

12.2 Types of Capital Rationing

TypeCauseExample
Hard (External)External market constraints -- cannot raise fundsBank refuses loan; stock market downturn
Soft (Internal)Self-imposed limits by managementBudget cap; risk aversion; no desire for external finance

12.3 Single-Period Capital Rationing

When the budget constraint applies to Year 0 only. Use the Profitability Index (PI) to rank projects.

Profitability Index (PI)

PI = PV of Future Cash Flows / Initial Investment

Or: PI = 1 + (NPV / Initial Investment)

Rank projects by PI (highest first) and select until budget is exhausted.

Example: Single-Period Capital Rationing

Budget available: $100,000. Four divisible projects available:

ProjectInitial Investment ($)NPV ($)PIRanking
A40,00012,0001.302
B30,00015,0001.501
C50,00010,0001.204
D20,0008,0001.403
Solution:

Step 1: Rank by PI: B (1.50), D (1.40), A (1.30), C (1.20)

Step 2: Allocate budget:

  • Project B: $30,000 invested, NPV = $15,000. Remaining: $70,000
  • Project D: $20,000 invested, NPV = $8,000. Remaining: $50,000
  • Project A: $40,000 invested, NPV = $12,000. Remaining: $10,000
  • Project C: Only $10,000 left. Invest 20% of C: NPV = 20% x $10,000 = $2,000

Total NPV = $15,000 + $8,000 + $12,000 + $2,000 = $37,000

Optimal portfolio: B + D + A + 20% of C

12.4 Multi-Period Capital Rationing

When budget constraints apply in multiple years. This requires Linear Programming or trial-and-error combinations. In ACCA FM, you typically need to identify the best combination through systematic trial.

Capital Rationing Key Points
  • Divisible projects: Can invest in fractions. Use PI ranking.
  • Indivisible projects: Must invest 100% or 0%. Use trial combinations and calculate total NPV for feasible sets.
  • With indivisible projects, there may be unused budget (surplus funds) -- this is acceptable.
  • Always check if surplus funds can be invested elsewhere at a known return.

Risk & Uncertainty in Investment Appraisal

13.1 Risk vs Uncertainty

RiskUncertainty
Can be quantified using probabilitiesCannot be quantified -- future is unknown
We can assign probabilities to outcomesNo basis for assigning probabilities
Example: 60% chance of success, 40% chance of failureExample: New technology -- no precedent

13.2 Techniques for Handling Risk & Uncertainty

A. Sensitivity Analysis

Tests how sensitive NPV is to changes in ONE variable at a time.

Sensitivity Formula

Sensitivity % = (NPV / PV of the variable) x 100%

Or for initial investment: Sensitivity = NPV / Initial Investment x 100%

Example: Sensitivity Analysis

A project has NPV = $20,000. Initial investment = $100,000. Annual cash flow = $30,000 for 5 years (PV = $113,730 at 10%).

Solution:

Sensitivity to initial investment:

= $20,000 / $100,000 x 100 = 20%

Investment can increase by 20% before NPV = 0.

Sensitivity to annual cash flow:

= $20,000 / $113,730 x 100 = 17.6%

Annual cash flow can fall by 17.6% before NPV = 0.

Sensitivity to cost of capital:

Requires trial: Find IRR. If IRR = 15.2% and cost of capital = 10%,

Sensitivity = (15.2% - 10%) / 10% x 100 = 52%

B. Probability Analysis / Expected NPV

When different outcomes have assigned probabilities, calculate Expected NPV (ENPV).

Expected Value Formula

Expected Value = Sum (Probability x Outcome)

Example: Expected NPV

A project has the following possible NPVs:

ScenarioProbabilityNPV ($)
Optimistic0.350,000
Most likely0.520,000
Pessimistic0.2(10,000)
Solution:

Expected NPV = (0.3 x $50,000) + (0.5 x $20,000) + (0.2 x -$10,000)

= $15,000 + $10,000 - $2,000 = $23,000

Since ENPV > 0, accept the project.

C. Simulation (Monte Carlo)

Uses computer modeling to run thousands of scenarios with random variations in multiple variables simultaneously. Produces a probability distribution of possible NPVs.

  • More sophisticated than sensitivity (varies multiple variables at once)
  • Requires probability distributions for each variable
  • Output shows range of possible outcomes and their likelihoods

D. Decision Trees

A graphical representation of decisions and possible outcomes. Useful for sequential decisions where choices at one point affect future options.

  • Square nodes = Decision points
  • Circle nodes = Chance points (probabilities)
  • Work backwards from right to left (rollback analysis)

E. Adjusted Payback

Use discounted payback as a risk indicator. Projects with shorter discounted payback are less risky because cash is recovered faster.

14.2 Top 10 Exam Traps to Avoid

Time 0 confusion: Initial investment is at Time 0. First operating cash flow is usually at Time 1 (end of Year 1).
Tax timing: Tax is almost always ONE YEAR IN ARREARS. Do not put it in the same year as the profit!
Balancing allowance/charge: Always calculate the final year WDV vs scrap. If you forget this, your TAD tax shield will be wrong.
Working capital: Only the CHANGE in WC is a cash flow. Total WC is released at the END of the project.
Real vs nominal mixing: Never mix real cash flows with nominal rates. If different inflation rates apply, use the nominal approach.
Annuity timing: Annuity factor AF(n) gives PV one period BEFORE the first cash flow. Adjust for delayed annuities!
Lease vs Buy discount rate: Use after-tax cost of debt, not WACC (unless question says otherwise).
Advance vs arrears: Lease payments in advance = start of period (Time 0, 1, 2...). Arrears = end of period (Time 1, 2, 3...).
NPV vs IRR conflict: When they conflict, ALWAYS follow NPV. NPV measures absolute wealth creation.
Capital rationing: For divisible projects, use PI. For indivisible, try combinations. Do not simply rank by NPV!

14.3 Quick Formula Reference Sheet

Essential Formulas
  • PV: FV / (1+r)^n
  • Annuity Factor: [1 - (1+r)^-n] / r
  • Perpetuity: C / r
  • Growing Perpetuity: C_1 / (r - g)
  • Fisher: (1+m) = (1+r)(1+i)
  • IRR Interpolation: L + [NPV_L / (NPV_L - NPV_H)] x (H-L)
  • EAC: PV of Costs / AF(r,n)
  • PI: PV of Inflows / Initial Investment
  • Sensitivity: NPV / PV of Variable

14.4 Exam Strategy

  • Read the requirement first -- know what you are calculating before reading the scenario.
  • Set up a clear table for NPV calculations. Examiners award marks for method even if arithmetic is slightly off.
  • Show your workings -- do not just write down final numbers.
  • State your decision clearly -- "Accept because NPV is positive."
  • Watch the marks -- a 20-mark NPV question will have 3-4 marks for discussion/decision, not just calculation.
  • Practice timing -- NPV questions can be time-consuming. Leave enough time for written parts.
