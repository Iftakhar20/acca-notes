1. The nature, elements and importance of working capital Syllabus C1(a)–(c) · Study Text Ch 7

Definitions to learn word-for-word

Working capital is the capital available for conducting the day-to-day operations of an organisation — normally the excess of current assets over current liabilities.

Working capital management is the management of all aspects of both current assets and current liabilities, in order to minimise the risk of insolvency while maximising the return on assets.

The four elements Level 1 — knowledge

ElementTypeWhat "managing it" means
InventoryCurrent assetHold enough to avoid stock-outs, not so much that cash is dead on a shelf
ReceivablesCurrent assetGrant credit to win business, but collect it quickly and avoid bad debts
CashCurrent assetHold enough for obligations, invest the surplus
PayablesCurrent liabilityA free source of finance — take the credit, but don't damage supplier relationships

The central conflict: liquidity vs profitability Level 2 — application

This single idea generates more discussion marks than almost anything else in Section C. Understand it properly and you can write three or four marks' worth on demand.

If you hold more working capital…If you hold less working capital…
Liquidity is safe. Bills get paid. No stock-outs. Customers happy with generous credit. Liquidity is at risk. Stock-outs, missed supplier payments, potential insolvency.
Profitability falls. Cash is tied up in inventory and receivables, earning nothing. That capital has a funding cost (overdraft interest) or an opportunity cost (foregone investments). Profitability rises. Less capital employed, lower financing cost, higher return on assets.
Dubai Health illustration

Suppose the pharmacy stores unit is asked to cut its consumables inventory from 60 days to 35 days of cost of sales, releasing AED 8m of cash.

  • Profitability effect: AED 8m no longer needs funding. At an overdraft rate of 6%, that saves AED 480,000 a year. Return on capital employed rises because the denominator has shrunk.
  • Liquidity/operational effect: the buffer against supply disruption is thinner. A single delayed shipment of a critical drug now becomes a clinical incident, not an inconvenience.

The exam point: the "correct" level of working capital is not zero and it is not infinite. It is the level at which the cost of holding capital is balanced against the cost of running out. In healthcare, the cost of running out is unusually high — which is precisely the kind of industry-specific reasoning that scores marks.

Why it's central to financial management

Three arguments to have ready:

  1. Insolvency is a cash event, not a profit event. Profitable companies fail because they run out of cash. Working capital management is the direct defence against that.
  2. It is a large investment. For most trading businesses, working capital is a bigger balance-sheet number than the next capital project. It deserves the same appraisal discipline.
  3. It is fast-acting. A five-day reduction in receivable days releases cash this quarter. A new investment project takes years to pay back.

2. The cash operating cycle and working capital ratios Syllabus C2(a)–(b) · Study Text Ch 7

Definition

The cash operating cycle (also called the working capital cycle or cash conversion cycle) is the length of time between paying cash to suppliers and receiving cash from customers.

The cycle — learn this cold
Inventory days + Receivables days − Payables days = Cash operating cycle

Think of it physically: you buy goods (inventory days start), you pay the supplier at some point (payables days), you sell the goods on credit (receivables days start), and finally the customer pays you. The gap between cash out and cash in is the cycle. The longer the cycle, the more working capital the business must fund.

The component ratios Level 2 — you must be able to calculate and interpret

RatioFormulaWatch out for
Inventory days
(inventory turnover period)		 Inventory ÷ Cost of sales × 365 Denominator is cost of sales, not revenue. If the question only gives revenue, say so and state your assumption.
Receivables days
(average collection period)		 Receivables ÷ Credit sales × 365 Use credit sales if given separately. Cash sales never sit in receivables.
Payables days
(average payment period)		 Payables ÷ Credit purchases × 365 If purchases aren't given, cost of sales is the accepted proxy — state the assumption.
Current ratio Current assets ÷ Current liabilities A crude liquidity measure. A "high is good" reading is naive — see the trap below.
Quick ratio
(acid test)		 (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities Strips out the least liquid current asset. More meaningful for slow-moving inventory.
Sales revenue / net working capital Revenue ÷ (Current assets − Current liabilities) How hard the working capital is working. A very high figure can signal overtrading.
The classic trap

Students write "the current ratio has risen from 1.4 to 2.1, so liquidity has improved." Often it has not. The rise may be caused by inventory piling up because it cannot be sold, or receivables ballooning because customers are not paying. Both are bad news dressed up as a good ratio.

Always interrogate the driver. Which component moved, and why? That is where the marks are — not in the arithmetic.

Worked example — Cash operating cycle

Medco Supplies LLC, a Dubai-based medical consumables distributor, reports the following for the year ended 31 December (AED 000):

Revenue (all on credit)48,000
Cost of sales32,000
Inventory5,600
Trade receivables9,200
Trade payables4,400

Required: calculate the cash operating cycle.

Solution

ComponentWorkingDays
Inventory days5,600 ÷ 32,000 × 36563.9
Receivables days9,200 ÷ 48,000 × 36570.0
Payables days4,400 ÷ 32,000 × 365 (purchases not given — cost of sales used as proxy)(50.2)

Interpretation (this is where the marks are): Medco funds roughly 84 days of operations out of its own pocket. Every AED of annual cost of sales requires about 84/365 = 23% of a year's funding. If receivables days could be pulled from 70 to 55, the receivables balance would fall to 48,000 × 55/365 = AED 7,233k, releasing almost AED 2m of cash immediately.

Exam tip

In Section C, a "calculate the cash operating cycle" requirement is almost always followed by "discuss". Budget your time so that the discussion gets written. The calculation might be 5 marks; the discussion is often 8–10. Students routinely produce a beautiful cycle calculation and then run out of time — that is how you get 11/20 instead of 16/20.

3. Determining the level of working capital investment Syllabus C3(a) · Study Text Ch 7

Working capital investment
Investment in working capital = Inventory + Receivables − Payables

Payables are subtracted because they are a free source of finance. Only the net figure needs funding — from an overdraft, a loan, or equity, all of which carry a cost.

Worked example — Level of investment and its cost

A distributor forecasts annual revenue of AED 60m and cost of sales of AED 40m. Its target policy is: inventory 45 days, receivables 60 days, payables 40 days. Its overdraft rate is 7%.

ElementWorkingAED 000
Inventory40,000 × 45/3654,932
Receivables60,000 × 60/3659,863
Payables40,000 × 40/365(4,384)
Net working capital investment10,411

Annual funding cost = 10,411 × 7% = AED 729k.

Note the direction of the drivers: receivables and inventory are driven by sales and cost of sales respectively. Get the denominator wrong and the whole answer collapses — receivables use revenue, inventory and payables use cost of sales / purchases.

What determines the right level? Level 2

  1. Length of the working capital cycle and terms of trade. A business that must offer 90-day credit to compete simply needs more working capital than one selling for cash.
  2. The organisation's policy on investment in current assets. Aggressive (lean) versus conservative (generous) — see below.
  3. The industry. A supermarket has near-zero receivables and fast inventory; a shipbuilder has enormous work-in-progress. Comparing ratios across industries is meaningless.

Investment policy: aggressive vs conservative

Careful — this is the investment policy, which is about how much current assets you hold. It is a different question from the funding policy (Section 8), which is about how you pay for them. Examiners deliberately test whether you can tell them apart.

Aggressive investment policyConservative investment policy
Level of current assetsLowHigh
InventoryMinimal buffer, JITGenerous buffer stock
ReceivablesTight credit terms, aggressive collectionGenerous credit to attract customers
CashLow balancesLarge precautionary balances
ResultHigher profitability, higher risk of stock-outs and lost customersLower profitability, lower operational risk

4. Managing inventory: EOQ, discounts, EBQ and JIT Syllabus C2(c) · Study Text Ch 8

The three costs of inventory

  • Purchase cost — the price paid. Irrelevant to EOQ unless bulk discounts are offered.
  • Ordering cost (Co) — cost per order: admin, delivery, inspection. Fewer, bigger orders reduce this.
  • Holding cost (Ch) — cost per unit per year: storage, insurance, obsolescence, cost of capital tied up. Fewer, bigger orders increase this.

Ordering cost and holding cost pull in opposite directions. The EOQ is the order size at which their total is minimised.

Economic Order Quantity — provided on the exam formula sheet
EOQ = √( 2 × Co × D ÷ Ch )

where Co = cost per order, D = annual demand in units, Ch = holding cost per unit per year.

Related quantities you will need:

  • Number of orders per year = D ÷ Q
  • Total annual ordering cost = Co × D ÷ Q
  • Total annual holding cost = Ch × Q ÷ 2   (average inventory is Q/2 with no buffer)
  • With buffer inventory b: average inventory = (Q/2) + b, so holding cost = Ch × (Q/2 + b)
Two traps that cost real marks

Trap 1 — units of Ch. The question may give holding cost per month. The formula needs it per year. Multiply by 12.

Trap 2 — buffer inventory does not change the EOQ. Buffer stock is held all year regardless of order size, so it does not affect the trade-off the EOQ is solving. It does add to total holding cost. Many students wrongly try to build it into the square root.

Worked example — EOQ with buffer inventory

A hospital stores unit uses 300,000 units of a surgical consumable each year. The cost of holding one unit for a month is AED 0.25. Each order costs AED 90 to place. A buffer inventory of 25,000 units is held at all times.

Step 1 — annualise the holding cost: Ch = 0.25 × 12 = AED 3.00 per unit per year

Step 2 — EOQ:

EOQ = √( 2 × 90 × 300,000 ÷ 3.00 ) = √18,000,000 = 4,243 units

Step 3 — total annual cost (excluding purchase price):

CostWorkingAED
Ordering90 × 300,000 / 4,2436,364
Holding — cycle stock3.00 × 4,243 / 26,365
Holding — buffer stock3.00 × 25,00075,000
Total87,729

Notice that at the EOQ, ordering cost ≈ cycle holding cost. That is a free check on your arithmetic — if they are wildly different, you have made an error.

EOQ with bulk discounts Level 2 — a common Section C sub-requirement

When a supplier offers a discount for ordering more, the EOQ may no longer be optimal, because the purchase cost now varies with order size. The method is mechanical — follow it exactly:

  1. Calculate the EOQ ignoring discounts. Compute total annual cost = purchase cost + ordering cost + holding cost.
  2. For each discount threshold above the EOQ, compute the total annual cost at the minimum order size that qualifies for that discount.
  3. Compare the total costs. Choose the lowest.
Critical detail

If holding cost is expressed as a percentage of purchase price (e.g. "holding cost is 10% of purchase price per year"), then the discount reduces the holding cost too. Recalculate Ch at each discount level. If holding cost is a fixed AED amount per unit, it does not change.

Worked example — Bulk discount

Annual demand 24,000 units. Ordering cost AED 200 per order. Purchase price AED 20 per unit. Holding cost is 10% of purchase price per year. The supplier offers a 2% discount on orders of 4,000 units or more.

Option 1 — order at the EOQ (no discount)

Ch = 10% × 20 = AED 2.00

EOQ = √( 2 × 200 × 24,000 ÷ 2.00 ) = √4,800,000 = 2,191 units

New price = 20 × 0.98 = AED 19.60. New Ch = 10% × 19.60 = AED 1.96

Non-financial caveat worth a mark: a larger order size means more storage space and, for medical consumables, a greater risk of expiry and obsolescence. Mention it.

Economic Batch Quantity (EBQ) Level 2

Used when inventory is produced internally at a finite rate rather than delivered all at once. Because units are consumed while the batch is still being made, average inventory never reaches the full batch size.

EBQ — provided on the exam formula sheet
EBQ = √[ 2 × Co × D ÷ ( Ch × (1 − D/R) ) ]

where R = annual replenishment (production) rate, D = annual demand. Average inventory = (Q/2) × (1 − D/R).

Note: R must exceed D, otherwise the business cannot meet demand. Co here is the set-up cost per batch.

Just-in-Time (JIT) Level 2 — usually a discussion requirement

JIT aims to hold near-zero inventory, with materials arriving exactly when needed. It is a philosophy, not a formula.

AdvantagesDisadvantages / requirements
  • Holding costs virtually eliminated
  • Cash released from inventory
  • Less obsolescence and waste
  • Quality problems surface immediately rather than being buried in a stockpile
  • Less warehouse space required
  • Zero buffer — any supply disruption halts operations immediately
  • Requires highly reliable suppliers, close to the site, with guaranteed quality
  • Bulk discounts are lost; ordering costs rise (many small deliveries)
  • Vulnerable to demand spikes
  • Needs stable, predictable demand
How to score the discussion marks

Never list JIT's pros and cons generically. Apply them to the scenario. For a healthcare provider, a JIT policy on critical drugs and blood products is close to indefensible — the cost of a stock-out is a patient harm event, not a lost sale. But JIT on office consumables or non-clinical supplies is entirely sensible. Making that distinction within the scenario is the difference between a generic 3 marks and a strong 6.

5. Managing accounts receivable Syllabus C2(d) · Study Text Ch 9

The receivables management framework Level 1

  1. Assess creditworthiness before granting credit — trade references, bank references, published accounts, credit agency reports, analysis of the customer's own ratios, past payment history.
  2. Set credit limits and terms appropriate to the assessed risk.
  3. Monitor — aged receivables analysis, days-sales-outstanding trend.
  4. Collect — invoice promptly and accurately, systematic reminder sequence, escalation, ultimately legal action or debt collection agency.

Early settlement discounts Level 2 — HIGH probability of examination

Offering "2/10 net 30" (2% off if paid in 10 days, otherwise full payment in 30) costs the company money. Is it worth it? There are two ways this is examined — know both, and read the question to see which is wanted.

Method A — the annualised cost of the discount

Annualised cost of a discount
Annual cost = [ 1 + d ÷ (100 − d) ] (365 ÷ n) − 1

where d = the discount percentage, and n = the number of days earlier the customer pays (i.e. normal credit period − discount period).

Compare the result with the company's cost of short-term finance. If the annualised cost exceeds the borrowing rate, the discount is too expensive to offer.

Why d ÷ (100 − d) and not d ÷ 100? Because you are giving up d in order to receive (100 − d) earlier. The cost is measured against what you actually receive.

Worked example — Annualised cost of a discount

A company offers a 2% discount for payment within 10 days, against normal terms of 40 days. Its overdraft rate is 9%. Should it offer the discount?

d = 2, n = 40 − 10 = 30 days

[ 1 + 2/98 ]365/30 − 1 = (1.020408)12.167 − 1 = 1.2793 − 1 = 27.9%

Conclusion: the discount effectively costs the company 27.9% per year, far above its 9% cost of borrowing. Do not offer it — the company would be better off simply funding the receivable on the overdraft.

Worked example — Full cost/benefit of a discount (the harder, more common Section C version)

Al Waha Medical Supplies has annual credit sales of AED 36m and its customers currently take an average of 75 days to pay. It is considering offering a 1% discount for payment within 30 days. It estimates 60% of customers (by value) would take up the discount; the rest continue at 75 days. The overdraft rate is 8%. Assume 360 days. Administration savings of AED 20,000 are expected.

Step 1 — current receivables balance

36,000,000 × 75 / 360 = AED 7,500,000

Step 2 — revised receivables balance

The interest saving and the discount cost cancel exactly. The decision is finely balanced and highly sensitive to the assumed 60% take-up rate. Writing that sentence earns marks; a bare "accept" does not.

Exam tip — always sensitise

Whenever a working capital decision comes out close to breakeven, say so and identify the key driver. Examiners are explicitly looking for evidence that you understand the fragility of the answer, not just the arithmetic.

Worked example — Should we factor?

Credit sales AED 20m; receivables days currently 80. A factor offers a non-recourse service that will reduce receivables days to 50, advance 80% of receivables at 9%, and charge a service fee of 1.5% of credit sales. The company would save AED 90,000 in administration costs and eliminate bad debts currently running at 0.6% of credit sales. The company's overdraft rate is 10%. Assume 365 days.

Step 1 — receivables balances

  • Current: 20,000,000 × 80/365 = AED 4,383,562
  • With factor: 20,000,000 × 50/365 = AED 2,739,726

Step 2 — evaluate annually

Benefit: overdraft interest saved on lower receivables(4,383,562 − 2,739,726) × 10%164,384

Note the interest line carefully. The factor advances 80% at 9%; that 80% would otherwise have been funded on a 10% overdraft. Because the factor's rate is cheaper, this is a small saving, not a cost. If the factor's rate had been higher than the overdraft rate, it would be a cost. Always compare the factor's rate to the company's existing rate — never just charge the full 9% as a cost. This is the most commonly botched line in the whole topic.

Managing foreign accounts receivable Level 2

Foreign receivables carry two extra problems: longer collection periods (distance, different legal systems, slower banking) and foreign exchange risk. Techniques:

  • Letters of credit — the customer's bank guarantees payment. Very secure, but slow and expensive.
  • Bills of exchange — a written order to pay on a specified date; can be discounted for early cash.
  • Documentary collections — documents of title released only against payment or acceptance.
  • Export factoring / forfaiting — sell the receivable to a specialist.
  • Export credit insurance — cover against non-payment.
  • Currency hedging — forward contracts, money market hedges (this links forward to Section G, which you'll cover in Week 6).
  • Invoicing in the home currency — transfers the FX risk to the customer, but may lose the sale.

Relevant to Dubai: because the AED is pegged to the USD, receivables invoiced in USD carry almost no practical FX risk for a UAE entity — but receivables in EUR, GBP or INR very much do. That's an application point worth making if a scenario allows it.

6. Managing accounts payable Syllabus C2(e) · Study Text Ch 9

Using trade credit effectively Level 1

Trade credit is normally the cheapest source of short-term finance available — it is free, provided no discount is being forgone and no penalty is incurred.

Advantages of taking maximum trade creditDangers of stretching it too far
  • Free finance — no interest
  • Readily available, informal, no security needed
  • Improves the cash operating cycle directly
  • Loss of early settlement discounts
  • Suppliers may withdraw credit, demand cash on delivery, or refuse to supply
  • Damaged relationships → worse prices, lower priority in a shortage
  • Damaged credit rating
  • For a public healthcare body: reputational and potentially regulatory consequences of late payment to suppliers

Evaluating an early settlement discount offered to us Level 2

This is the mirror image of the receivables discount. The same formula, but the decision reverses:

The decision rule flips

As the customer (payables): if the annualised benefit of taking the discount exceeds our cost of borrowing → TAKE the discount (borrow on the overdraft if needed and pay early).

As the supplier (receivables): if the annualised cost of the discount exceeds our cost of borrowing → DO NOT OFFER it.

Same number, opposite conclusion. Read the question carefully to establish which side of the transaction you are on.

Worked example — Should we take the discount offered by our supplier?

A supplier offers 1.5% discount for payment within 15 days, against normal terms of 60 days. Our overdraft rate is 10%.

d = 1.5, n = 60 − 15 = 45 days

[ 1 + 1.5/98.5 ]365/45 − 1 = (1.015228)8.111 − 1 = 1.1308 − 1 = 13.1%

Conclusion: taking the discount is equivalent to earning 13.1% per year on the funds. Since we can borrow at 10%, we should borrow on the overdraft and take the discount — a net gain of about 3.1% per year on the amounts involved.

Bulk purchase discounts

Evaluated exactly as in the EOQ-with-discounts method above: compare total annual cost (purchase + ordering + holding) at the EOQ against total annual cost at each discount threshold.

Managing foreign accounts payable

The mirror of foreign receivables: an obligation to pay a foreign currency amount in the future creates a transaction exposure. If the foreign currency strengthens, the AED cost rises. Manage with forward contracts, money market hedges, or by negotiating to be invoiced in AED/USD. Also note the working capital implication: letters of credit tie up banking facilities and may require cash collateral, so a "safer" payment method can be a worse use of working capital.

Why hold cash at all? The three motives Level 1 — easy MCQ marks

Cash budgets / cash flow forecasts Level 2 — very examinable

A cash budget shows expected receipts and payments period by period, producing a running cash balance. It is the single most practical tool in this chapter — and the one closest to your day job.

The golden rule of cash budgets

Include only actual cash movements, in the period the cash actually moves.

Therefore EXCLUDE: depreciation, revaluations, accruals and prepayments adjustments, profit or loss on disposal, bad debt write-offs, and any other non-cash accounting entry.

INCLUDE: share issues, loan receipts and repayments, interest, tax payments, dividends paid, capital expenditure, and asset disposal proceeds.

The most common exam trap: a question mentions depreciation of AED X, and students helpfully deduct it. It is not a cash flow. Never.

Worked example — Cash budget with credit-sales timing

A clinic forecasts revenue of AED 400,000 in January, 500,000 in February and 600,000 in March. All sales are on credit. Customers pay: 50% in the month of sale, 45% in the following month, 5% written off as bad debts. December sales were AED 350,000.

Cash receipts:

Total January receipts357,500
February50% of February sales: 500,000 × 50%250,000

What to do with the answer — the discussion marks

A cash budget requirement is nearly always followed by "discuss how the company should manage the forecast position". Have these ready:

Forecast DEFICIT → arrange an overdraft; accelerate receivables collection (discounts, chasing, factoring); delay non-essential capital expenditure; negotiate longer supplier credit; reduce inventory; consider a short-term loan if the deficit is sustained rather than temporary.

Forecast SURPLUS → is it short-term or long-term? Short-term surplus: invest in low-risk, highly liquid instruments (treasury bills, money market deposits, short-dated certificates of deposit). Long-term surplus: consider paying down debt, increasing dividends, or a new investment project.

The criteria for short-term investment of surplus cash — remember these three: risk (must be low — this is not speculation), liquidity (must be convertible to cash when needed), return (maximise, but only subject to the first two).

Centralised treasury management Level 2

Highly relevant to a large, multi-entity organisation — including a health authority with multiple hospitals and clinics.

Advantages of centralisingDisadvantages
  • Netting off — one unit's surplus offsets another's deficit, so the group holds a smaller total cash balance
  • Larger pooled balances earn better deposit rates and borrow at finer rates
  • Specialist treasury expertise concentrated in one team
  • Group-wide FX exposures can be netted before hedging — cheaper
  • Better control and standardised policy
  • Local units lose autonomy and may become less accountable for cash
  • Local knowledge and relationships with local banks are lost
  • Delays if local units must go to the centre for every decision
  • Cost of running a central function

Cash management models

The Baumol model Level 2

Baumol applies the EOQ logic to cash. It assumes cash is used at a steady, predictable rate, and is replenished by selling securities in fixed-size chunks.

Baumol — optimum amount to transfer each time
F = √[ 2 × C × D ÷ (r1 − r2) ]

where C = transaction cost per transfer, D = annual cash demand, r1 = interest rate on the investment, r2 = interest rate on the current account (usually zero).

Worked example — Baumol

A company needs AED 200,000 of cash per year, drawn evenly. It replenishes its non-interest-bearing current account by selling treasury bills earning 6%. Each sale costs a fixed AED 15.

F = √( 2 × 15 × 200,000 ÷ 0.06 ) = √100,000,000 = AED 10,000
  • Number of transfers per year = 200,000 ÷ 10,000 = 20
  • Annual transaction cost = 20 × 15 = AED 300
  • Average cash balance = 10,000 ÷ 2 = AED 5,000; opportunity cost = 5,000 × 6% = AED 300

Again the two costs are equal at the optimum — your built-in arithmetic check.

Baumol's weaknesses — worth 3–4 discussion marks
  • Assumes cash demand is constant and known with certainty. In reality it is lumpy and uncertain — payroll, quarterly tax, seasonal peaks.
  • Assumes transaction cost and interest rates are constant and known.
  • Ignores buffer cash for unforeseen events.
  • Assumes no cash inflows during the period.

Because of these, Baumol is rarely useful in practice — a business with variable cash flows is better served by Miller-Orr. Saying exactly this, applied to the scenario, is a reliable source of marks.

The Miller-Orr model Level 2

Miller-Orr's advantage over Baumol is that it accommodates uncertainty — cash flows fluctuate randomly. It works by setting control limits.

Miller-Orr — provided on the exam formula sheet
Spread = 3 × [ ( ¾ × transaction cost × variance of cash flows ) ÷ interest rate ]⅓
Return point = Lower limit + ( ⅓ × Spread )
Upper limit = Lower limit + Spread

How it works:

  • Management sets the lower limit (based on its risk appetite and how bad a cash shortfall would be). The model calculates the rest.
  • The cash balance is allowed to wander freely between the limits — no action is taken.
  • If the balance hits the upper limit: buy securities, moving cash down to the return point. Amount = Upper limit − Return point.
  • If the balance hits the lower limit: sell securities, moving cash up to the return point. Amount = Return point − Lower limit.
Three mechanical traps — this is where marks are lost
  1. Variance, not standard deviation. If the question gives a standard deviation of daily cash flows, you must square it.
  2. Everything must be in DAILY terms. If the interest rate is given per year, divide by 365 (or use the daily rate given).
  3. The upper limit is calculated by the model; the lower limit is set by management. Exam MCQs test this the wrong way round constantly.
Worked example — Miller-Orr

A company's daily cash flows have a standard deviation of AED 3,000. Moving cash between the current account and the deposit account costs AED 25 per transaction. The deposit account earns 7.3% per year. Management has set a minimum cash balance of AED 10,000. Assume 365 days.

Step 1 — convert to daily terms

  • Variance = 3,000² = 9,000,000
  • Daily interest rate = 7.3% ÷ 365 = 0.02% = 0.0002

Step 2 — spread

Spread = 3 × [ ( 0.75 × 25 × 9,000,000 ) ÷ 0.0002 ]⅓
= 3 × [ 168,750,000 ÷ 0.0002 ]⅓ = 3 × ( 843,750,000,000 )⅓
= 3 × 9,455 = AED 28,365 (≈ AED 28,000)

Step 3 — the limits

LimitWorkingAED
Lower limitSet by management10,000
Return point10,000 + (⅓ × 28,365)19,455
Upper limit10,000 + 28,36538,365

Action: if the balance reaches AED 38,365, invest (38,365 − 19,455) = AED 18,910 in securities. If it falls to AED 10,000, sell (19,455 − 10,000) = AED 9,455 of securities.

Note the return point sits one-third of the way up, not in the middle. That is deliberate: it is cheaper to correct an excess of cash than a shortage, so the model biases the balance downwards.

8. Working capital funding strategies Syllabus C3(b) · Study Text Ch 10

Section 3 asked how much current assets to hold. This asks: how do we pay for them?

The key distinction — memorise this

Permanent current assets — the minimum level of current assets the business always needs, even at its quietest. A core inventory buffer; a base level of receivables that never goes away because new sales constantly replace collected ones. It looks like a current asset on the balance sheet, but economically it is a long-term investment.

Fluctuating current assets — the extra current assets required at seasonal or cyclical peaks. Genuinely temporary.

The three funding policies Level 2

PolicyWhat it doesRiskReturn
Aggressive Uses short-term finance for all fluctuating current assets and part of the permanent current assets High. Short-term finance (overdraft) is repayable on demand and its interest rate can move against you. Renewal is not guaranteed. High. Short-term finance is normally cheaper, so profits and EPS are higher.
Matching Matches the duration of the finance to the duration of the asset. Permanent current assets → long-term finance. Fluctuating → short-term finance. Moderate — the theoretically "correct" middle ground. Moderate
Conservative Uses long-term finance for permanent current assets and a proportion of the fluctuating ones. Short-term finance is used only for the peaks. Low. Long-term finance is secure and cannot be withdrawn. Low. Long-term finance is more expensive; there will be periods of idle surplus cash earning little.
The one sentence that must be right

Short-term finance is CHEAPER but RISKIER. Long-term finance is more EXPENSIVE but SAFER.

Why cheaper? Lenders demand less compensation for tying money up for a short period, and an overdraft is only charged on the balance actually used.

Why riskier? An overdraft is repayable on demand; the rate is variable; and renewal of short-term facilities is never guaranteed — as many businesses discovered in credit crises.

Get this the wrong way round and every subsequent sentence you write will be wrong. It is the most frequently reversed fact in the entire syllabus.

What actually drives the choice in practice?

  • Management's attitude to risk — risk-averse managers lean conservative.
  • Previous funding decisions — past experience shapes managers' confidence in anticipating working capital swings.
  • Organisation size — small companies often have no choice but to be aggressive: without a track record, marketable shares, or assets to offer as security, long-term finance is simply unavailable to them.
  • Volatility of the sector's earnings — volatile earnings push towards conservative funding, to survive the bad years.
  • Willingness of creditors to lend.

9. Overtrading (under-capitalisation) Frequently examined as a discussion requirement

Definition

Overtrading occurs when a business expands its trading volume rapidly without a corresponding increase in its long-term capital base. It is growing faster than it can fund. The business may be highly profitable on paper and still collapse for want of cash.

The symptoms — learn the list, it's an easy 4–5 marks

  • Rapid growth in revenue, out of proportion to growth in capital employed
  • Sharp rise in the sales revenue / net working capital ratio
  • Inventory days and receivables days rising (stretching to support growth)
  • Payables days rising sharply — the business is leaning on suppliers to fund itself
  • Current and quick ratios falling
  • A rapidly increasing overdraft, often near or at its limit
  • Falling profit margins (discounting to drive volume)
  • Growth financed almost entirely by short-term sources

The remedies

  1. Introduce new long-term capital — a share issue or long-term debt. This is the fundamental fix: the capital base must catch up with the trading volume.
  2. Improve working capital management — cut inventory days, chase receivables harder, use factoring.
  3. Slow the rate of growth — reduce the pace of expansion until funding can catch up. Unpalatable, but effective.
Exam tip

If a Section C scenario gives you two years of financial data and asks you to "discuss the company's working capital position", overtrading is very often the answer they are fishing for. Calculate the ratios for both years, spot the pattern (revenue up sharply, ratios deteriorating, overdraft ballooning), name it explicitly as overtrading, and then give the remedies. That structure — calculate, diagnose, prescribe — is a full-marks answer.

Test yourself

Answer before you scroll. If you get one wrong, go back to the relevant section rather than just reading the explanation.

1. A company has inventory days of 55, receivables days of 68 and payables days of 47. What is its cash operating cycle?

B. 55 + 68 − 47 = 76 days. Inventory and receivables extend the cycle; payables shorten it because suppliers are financing you.

2. Which of the following statements is consistent with an aggressive working capital FUNDING policy?

C. Aggressive funding = lean on cheap, risky short-term finance, even for permanent current assets. A is conservative funding; D is matching; B describes an investment policy, not a funding policy — the classic distractor.

3. In the Miller-Orr model, which statement is TRUE?

A. Management sets the lower limit based on its risk appetite; the model then derives the spread, and hence the upper limit. D is wrong — the return point sits one third of the way up the spread, not halfway.

4. Annual demand is 120,000 units, the cost per order is AED 150, and holding cost is AED 4 per unit per year. What is the EOQ?

D. √(2 × 150 × 120,000 ÷ 4) = √9,000,000 = 3,000 units. Check: ordering cost = 150 × 120,000/3,000 = AED 6,000; holding cost = 4 × 3,000/2 = AED 6,000. Equal — so the answer is right.

5. Which of the following should NOT be included in a cash flow forecast?

B. A revaluation is a purely accounting adjustment — no cash moves. Everything else is a genuine cash flow. Depreciation, profit/loss on disposal, and bad debt write-offs belong in the same excluded category.

6. A supplier offers a 2% discount for payment in 10 days instead of the normal 45 days. Our overdraft rate is 15%. What should we do?

C. [1 + 2/98]365/35 − 1 = (1.020408)10.43 − 1 ≈ 23.4%. We are the customer here, so a high annualised figure is a benefit: borrow at 15% to earn 23.4%. Option A gets the arithmetic right but the direction wrong — which is exactly the trap. Always ask: am I the supplier or the customer?

7. A company's revenue has grown 60% in a year, receivables days have risen from 45 to 72, payables days from 30 to 65, and the overdraft is at its limit. Profits are healthy. What is the most likely diagnosis?

A. Rapid revenue growth, deteriorating working capital ratios, and an overdraft at its limit — while remaining profitable — is the textbook signature of overtrading. The business is expanding faster than its long-term capital base can support. Remedy: inject long-term capital, tighten working capital management, or slow the growth.
