A.1

The Nature & Purpose of Financial Management

📖 Core Definition

Financial management is concerned with the efficient acquisition and deployment of both short- and long-term financial resources, to ensure the objectives of the enterprise are achieved.

Think of it this way — in your role as an FP&A professional at Super Health, you routinely make resource allocation decisions: Which departments get capital? How do we fund this expansion? Do we pay a dividend or reinvest? That, in essence, is financial management.

⚡ The Three Core Financial Management Decisions

Every financial management decision falls into one of three buckets:

📊

Investment Decision

Where do we deploy capital? Long-term (non-current assets) and short-term (working capital). Covered in Chapters D & C.

💰

Financing Decision

How do we raise funds? Equity, debt, short-term or long-term? Covered in Chapter E.

📤

Dividend Decision

How much cash do we return to shareholders vs. retain for reinvestment? Covered in Chapter E.

💡 Real-World Example — Healthcare Context

Investment: Super Health decides to build a new specialist cancer centre (long-term capital investment) AND maintain enough pharmacy stock to treat daily patients (working capital investment).

Financing: Should the centre be funded through government grants, sukuk bonds, or internal reserves?

Dividend: Not applicable for a public healthcare authority, but the equivalent is: reinvest surplus into services or return it to the Ministry of Finance?

⚠️ Important: These Decisions Are Interconnected!

Increasing capital allocated for investment → less cash available for dividends or need to raise more financing. The three decisions do not exist in isolation.

🔗 Relationship with Other Financial Roles

The FM exam distinguishes clearly between three different financial functions:

Role Time Horizon Focus Example Tasks
Financial Management Long-term Raising & allocating capital; achieving financial objectives Dividend policy; capital structure; investment appraisal
Management Accounting Short-term (12 months) Planning, control & internal decision-making Budgets, variance analysis, overhead apportionment
Financial Accounting Historical Reporting past results to external stakeholders Income statement, balance sheet, depreciation
🎯 Exam Tip — Syllabus Point A1(b) [Level 1]

You may be asked to classify a task. Remember: establishing dividend policy = Financial Management. Reviewing overtime spending = Management Accounting. Identifying accruals = Financial Accounting.

A.2

Financial Objectives & the Relationship with Corporate Strategy

🏗️ The Strategy Hierarchy

Objectives define what the organisation wants to achieve. Strategy defines how to get there. They cascade downward through the organisation:

1
Mission / Purpose — The overriding reason the organisation exists (e.g. "To provide world-class healthcare to the people of Dubai")
↓
2
Corporate Goals — Broad aims aligned with mission (e.g. "Expand specialist services")
↓
3
Objectives — Specific, measurable targets (e.g. "Increase ROCE by 3% within 2 years")
↓
4
Strategies — Plans to achieve objectives at corporate, business & operational levels
↓
5
Targets — Departmental-level KPIs (e.g. "Credit control team: reduce debtor days to 30")

🎯 Financial Objectives for Listed Companies

The primary financial objective of a for-profit company is to maximise shareholder wealth. Other common financial objectives include:

Objective What it means Key Limitation
Shareholder Wealth Maximisation ⭐ Maximise share price + dividends over long term (Total Shareholder Return = TSR) Hardest to measure day-to-day but theoretically superior
Profit Maximisation Maximise accounting profit Ignores timing, risk, cash. Can be manipulated. Long-run vs short-run conflicts.
EPS Growth Grow earnings per share = Profit after tax ÷ shares in issue Accounting measure — not the same as shareholder income. Open to same flaws as profit.
💡 Why Profit Maximisation ≠ Shareholder Wealth Maximisation

Tesla example from your Kaplan Study Text: Tesla's share price rose from ~$4.67 (2010) to ~$63.40 (2018) despite recording annual losses throughout this period. The stock market looked beyond current profits and valued long-term potential.

Three reasons profit can mislead:

  • Short vs. long run: Cutting R&D boosts profit this year but destroys future value
  • Quality/risk: High-risk profits are valued less by investors — the market discounts risky earnings
  • Cash vs. accrual: Dividends are paid in cash, not accounting profit. Companies with high profit but poor cash conversion may still struggle.

🔄 Maximising vs. Satisficing

📌 Key Distinction
  • Maximising — Seeking the maximum level of returns even if it means higher risk and management effort (e.g. aggressively expanding market share)
  • Satisficing — Finding an "adequate" outcome; holding returns at a satisfactory level, avoiding risk, reducing workload (e.g. a family-owned firm that is content with steady annual profits)

Management typically tries to maximise returns to shareholders while satisficing for other stakeholders (employees, lenders, etc.).

📐 Strategy Levels

Level Who Decides Example
Corporate Strategy Senior management / Board "Acquire a rival hospital group via share-for-share deal"
Business Strategy Business unit managers "Compete on quality rather than price in private patient services"
Operational Strategy Functional managers "Implement JIT inventory for medical supplies to reduce holding cost"
🎯 Exam Tip — Syllabus A2 [Level 2]

A common question asks you to classify items as financial objective, commercial (non-financial) objective, or strategy. Use this test:

  • Does it have a number and relate to money/returns? → Financial objective
  • Is it about market position, customer satisfaction, or brand? → Commercial objective
  • Is it an action or plan that achieves an objective? → Strategy

Example: "Increase EPS by 5%" = Financial objective. "Acquire a rival" = Corporate strategy. "Improve brand awareness in the UK" = Commercial objective.

A.3

Stakeholders & Impact on Corporate Objectives

📖 Definition

A stakeholder is any individual or group with a vested interest in the organisation. They can be internal, connected, or external.

👥 Stakeholder Groups & Their Objectives

Internal

👔 Managers / Directors

Job security, bonuses, power, empire building, salary maximisation

Internal

👷 Employees

Good wages, job security, safe working conditions, career progression

Connected

📈 Shareholders

Shareholder wealth maximisation: dividend income + capital gains

Connected

🏦 Lenders / Banks

Repayment of principal + interest; long-term cash generation ability

Connected

🛒 Customers

Value for money; quality products/services; reliable supply

Connected

📦 Suppliers

Prompt payment; long-term contracts; regular business

External

🏛️ Government

Tax revenue; employment; compliance with law; export growth

External

🌍 Community

Environmental responsibility; pollution control; social welfare

External

📣 Pressure Groups

Environmental protection; animal rights; ethical supply chains

⚔️ Conflicts Between Stakeholders

Stakeholder Conflict Nature of Conflict
Shareholders vs. Employees Shareholders want lower wages to boost profits; employees want higher pay
Shareholders vs. Managers Managers may pursue empire building, high salaries, or risk-averse strategies that don't maximise shareholder wealth (the Agency Problem)
Shareholders vs. Lenders Shareholders may prefer risky projects (high return if successful); lenders prefer safe, predictable cash flows
Customers vs. Shareholders Customers want low prices / high quality; shareholders want high margins
Government vs. Shareholders Government wants high tax; companies try to minimise tax legally
Customers vs. Community Customers may want cheap goods produced in environmentally harmful ways; community wants environmental protection

🌐 The Stakeholder View

Professor Charles Handy argued that shareholder wealth maximisation cannot be the single overall objective of organisations. Modern companies must balance competing claims of all stakeholders, especially given that some multinationals have revenues exceeding the GDP of small nations.

🎯 Exam Tip — Syllabus A3(a)(b)(c) [Level 2]

In exam questions involving stakeholder conflicts, always:

  • Identify the specific groups and their objectives
  • Explain the nature of the conflict
  • Suggest management's role in resolving/balancing the conflict

The examiner wants analysis, not just a list of stakeholders!

A.4 / A.3(d)

Measuring Achievement of Corporate Objectives

Ratio analysis is the primary tool for measuring corporate performance. The FM syllabus covers four main categories (detailed in Chapter 19 of the Study Text):

📈

Profitability & Return

ROCE, Return on Equity, Net Profit Margin, Gross Profit Margin

⚖️

Debt & Gearing

Debt/Equity ratio, Interest Cover, Debt/Capital ratio

💧

Liquidity

Current Ratio, Quick Ratio, Cash Flow ratios

👥

Investor Ratios

EPS, P/E ratio, Dividend Yield, Total Shareholder Return (TSR)

🏆 Total Shareholder Return (TSR)

TSR is the key measure of shareholder wealth creation, combining both capital gain and dividend income:

Formula — Total Shareholder Return
TSR = (Dividend per share + Capital gain) ÷ Opening share price × 100%
💡 TSR Example

A company's share price rises from $5.00 to $5.60 during the year, and a dividend of $0.30 is paid.

TSR = ($0.30 + $0.60) ÷ $5.00 × 100% = $0.90 ÷ $5.00 = 18%

This means investors received an 18% return on their investment — both from dividends and share price appreciation.

📊 EPS (Earnings Per Share)

Formula — Earnings Per Share
EPS = Profit after tax − Preference dividends ÷ Number of ordinary shares in issue
💡 EPS Example

ABC Corp earns $10m after tax, has preference dividends of $1m, and 30m ordinary shares in issue.

EPS = ($10m − $1m) ÷ 30m = $0.30 per share

If last year's EPS was $0.25, then EPS growth = ($0.30 − $0.25) ÷ $0.25 = 20% growth

🎯 Exam Tip — Syllabus A3(d) [Level 2]

You need to know ROCE, Return on Equity, EPS, Dividend per Share, and TSR. These ratios appear not only in Chapter 1 questions but also in longer scenario questions asking you to evaluate financial performance against objectives.

A.3(c)(e)

Agency Theory & Managerial Reward Schemes

📖 Agency Theory Definition

An agency relationship arises when one party (the principal) employs another (the agent) to perform a task on their behalf.

In a company: Shareholders = Principal  |  Directors/Managers = Agent

💔 The Agency Problem — Divorce of Ownership & Control

Once managers are appointed, they may act in their own interests rather than those of shareholders. This is the core of the agency problem. Historical examples of director self-interest include:

Problem What Happens Real Example
Excessive Remuneration Directors award themselves large bonuses even when company performs poorly UK banks during 2008–09 recession — directors paid bonuses while receiving government bailouts
Empire Building CEOs pursue acquisitions for prestige/power rather than shareholder value 1980s wave of conglomerate takeovers — returns to shareholders often disappointing
Creative Accounting Directors manipulate accounts to flatter profits/share price Enron (2001), WorldCom — collapsed after accounting scandals
Off-Balance-Sheet Finance Moving liabilities into entities not consolidated in accounts Quasi-subsidiaries used by Enron to hide debts
Takeover Defence Directors resist takeovers even when they benefit shareholders, to protect their jobs Companies spending shareholder money on expensive advisors to block bids
Unethical Activities Pursuing activities harmful to society but profitable for the company Trading with sanctioned regimes; environmental pollution

🎁 Managerial Reward Schemes — Aligning Interests

A key solution to the agency problem is to design remuneration packages that align managers' incentives with shareholders' objectives. A good scheme should:

  • Be clearly defined, impossible to manipulate, and easy to monitor
  • Link rewards to changes in shareholder wealth
  • Match managers' time horizons to shareholders' time horizons
  • Encourage managers to adopt similar attitudes to risk as shareholders
Scheme How it Works Advantages Disadvantages
Profit-linked remuneration Bonus paid when profit exceeds minimum threshold Simple to set up and monitor Encourages short-termism; creative accounting; managers "relax" once target hit; may sacrifice long-term investment
EVA-linked remuneration Bonus linked to Economic Value Added (increase in shareholder wealth) More closely aligns with shareholder wealth creation Complex to calculate; managers may not understand it
Revenue growth-linked Bonus tied to revenue/sales growth Drives growth; economies of scale Revenue can grow without profit growing; managers may chase unprofitable revenue
Executive Share Option Plan (ESOP) Managers granted the right to buy shares at a fixed price in the future Directly links reward to share price → long-term focus; aligns manager & shareholder interests Managers sell immediately on exercise; useless if share price below exercise price; dilutes existing shareholders; may incentivise creative accounting to protect share price
💡 ESOP Example

CEO is granted options to buy 500,000 shares at $4.00 each, exercisable in 3 years. If the share price rises to $6.00:

  • CEO buys shares at $4.00 (exercise price) = costs $2,000,000
  • Sells immediately at $6.00 = receives $3,000,000
  • Profit = $1,000,000

This motivates the CEO to grow the share price. However, once the options are exercised, the incentive ends — the CEO may not care about future share price performance.

🎯 Exam Tip — Syllabus A3(c)(e) [Level 2]

Questions on agency theory often ask you to: (1) explain the agency problem in context, (2) evaluate the suitability of a specific reward scheme, or (3) suggest how corporate governance can reduce agency costs. Always relate your answer to the specific scenario given.

A.3(e)

Corporate Governance

Corporate governance codes represent a regulatory approach to reducing the agency problem. Rather than relying on incentives alone, governance codes set rules for how companies should be managed.

📜 Key Corporate Governance Requirements

Area Requirement Why it Matters
Independent NEDs At least half the board (excluding chair) should be independent Non-Executive Directors NEDs provide oversight without being involved in daily management; harder for executives to "capture" the board
NED Restrictions NEDs should NOT hold share options; should not have relationships that compromise independence Prevents NEDs being influenced by personal financial interests
Chairman/CEO Split Chairman and CEO must be different people; CEO should NOT later become Chairman Avoids concentration of power; ensures CEO is accountable to independent Chair
Director Re-election All directors must stand for re-election at least every 3 years Shareholders retain ongoing ability to remove directors who are not serving their interests
Remuneration Disclosure Clear disclosure of total emoluments for all directors Transparency allows shareholders to challenge excessive pay
Short Contract Periods Directors' service contracts should aim for 1 year or less Reduces "golden handshake" payments when poor-performing directors are removed
Senior Independent NED One independent NED designated as champion for shareholders Provides a direct channel for shareholders to raise concerns
📌 Key Background — Why Governance Codes Were Created

The codes emerged after high-profile corporate scandals:

  • Maxwell Communications (1991) — Robert Maxwell stole from pension funds
  • Enron (2001) — Off-balance-sheet fraud; collapsed overnight wiping out thousands of employees' savings
  • WorldCom (2002) — $11bn accounting fraud

Stock exchanges now refer to governance codes in listing requirements — though compliance is typically "comply or explain" rather than legally mandatory.

🎯 Exam Tip

Corporate governance is a Level 2 topic — you need to apply it to scenarios, not just recite requirements. If a question describes a company where the CEO is also Chairman, or where all directors have 5-year contracts, you should identify this as a governance weakness and explain the risks.

A.4

Financial & Other Objectives in Not-for-Profit Organisations

📖 Definition

A Not-for-Profit Organisation (NFP/NPO) does not have profit maximisation or shareholder wealth as its primary objective. Instead, it aims to benefit prescribed groups of people (e.g. patients, students, the public).

Examples: Hospitals, government agencies (like Super Health as a public authority), charities, universities, NGOs, central government departments.

🌟 Why NFP Objectives are Different

Feature Commercial Company Not-for-Profit Organisation
Primary Objective Maximise shareholder wealth Provide service / benefit society
Performance Measure Profit, TSR, EPS, share price Value for Money (VFM), 3 Es, service quality
Stakeholders Relatively clear hierarchy (shareholders primary) Wide range with complex, often conflicting interests
Funding Revenue from customers, equity from shareholders Grants, government funding, donations — often in tranches
Objectives measurability Mostly financial — clear and quantifiable Many are qualitative (e.g. "quality of patient care") — very hard to measure

💰 Value for Money (VFM) — The Core NFP Objective

🌿 Definition

Value for Money = Achieving the desired level and quality of service at the most economical cost.

VFM is assessed using the Three Es framework:

E

Economy

Acquiring resources of appropriate quality and quantity at the lowest cost. Not just cheapest — quality must be fit for purpose.

"Buying medical gloves at the lowest price that still meets safety standards"

E

Efficiency

Maximising output from a given level of input, or minimising input to achieve required output.

"Treating more patients per doctor per day without reducing care quality"

E

Effectiveness

Ensuring outputs actually achieve the predetermined objectives.

"A sports centre built cheaply and efficiently but located far from deprived communities fails on effectiveness"

💡 Super Health Context — The 3 Es in Practice

Imagine Super Health wants to evaluate its outpatient clinic service:

  • Economy: Are medical supplies and staff being acquired at the best possible price without compromising quality standards?
  • Efficiency: How many patients per hour does each doctor see? Is the admin system minimising the time to process appointments?
  • Effectiveness: Is the clinic actually improving patient health outcomes? Are chronic disease patients returning for fewer emergency visits after attending the clinic?

A clinic could score well on Economy (cheap) and Efficiency (fast) but fail on Effectiveness if patients aren't getting better — meaning VFM overall is poor.

🔧 Efficiency: Two Types

Type Definition Example
Input-driven efficiency Maximise output from strictly limited resources (few income sources) A library providing maximum opening hours with a fixed budget
Output-driven efficiency Provide a required/statutory level of service at minimum cost Prison service — must meet statutory obligations; goal is to do so cost-effectively

📏 Measuring Achievement in NFPs

Since profits cannot be used as a performance measure, NFPs typically set quantitative and qualitative targets such as:

  • Total grants/donations to be raised
  • Maximum % of income that fundraising costs can represent
  • Amounts to be spent on specific programmes or projects
  • Maximum permitted administration costs
  • Meeting agreed budgets
  • Breaking even in the long run (income ≥ expenditure)
💡 NFP Performance Measures — Hospital Example

A public hospital might use:

  • Economy: Cost per bed-night; drug procurement cost vs. benchmark
  • Efficiency: Beds occupied per day (occupancy rate); average length of stay; operations per theatre per week
  • Effectiveness: Patient satisfaction scores; re-admission rates within 30 days; infection rates; mortality rates vs. expected

⚡ Factors Making NFP Objectives More Complex

  • Wide range of stakeholders — government funders, service users, staff, regulators, community — all with different priorities
  • High stakeholder involvement — funders and sponsors often have their own conditions attached
  • Little financial input from recipients — patients/students don't pay full cost, removing market price signals
  • Funding in tranches — often project-based or annual grants, not stable commercial revenues
  • Longer planning horizons — healthcare infrastructure takes decades
  • Government influence — objectives may be determined politically rather than purely on merit
🎯 Exam Tip — Syllabus A4(a)(b)(c) [Level 2]

NFP questions are popular in FM! Exam scenarios often describe a public sector body (hospital, council, charity) and ask you to:

  • Explain VFM and how it applies to the scenario
  • Suggest appropriate performance measures (link to 3 Es)
  • Discuss why profit is not an appropriate objective for the organisation
  • Identify stakeholders and their conflicting objectives

Practice Questions — Exam Style

Work through these questions carefully. They are based on real exam question styles for Chapter 1. Click an option to see the answer.

Question 1 — Level 1

Which of the following is NOT one of the three main types of decision facing the financial manager in a company?

  • A Dividend decision
  • B Investment decision
  • C Economic decision
  • D Financing decision
✅ Correct! C — Economic decision. The three core financial management decisions are: Investment, Financing, and Dividend. The "economic decision" does not exist as a financial management category.
❌ Incorrect. The answer is C — Economic decision. The three core financial management decisions are: (1) Investment, (2) Financing, and (3) Dividend. "Economic decision" is not one of them.
Question 2 — Level 2

The agency problem is a driving force behind the growing importance attached to sound corporate governance. In this context, the 'agents' are the:

  • A Customers
  • B Shareholders
  • C Managers
  • D Auditors
✅ Correct! C — Managers. In the principal-agent relationship: Shareholders are the principals (they own the company and set objectives). Managers/Directors are the agents (hired to manage the company on behalf of principals). The agency problem arises because agents may act in their own interests.
❌ Incorrect. The answer is C — Managers. Shareholders = principals; Managers = agents. This is the core of agency theory. Corporate governance codes aim to ensure managers (agents) act in shareholders' (principals') interests.
Question 3 — Level 2

Which of the following is an example of a FINANCIAL objective that a company might choose to pursue?

  • A Provision of good wages and salaries
  • B Dealing honestly and fairly with customers on all occasions
  • C Producing environmentally friendly products
  • D Restricting the level of gearing to below a specified target level
✅ Correct! D — Restricting gearing below a target level. This is a quantifiable financial target relating to capital structure. Options A, B, and C are non-financial (social/ethical/HR) objectives — they cannot be directly expressed as a financial number.
❌ Incorrect. The answer is D. "Restricting gearing to below a target level" is measurable in financial terms and relates to capital structure. Good wages, honest dealings, and environmental products are important but are non-financial objectives (commercial/ethical in nature).
Question 4 — Level 2 (NFP)

In the context of managing performance in 'not-for-profit' organisations, which of the following definitions is INCORRECT?

  • A Value for money means providing a service in a way which is economical, efficient and effective
  • B Economy means doing things cheaply: not spending $2 when the same thing can be bought for $1
  • C Efficiency means doing things quickly: minimising the amount of time that is spent on a given activity
  • D Effectiveness means doing the right things: spending funds so as to achieve the organisation's objectives
✅ Correct! C is INCORRECT. Efficiency does NOT mean "doing things quickly." It means maximising output for a given input (or minimising input for a required output). Speed alone is not the measure — what matters is the ratio of outputs to inputs. A fast but wasteful process is not efficient.
❌ Incorrect. The answer is C. "Doing things quickly" is wrong as a definition of efficiency. Efficiency = maximising useful output for a given level of input (or minimising input to produce a required output). Speed is not the criterion — resource utilisation is.
Question 5 — Level 2 (Agency)

A growing company with cash flow problems currently uses an EPS-based bonus scheme. Which of the following BEST describes a weakness of this scheme in this context?

  • A EPS growth is too difficult to calculate for most managers to understand
  • B EPS rewards accounting profit, which may lead managers to prioritise short-term earnings over investment needed for long-term growth, worsening cash flow
  • C EPS bonuses require regulatory approval from the stock exchange
  • D EPS growth always leads to a reduction in the share price
✅ Correct! B. EPS is an accounting measure (profit-based, not cash-based). In a fast-growing company with cash flow problems, managers incentivised by EPS may cut investment or capital expenditure to boost reported earnings — this makes the cash flow problem worse and damages long-term shareholder wealth. A better scheme might link bonuses to cash flow generation or EVA.
❌ Incorrect. The answer is B. EPS is an accounting measure. In a high-growth company with cash flow pressures, managers chasing EPS bonuses may cut investment or hoard cash in ways that damage future growth. The scheme creates a misalignment between short-term accounting profit (EPS) and long-term shareholder value creation.

Chapter 1 — Key Points Summary

🎯

FM Core Purpose

Efficient acquisition & deployment of financial resources to achieve objectives. Three decisions: Investment, Financing, Dividend.

📈

Primary Objective

Maximise shareholder wealth (TSR = dividend + capital gain). Superior to profit maximisation — includes time, risk, cash.

👥

Stakeholders

Internal (managers, employees), Connected (shareholders, lenders, customers), External (government, community). Conflicts must be managed.

⚖️

Agency Theory

Shareholders (principal) vs. Managers (agent). Solved through: reward schemes (ESOP, EVA-linked) and corporate governance codes.

📏

Measurement

Ratio analysis: Profitability (ROCE), Gearing, Liquidity, Investor (EPS, TSR, P/E). Detailed in Chapter 19.

🏥

Not-for-Profit

Primary goal: benefit society. No profit measure → use VFM / 3 Es: Economy, Efficiency, Effectiveness.
