📘 Working Capital Management

ACCA FM · Study Guide 📅 Sep-26 Exam Window

Master the entire Working Capital syllabus — from the operating cycle to cash management, receivables, inventory, payables, and short‑term financing. 💡 Exam‑focused

1. Nature & Scope of Working Capital

Working capital = the capital available for day‑to‑day operations. It represents the excess of current assets over current liabilities.

Gross WC Total Current Assets
Net WC Current Assets − Current Liabilities
Negative WC Current Liabilities > Current Assets

🔹 Current Assets

  • Inventory (raw materials, WIP, finished goods)
  • Trade receivables (debtors)
  • Cash & bank balances
  • Short‑term investments
  • Prepayments

🔸 Current Liabilities

  • Trade payables (creditors)
  • Bank overdraft
  • Short‑term loans
  • Accrued expenses
  • Tax payable
📌 EXAM TIP

Net working capital is the key metric. A positive NWC indicates liquidity buffer; negative NWC is common in supermarkets (cash sales & slow payables) but can signal distress in other sectors.

2. The Operating (Cash) Cycle

Operating cycle = the time from placing an order for raw materials to collecting cash from customers.

Inventory days + Receivable days − Payable days

Cash conversion cycle (CCC) = operating cycle − payables deferral period.

📝 EXAMPLE 1

Alpha Ltd has:

  • Inventory turnover: 60 days
  • Receivable collection: 45 days
  • Payable payment: 30 days

Operating cycle = 60 + 45 = 105 days
Cash cycle = 105 − 30 = 75 days

💰 The firm needs to finance 75 days of operations before cash comes in.

📌 EXAM TIP

A shorter cash cycle is usually better — it reduces the need for external financing. Exam questions often ask you to calculate the cycle from financial statements and interpret the result.

3. Working Capital Investment & Financing Policies

3.1 Investment Policies

🟢 Conservative

  • High levels of current assets
  • Low risk, low return
  • Large buffer against uncertainty
  • Higher holding costs

🔴 Aggressive

  • Low levels of current assets
  • High risk, high return
  • Vulnerable to stock-outs / bad debts
  • Lower holding costs

⚪ Moderate (Matching)

  • Balance between risk and return
  • Match asset maturity with funding
  • Most common in practice

3.2 Financing Policies

🟢 Conservative

  • Use long‑term finance for all assets
  • Low liquidity risk
  • Higher cost of capital

🔴 Aggressive

  • Use short‑term finance for some non‑current assets
  • Higher liquidity risk
  • Lower cost of capital
📌 EXAM TIP

In the exam, you may be asked to identify a firm's policy from data (e.g., current ratio, quick ratio) and discuss the risk‑return trade‑off. Always link to the firm's industry and business model.

4. Inventory Management

Key models: EOQ (Economic Order Quantity), JIT (Just‑in‑Time), ABC analysis, reorder levels, buffer (safety) stock.

📦 EOQ — Economic Order Quantity

EOQ = √( 2 × D × Cₒ / Cₕ )
  • D = annual demand (units)
  • Cₒ = cost per order (ordering cost)
  • Cₕ = holding cost per unit per year
📝 EXAMPLE 2 — EOQ

Beta Ltd uses 24,000 units of a component per year. Ordering cost = $150 per order. Holding cost = $4 per unit per year.

EOQ = √( 2 × 24,000 × 150 / 4 ) = √( 7,200,000 / 4 ) = √1,800,000 = 1,342 units

📌 Number of orders = 24,000 / 1,342 ≈ 18 orders per year.
Total cost = (18 × $150) + (1,342/2 × $4) = $2,700 + $2,684 = $5,384

🛡️ Buffer (Safety) Stock

Buffer stock = extra inventory held to protect against demand variability or supply delays.

Reorder level = (max usage × max lead time) + buffer
Min inventory = buffer stock
Max inventory = reorder level + EOQ − (min usage × min lead time)
📝 EXAMPLE 3 — Buffer Stock

Gamma Ltd: Usage 200 units/day (normal), max 250/day. Lead time 5 days (normal), max 8 days. EOQ = 1,200 units.

Buffer stock = (250 × 8) − (200 × 5) = 2,000 − 1,000 = 1,000 units
Reorder level = (250 × 8) = 2,000 units

📌 EXAM TIP

EOQ questions are highly examinable. Be ready to calculate EOQ, number of orders, total cost, and discuss the assumptions (constant demand, no quantity discounts, etc.). Also know JIT — its benefits (lower holding costs) and risks (supply disruptions).

5. Receivables (Debtors) Management

Goal: Optimise the trade‑off between profit (from additional sales) and risk (bad debts, delayed cash).

Key tools: Credit policy, ageing analysis, factoring, invoice discounting, early settlement discounts.

📊 Credit Policy Decisions

When a firm relaxes credit policy (longer terms, looser checks):

  • ✅ Higher sales revenue
  • ✅ Higher contribution / profit
  • ❌ Higher investment in receivables (financing cost)
  • ❌ Higher bad debts
  • ❌ Higher administrative costs
📝 EXAMPLE 4 — Credit Policy Change

Delta Ltd is considering extending credit terms from 30 to 60 days.

  • Annual sales: $10m (all credit)
  • Contribution margin: 30%
  • Current receivables: 30 days → $10m × 30/365 = $0.822m
  • New receivables: 60 days → $10m × 60/365 = $1.644m
  • Extra investment: $0.822m
  • Cost of capital: 10% → extra financing cost = $0.082m

Decision: If the increase in contribution from extra sales > the financing cost + bad debts, relax; otherwise tighten.

🏦 Factoring & Invoice Discounting

Factoring

  • Sell receivables to a factor
  • Factor provides finance (80‑90%)
  • Factor also does credit control & bad debt protection
  • Higher cost but reduces admin

Invoice Discounting

  • Borrow against receivables (collateral)
  • No sale of receivables
  • Company retains credit control
  • Lower cost than factoring
  • No bad debt protection
📌 EXAM TIP

Questions on credit policy often require a cost‑benefit analysis. Always calculate the incremental profit vs incremental cost. Also remember the early settlement discount formula — compare the cost of discount with the cost of borrowing.

6. Payables (Creditors) Management

Trade credit is a free (or cheap) source of finance if discounts are not taken. But delaying payment can damage supplier relationships and credit rating.

📝 EXAMPLE 5 — Cost of Trade Credit

Epsilon Ltd is offered 2/10, net 30 (2% discount if paid within 10 days, otherwise full payment in 30 days).

Cost of not taking discount = (2 / (100 − 2)) × (365 / (30 − 10))
= (2/98) × (365/20) = 0.0204 × 18.25 = 37.2% per year

⚠️ If the firm can borrow at < 37.2%, it should take the discount and pay early.

📌 EXAM TIP

The cost of trade credit formula is a favourite in Section C questions. Also be ready to discuss the benefits of stretching payables (free finance) vs risks (supplier penalties, loss of goodwill).

7. Cash Management

Cash is the most liquid asset. The goal is to optimise cash balances — not too much (idle funds) and not too little (risk of insolvency).

📋 Cash Budget

A cash budget forecasts cash inflows and outflows over a period. It highlights surpluses (to invest) and deficits (to finance).

Key components: Opening balance, receipts (sales, dividends), payments (suppliers, wages, capex), net cash flow, closing balance.

📝 EXAMPLE 6 — Cash Budget (extract)
MonthReceipts ($k)Payments ($k)Net ($k)Closing ($k)
Jan120100+2045
Feb110130−2025
Mar140115+2550

Opening balance in Jan = $25k. The firm has a deficit in Feb — could use an overdraft.

📈 Baumol Model (Cash EOQ)

Optimum cash balance = √( 2 × D × Cₒ / i )
  • D = annual cash outflow (disbursements)
  • Cₒ = cost per conversion (selling investments/borrowing)
  • i = opportunity cost of holding cash (interest rate)
📝 EXAMPLE 7 — Baumol

Zeta Ltd has annual cash outflows of $500,000. Cost per conversion = $100. Interest rate = 8%.

Optimum cash = √( 2 × 500,000 × 100 / 0.08 ) = √( 100,000,000 / 0.08 ) = √1,250,000,000 = $35,355

Number of conversions = 500,000 / 35,355 ≈ 14 times per year.

📊 Miller‑Orr Model

For cash flows that are uncertain (random daily fluctuations).

  • Upper limit (U) = return point + 3 × spread
  • Return point (R) = lower limit + spread
  • Spread = ( 3 × transaction cost × variance / (4 × interest) )1/3

When cash hits the upper limit, buy securities to bring back to R. When it hits the lower limit, sell securities to bring back to R.

📌 EXAM TIP

You are not required to memorise the Miller‑Orr formula in full (it's in the exam formula sheet). But you must understand the logic and be able to interpret the results. The Baumol model is simpler and more frequently tested in Section A/B.

8. Short‑Term Financing

🏦 Bank Overdraft

  • Flexible, variable interest
  • Can be called in at short notice
  • Cheap for short‑term needs

📄 Commercial Paper

  • Unsecured short‑term debt
  • Issued by large, creditworthy firms
  • Maturity: 1‑365 days
  • Lower cost than bank borrowing

📦 Trade Credit

  • Suppliers provide goods on credit
  • "Free" finance if discount not taken
  • Can be expensive if discount is forgone

📈 Short‑Term Loans

  • Fixed term (e.g., 1‑3 years)
  • Fixed or variable interest
  • More certain than overdraft
📌 EXAM TIP

Be able to compare sources of short‑term finance on cost, flexibility, availability, and risk. Overdrafts are flexible but revocable; commercial paper is cheap but only for large firms.

9. Overtrading

Overtrading = growing too quickly without sufficient long‑term capital. The firm is under‑capitalised.

Signs:

  • 📈 Rapid sales growth (especially on credit)
  • 📉 Declining current ratio / quick ratio
  • 📈 Increasing receivables & inventory
  • 📈 Increasing payables (stretching suppliers)
  • 📉 Declining cash balance / high overdraft

Solutions: Inject equity, reduce dividends, improve collection, sell assets, or slow growth.

📝 EXAMPLE 8 — Overtrading

Theta Ltd has sales growing at 40% p.a. but:

  • Current ratio: 1.1 (down from 1.8 last year)
  • Receivable days: 75 (up from 45)
  • Inventory days: 90 (up from 60)
  • Overdraft: $2.5m (up from $0.2m)

Conclusion: Theta is overtrading. It needs to either slow growth or raise long‑term finance.

10. Treasury & Other Key Areas

Treasury management = the efficient management of an organisation's liquidity, funding, and financial risk.

Key functions: Cash management, funding (short/long‑term), risk management (interest rate, foreign exchange), and banking relationships.
