1️⃣ Time Value of Money (TVM)

Core concept: £1 today is worth more than £1 in the future because money can be invested to earn interest. This is the foundation of all investment appraisal techniques.

1.1 Simple Interest

Interest is earned only on the original principal each year.

FV = PV × (1 + r × n)

Where: FV = Future Value, PV = Present Value, r = interest rate (decimal), n = number of years

Example 1

Simple Interest

You deposit £1,000 at 5% simple interest for 4 years. What is the future value?

Solution: FV = £1,000 × (1 + 0.05 × 4) = £1,000 × 1.20 = £1,200

Total interest earned = £200 (same £50 each year × 4)

1.2 Compound Interest

Interest is earned on both the principal AND accumulated interest from previous periods. This is the basis of discounted cash flow (DCF) analysis.

FV = PV × (1 + r)n
Example 2

Compound Interest

You deposit £1,000 at 5% compound interest for 4 years. What is the future value?

Solution: FV = £1,000 × (1.05)4 = £1,000 × 1.2155 = £1,215.51

Compare: Compound (£1,215.51) > Simple (£1,200) — the "magic" of compounding!

Exam Focus: FM exam questions use compound interest unless explicitly told otherwise. All DCF techniques (NPV, IRR) are built on compounding/discounting.

2️⃣ Discount Factors & Present Value

Discounting is the reverse of compounding — we convert future cash flows back to their present-day equivalent.

PV = FV × (1 + r)−n  =  FV / (1 + r)n

The Discount Factor (DF) at rate r for year n is: DF = (1 + r)−n

These are provided in the ACCA exam Present Value Table — you do NOT need to calculate (1+r)−n manually; just look up the table!

Example 3

Discounting a Single Cash Flow

A project will generate £50,000 in 5 years. The discount rate is 10%. What is its present value?

Solution: DF(10%, 5yr) = (1.10)−5 = 0.621 (from PV table)

PV = £50,000 × 0.621 = £31,050

Interpretation: £50,000 in 5 years is "worth" £31,050 today at a 10% discount rate.

Tip: In the ACCA exam, you will be given discount factor tables for various rates (usually 1% to 20%+). Always use the table provided rather than calculating manually — it saves time and avoids rounding errors.

3️⃣ Annuities

An annuity is a series of equal cash flows occurring at regular intervals for a fixed number of periods.

3.1 Ordinary Annuity (Annuity in Arrears)

Cash flows occur at the END of each period. This is the default assumption in ACCA FM unless stated otherwise.

PV of Annuity = Annual Cash Flow × AF(r, n)
where AF(r, n) = [1 − (1 + r)−n] / r

The Annuity Factor (AF) is also provided in the ACCA exam's Annuity Table.

Example 4

Ordinary Annuity PV

A project generates £20,000 per year for 6 years (end of each year). Discount rate = 8%. Find the PV.

Solution: AF(8%, 6yr) = 4.623 (from annuity table)

PV = £20,000 × 4.623 = £92,460

3.2 Annuity Due (Annuity in Advance)

Cash flows occur at the START of each period. Multiply the ordinary annuity factor by (1 + r).

PV of Annuity Due = Annual CF × AF(r, n) × (1 + r)
Example 5

Annuity in Advance

Same as Example 4, but cash flows occur at the start of each year.

Solution: PV = £20,000 × 4.623 × 1.08 = £99,856.80

(Higher PV because each cash flow is received one year earlier.)

3.3 Delayed Annuity

When the annuity starts after a delay (e.g., first cash flow in year 4, running for 5 years).

Method: Calculate the annuity PV as at the start of the annuity period, then discount that lump sum back to time 0.

Example 6

Delayed Annuity

A project generates £15,000 per year for 5 years, starting at the end of year 3 (i.e., years 3–7). Discount rate = 10%.

Step 1: PV at start of annuity (t2) = £15,000 × AF(10%, 5yr) = £15,000 × 3.791 = £56,865

Step 2: Discount back to t0: PV = £56,865 × DF(10%, 2yr) = £56,865 × 0.826 = £46,970.49

Common Mistake: Students often discount the delayed annuity back by the wrong number of years. Always draw a timeline! If the annuity starts at year 3 (end), the "PV of the annuity" sits at year 2 — so discount by 2 years, not 3.

4️⃣ Perpetuities

A perpetuity is an annuity that continues forever — equal cash flows indefinitely.

PV of Perpetuity = Annual Cash Flow / r

This assumes the first cash flow occurs at the end of year 1.

Example 7

Simple Perpetuity

A scholarship fund pays £5,000 per year indefinitely, starting at the end of year 1. Discount rate = 4%. What is the fund's value today?

Solution: PV = £5,000 / 0.04 = £125,000

4.1 Delayed Perpetuity

When the perpetuity starts after a delay. Calculate PV as at the start of the perpetuity, then discount back.

Example 8

Delayed Perpetuity

A project generates £8,000 per year indefinitely, starting at the end of year 5. Discount rate = 10%. Find PV.

Step 1: PV at start of perpetuity (t4) = £8,000 / 0.10 = £80,000

Step 2: Discount back to t0: PV = £80,000 × DF(10%, 4yr) = £80,000 × 0.683 = £54,640

4.2 Growing Perpetuity (Gordon Growth Model)

When cash flows grow at a constant rate g indefinitely.

PV = CF1 / (r − g)    (where g < r)
Example 9

Growing Perpetuity

A dividend of £2.00 is expected at the end of year 1, growing at 3% per year indefinitely. Required return = 8%. Find PV.

Solution: PV = £2.00 / (0.08 − 0.03) = £2.00 / 0.05 = £40.00

ACCA FM Note: The growing perpetuity formula appears in the context of dividend valuation and sometimes in project appraisal where terminal values are estimated. Always check that g < r — otherwise the formula breaks down.

5️⃣ Net Present Value (NPV) ⭐

NPV is the most important investment appraisal technique in ACCA FM. It calculates the total present value of all cash flows (positive and negative) associated with a project.

NPV = Σ [CFt × (1 + r)−t] − Initial Investment
Or simply: NPV = PV of all future cash flows − Initial Outlay

Decision Rule:

✅ NPV > 0 → ACCEPT (project adds value)

❌ NPV < 0 → REJECT (project destroys value)

If NPV = 0, the project earns exactly the required return — indifferent.

The cost of capital is 10%. Calculate NPV and advise.

Solution:

Decision: NPV = +£13,400 > 0 → ✅ ACCEPT the project. It adds £13,400 of shareholder value.

6️⃣ Internal Rate of Return (IRR) ⭐

The IRR is the discount rate that makes the NPV exactly equal to zero. It represents the project's "break-even" rate of return.

Decision Rule:

✅ IRR > Cost of Capital → ACCEPT

❌ IRR < Cost of Capital → REJECT

6.1 Calculating IRR by Linear Interpolation

Since IRR cannot usually be solved directly, we use linear interpolation with two NPVs at different rates:

IRR ≈ L + [NPVL / (NPVL − NPVH)] × (H − L)

Where: L = lower discount rate, H = higher discount rate, NPVL = NPV at rate L (positive), NPVH = NPV at rate H (negative)

Example 11

IRR by Interpolation

Using the project from Example 10 (initial outlay £200,000):

At 10% → NPV = +£13,400 (positive)

At 15% → NPV = −£8,200 (negative) [calculated separately]

Solution:

IRR ≈ 10% + [13,400 / (13,400 − (−8,200))] × (15% − 10%)

IRR ≈ 10% + [13,400 / 21,600] × 5%

IRR ≈ 10% + 0.6204 × 5% = 10% + 3.10% = 13.10%

If the cost of capital is 10%, then IRR (13.10%) > 10% → ✅ ACCEPT

Accuracy Check: For linear interpolation to be reasonably accurate, the two rates (L and H) should be no more than 5% apart. The wider the gap, the less accurate the estimate — because the NPV curve is not a straight line.

Advantages & Disadvantages of IRR

Advantages ✅Disadvantages ❌
Gives a percentage return — easy to understandCan give multiple IRRs if cash flows change sign more than once
Considers the time value of moneyAssumes reinvestment at the IRR (which may be unrealistic)
Considers all cash flowsCannot rank projects reliably when project sizes differ
No need to specify an exact discount rate upfrontLess reliable than NPV for mutually exclusive projects

7️⃣ Payback Period

The payback period is the time it takes for a project's cash inflows to recover the initial investment.

7.1 Simple Payback

Example 12

Simple Payback

Initial investment: £200,000. Cash inflows: Year 1: £50k, Year 2: £60k, Year 3: £70k, Year 4: £60k, Year 5: £40k.

YearCash Flow (£)Cumulative (£)
0(200,000)(200,000)
150,000(150,000)
260,000(90,000)
370,000(20,000)
460,00040,000

Payback occurs during Year 4. Fraction = 20,000 / 60,000 = 0.33 years.

Payback = 3.33 years (3 years and 4 months approx.)

7.2 Discounted Payback

Same method but using discounted cash flows (PV of each cash flow). This accounts for the time value of money.

Exam Note: Payback is a supplementary tool, not a primary decision-making method in FM. It's often used alongside NPV/IRR to assess liquidity risk — how quickly cash is recovered.
Advantages ✅Disadvantages ❌
Simple and easy to understandIgnores time value of money (simple payback)
Useful for liquidity assessmentIgnores all cash flows after payback
Good for risky projects where quick recovery is desiredNo clear "accept/reject" threshold

8️⃣ Accounting Rate of Return (ARR / ROCE)

ARR is based on accounting profit (not cash flow) and is expressed as a percentage.

ARR = (Average Annual Profit / Average Investment) × 100%

Where:

  • Average Annual Profit = (Total profit over life − Total depreciation) ÷ Number of years
  • Average Investment = (Initial Investment + Scrap Value) ÷ 2  (commonly)
Example 13

ARR Calculation

Initial investment: £200,000, scrap value: £20,000, project life: 5 years. Total undiscounted cash inflows: £280,000. Total depreciation = £200,000 − £20,000 = £180,000.

Total profit = £280,000 − £180,000 = £100,000

Average annual profit = £100,000 / 5 = £20,000

Average investment = (£200,000 + £20,000) / 2 = £110,000

ARR = (£20,000 / £110,000) × 100% = 18.18%

Compare with a target ARR (e.g., 15%) → Accept if ARR > target.

Key Limitation: ARR uses profit, not cash flow, and ignores the time value of money. It's rarely used as a primary method but may appear in exam questions. Always be aware of its weaknesses.

9️⃣ Relevant Cash Flows for DCF

Only incremental, future cash flows that arise as a direct consequence of the project should be included in NPV analysis.

9.1 Rules for Relevant Cash Flows

ItemInclude? ✅/❌Reason
Sunk Costs (already incurred, e.g., R&D spent last year)❌ EXCLUDENot incremental — already spent regardless of decision
Opportunity Costs (e.g., using owned land)✅ INCLUDERepresents a genuine economic sacrifice
Incremental Revenues & Costs✅ INCLUDEDirect consequence of the project
Working Capital (inventory, receivables, payables)✅ INCLUDEInitial outflow, recovered at end of project
Allocated Overheads❌ EXCLUDENot incremental unless specifically caused by the project
Financing Costs (interest, dividends)❌ EXCLUDEAlready captured in the discount rate (cost of capital)
Taxation✅ INCLUDEReal cash outflow; impacts project viability
Residual / Scrap Value✅ INCLUDEFuture cash inflow at end of project life
Cannibalisation (lost sales of existing products)✅ INCLUDENegative incremental effect
Example 14

Identifying Relevant Cash Flows

A company considers launching a new product. Which of these are relevant?

  • Market research already conducted: £30,000 → ❌ Sunk cost
  • Use of vacant factory (could rent for £15,000/yr) → ✅ Opportunity cost = £15,000/yr
  • Expected sales: £200,000/yr → ✅ Incremental revenue
  • Apportioned head office costs: £10,000/yr → ❌ Not incremental
  • Interest on loan to fund project: £8,000/yr → ❌ Excluded from cash flows (in discount rate)
Golden Rule: If a cash flow would occur regardless of whether the project goes ahead, it is NOT relevant. Only include cash flows that change because of the decision.

🔟 Taxation in Investment Appraisal

Tax is a real cash outflow and must be included in NPV calculations. In ACCA FM, tax is typically assumed to be paid one year in arrears (unless told otherwise).

10.1 Tax on Operating Cash Flows

Tax is calculated on taxable operating profits (revenues minus operating costs minus tax-allowable depreciation / capital allowances).

Example 15

Tax on Operating Profits

A project generates net operating cash inflows of £100,000 per year for 4 years. Tax rate = 25%, paid one year in arrears. Capital allowances (tax depreciation) = £30,000 per year.

Taxable profit = £100,000 − £30,000 = £70,000

Tax payable = £70,000 × 25% = £17,500 per year (paid in the following year)

So Year 1's tax of £17,500 is paid in Year 2; Year 2's tax in Year 3; etc. Year 4's tax is paid in Year 5.

10.2 Writing Down Allowances (WDAs) / Capital Allowances

In the UK tax system (relevant to ACCA FM), capital allowances are given on plant & machinery, often as reducing balance (e.g., 25% WDA). The final year includes a balancing allowance or charge.

Example 16

Writing Down Allowances

Asset cost: £200,000, WDA rate: 25% reducing balance, sold after 4 years for £50,000, tax rate 25%, tax paid one year in arrears.

YearWritten Down Value (WDV) b/fWDA @ 25%Tax Saving @ 25%Timing of Tax Saving
1£200,000£50,000£12,500Year 2
2£150,000£37,500£9,375Year 3
3£112,500£28,125£7,031Year 4
4£84,375Sale proceeds £50,000 → Balancing Allowance = £84,375 − £50,000 = £34,375£8,594Year 5

Note: Tax savings from WDAs are cash inflows (reducing tax paid) and are included in the NPV calculation.

1️⃣1️⃣ Inflation: Real vs Nominal Approach

Inflation affects both cash flows and discount rates. There are two equivalent methods — both give the same NPV if applied consistently.

ApproachCash FlowsDiscount Rate
Real ApproachStated in today's prices (real terms, no inflation)Real cost of capital (rreal)
Nominal (Money) ApproachInclude inflation (nominal terms)Nominal (money) cost of capital (rnom)
(1 + rnom) = (1 + rreal) × (1 + i)   — Fisher Equation

Where i = general inflation rate.

Example 17

Real vs Nominal

Real cost of capital = 8%, inflation = 3%. A project generates £50,000 real cash flow per year for 3 years.

Nominal discount rate: (1.08)(1.03) − 1 = 0.1124 = 11.24%

Nominal cash flows: Y1: £50,000 × 1.03 = £51,500; Y2: £50,000 × 1.03² = £53,045; Y3: £50,000 × 1.03³ = £54,636

Real approach PV: £50,000 × AF(8%, 3yr) = £50,000 × 2.577 = £128,850

Nominal approach PV: £51,500/1.1124 + £53,045/1.1124² + £54,636/1.1124³ = £128,850 ✅ Same result!

Exam Tip: The nominal approach is more common in ACCA FM questions, especially when specific inflation rates are given for different cost/revenue items. Always clearly label which approach you're using.

1️⃣2️⃣ Capital Rationing

Capital rationing occurs when a company has limited funds and cannot invest in all positive-NPV projects.

  • Hard Capital Rationing: External constraints — cannot raise funds from capital markets at any reasonable cost.
  • Soft Capital Rationing: Internal constraints — management-imposed limits on investment spending.

12.1 Single-Period Capital Rationing — Profitability Index (PI)

When funds are limited in one period only and projects are divisible (can be partially undertaken).

Profitability Index (PI) = NPV / Initial Investment
(or sometimes: PV of Future Cash Flows / Initial Investment)

Rank projects by PI descending and allocate funds until exhausted.

Example 18

Divisible Projects — Profitability Index

Available funds: £500,000. Cost of capital: 10%.

ProjectInvestment (£)NPV (£)PI (NPV/Inv)Rank
A300,00045,0000.1502
B200,00036,0000.1801
C250,00030,0000.1203
D150,00018,0000.1204

Allocation: B first (£200k, NPV £36k) → Remaining £300k → A next (£300k, NPV £45k) → Funds exhausted.

Total NPV = £81,000

12.2 Non-Divisible Projects

If projects cannot be partially undertaken, use trial and error / integer programming to find the combination that maximises total NPV within the budget constraint.

Exam Focus: Single-period capital rationing with divisible projects (using PI) is the most commonly tested scenario in ACCA FM. Multi-period rationing (linear programming) is mentioned but rarely requires full LP formulation.

1️⃣3️⃣ Lease vs Buy Analysis ⭐

When acquiring an asset, a company must decide whether to lease or buy. The decision is made by comparing the NPV of the net cost of each option — choose the one with the lower net present cost (higher NPV).

13.1 Approach

  1. Calculate the PV of all costs associated with buying (purchase price, maintenance, tax savings on WDAs, residual value).
  2. Calculate the PV of all costs associated with leasing (lease payments, any costs borne by lessee, tax relief on lease payments).
  3. Compare: Choose the option with the lower net present cost (i.e., less negative NPV).
Example 19

Lease vs Buy Decision

An asset costs £100,000 to buy, with £10,000 annual maintenance for 5 years. It can be sold for £15,000 after 5 years. WDAs are at 25% reducing balance. Alternatively, it can be leased for £28,000 per year for 5 years (payable at year-end), with maintenance included. Tax rate = 25%, tax paid one year in arrears. After-tax cost of borrowing = 8%.

BUY OPTION — PV of Costs:

Key Point: The discount rate for lease vs buy is the after-tax cost of borrowing (not the WACC), because the decision is essentially a financing choice. Both options are evaluated at the same discount rate for fair comparison.

1️⃣4️⃣ Asset Replacement Decisions

How often should an asset be replaced? The goal is to find the optimal replacement cycle that minimises the Equivalent Annual Cost (EAC).

EAC = PV of all costs over the cycle / AF(r, n)

Choose the replacement interval with the lowest EAC.

Example 20

Optimal Replacement Cycle

An asset costs £50,000 to buy. Running costs and resale values depend on age:

YearRunning Cost (£)Resale Value (£)
15,00035,000
28,00025,000
312,00015,000
418,0005,000

Discount rate = 10%. Find the optimal replacement cycle.

Replace every 2 years:

PV costs = 50,000 + 5,000×DF(1yr) + 8,000×DF(2yr) − 25,000×DF(2yr)

= 50,000 + 5,000×0.909 + 8,000×0.826 − 25,000×0.826 = 50,000 + 4,545 + 6,608 − 20,650 = £40,503

EAC = £40,503 / AF(10%, 2yr) = £40,503 / 1.736 = £23,331

(Similarly calculate for 1, 3, and 4-year cycles and pick the lowest EAC.)

1️⃣5️⃣ Sensitivity Analysis

Sensitivity analysis measures how sensitive the NPV is to changes in a single variable — e.g., sales volume, material cost, discount rate.

Sensitivity Margin (%) = (NPV / PV of the variable being tested) × 100%

This tells us by what percentage the variable can worsen before NPV becomes zero.

Example 21

Sensitivity of Sales Revenue

A project has an NPV of £50,000. The PV of total sales revenue (before deducting costs) is £400,000. What is the sensitivity of NPV to sales revenue?

Sensitivity = £50,000 / £400,000 × 100% = 12.5%

Interpretation: If sales revenue falls by more than 12.5%, the NPV becomes negative. The lower the sensitivity margin, the more sensitive (riskier) the project is to that variable.

Exam Tip: Sensitivity analysis is a simple risk assessment tool. Variables with the lowest sensitivity margin are the most critical — management should focus on these.

1️⃣6️⃣ Risk & Uncertainty

16.1 Expected Values (EV)

When probabilities of different outcomes are known, use Expected Values.

EV = Σ (Outcome × Probability)
Example 22

Expected NPV

ScenarioProbabilityNPV (£)
Optimistic0.3200,000
Most Likely0.580,000
Pessimistic0.2(40,000)

EV(NPV) = 0.3×200,000 + 0.5×80,000 + 0.2×(−40,000) = 60,000 + 40,000 − 8,000 = £92,000

16.2 Risk-Adjusted Discount Rate

Increase the discount rate for riskier projects — this reduces the PV of future cash flows, making it harder for risky projects to show a positive NPV.

16.3 Certainty Equivalents

Multiply each cash flow by a certainty equivalent factor (α, where 0 < α < 1) to convert uncertain cash flows to "certain" equivalents, then discount at the risk-free rate.

NPV = Σ [αt × CFt × (1 + rf)−t] − I0
Note: Certainty equivalents are theoretically superior but rarely used in practice due to the subjectivity of choosing α values. Risk-adjusted discount rates are far more common.

1️⃣7️⃣ NPV vs IRR — Conflicts

For mutually exclusive projects, NPV and IRR can give conflicting recommendations in certain situations:

SituationWhy Conflict ArisesWhich to Follow?
Different project sizes (scale)A small project may have a high IRR but low absolute NPV✅ NPV — maximises shareholder wealth in £ terms
Different cash flow patterns (timing)Early cash flows boost IRR; later cash flows may give higher NPV at low discount rates✅ NPV — more reliable
Different project livesIRR ignores the length of the investment✅ Use Equivalent Annual NPV or lowest common multiple
ACCA FM Rule: When NPV and IRR conflict for mutually exclusive projects, always prefer NPV. NPV directly measures the increase in shareholder wealth and is theoretically superior.

1️⃣8️⃣ Exam Tips & Common Pitfalls 🎯

  1. ALWAYS draw a timeline — It helps you correctly place cash flows, tax payments, and working capital movements.
  2. Use clear table formats for NPV calculations — Columns: Year / Item / Cash Flow / DF / PV. This earns method marks.
  3. Tax is usually paid one year in arrears — Unless the question explicitly says "tax is paid in the same year."
  4. Working capital — Outflow at start (investment), recovered at end (inflow). Don't forget the recovery!
  5. Inflation — Be consistent: real cash flows → real discount rate; nominal cash flows → nominal discount rate. Never mix them.
  6. Lease vs Buy — Use the after-tax cost of borrowing as the discount rate, not WACC.
  7. Sunk costs and allocated overheads — Exclude them. They are not relevant.
  8. Opportunity costs — Include them. They represent real economic sacrifices.
  9. IRR interpolation — Keep the two rates within 5% of each other for better accuracy. Show full workings.
  10. Capital rationing — Check if projects are divisible (use PI) or non-divisible (use combinations).
  11. State your decision clearly — "Accept because NPV > 0" or "Reject because IRR < cost of capital." Don't leave the examiner guessing.
Final Reminder: The ACCA FM exam tests your ability to apply these techniques, not just memorise formulas. Practise as many past exam questions as possible under timed conditions. Investment appraisal questions are often worth 15–20 marks — mastering this topic is crucial for passing!
