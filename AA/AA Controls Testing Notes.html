Section 01

The Big Picture — Why Controls Matter to an Auditor

Think of a company like a factory. Products go in, products come out. But without rules and checks in place, things go wrong — errors creep in, fraud happens, and money disappears. These rules and checks are called internal controls.

As an auditor, you don't just check the final numbers. You first look at the system that produces those numbers. If the system has good controls, you can place reliance on it and do less detailed checking of individual transactions. If the system is weak, you do more detailed testing (substantive procedures).

🎯 The Auditor's Journey
STEP 1
Understand the System

Walk through the client's processes (payroll, sales, purchases).

STEP 2
Identify Controls

Find the key direct controls in place.

STEP 3
Test Controls

Check if those controls are actually working (tests of control).

STEP 4
Spot Weaknesses

Identify deficiencies and report to management.

This entire process is called controls testing, and it sits at the heart of your AA exam. Now let's build up each piece.

Section 02

Control Objectives

What Are Control Objectives?

A control objective is simply the goal or purpose that a system of controls is trying to achieve. It answers the question: "What are we trying to prevent going wrong here?"

💡 Simple Analogy

Imagine you run a coffee shop. Your control objective for cash is: "Ensure all cash received from customers is accurately recorded and banked." Your control to achieve this might be a till that issues receipts and a manager who counts the till at the end of each day. The objective is the goal. The control is the mechanism to achieve it.

The ACCA Formula for Writing Control Objectives

In the exam, control objectives are written in a standard way. They always follow this pattern:

The Formula

[Action word] + [what you are protecting / ensuring] + [the risk you are preventing]


Example for a sales system:

"To ensure that all goods dispatched are invoiced to customers — preventing revenue being understated."


"To ensure that only valid, authorised orders are accepted — preventing sales to customers who cannot pay."

Notice the language: "To ensure that..." or "To prevent..." — these are classic exam sentence starters. The objective is always outcome-focused, not process-focused.

⚠️ Common Mistake

Students often confuse a control objective with a control procedure. A control objective is the GOAL. A control procedure is the mechanism.

❌ Wrong as a control objective: "The supervisor checks the payslips." — this is a control, not an objective.
✅ Correct control objective: "To ensure that gross-to-net pay calculations are accurate and complete."

Section 03

Types of Controls

Preventive vs Detective Controls

This is one of the most tested distinctions in the AA exam.

🛡️ Preventive Control

Stops an error or fraud from happening in the first place. Like a seatbelt — it prevents harm before it occurs.

🔍 Detective Control

Identifies that an error or fraud has already happened. Like a smoke alarm — it tells you something has gone wrong after the fact.

🔑 Example — Preventive

A credit check before accepting a new customer prevents selling goods to someone who can't pay.

🔑 Example — Detective

A monthly aged receivables review detects overdue balances that already exist on the ledger.

Manual vs Automated Controls

Controls can also be performed by people or by computer systems.

TypeDescriptionExampleStrength
Manual Performed by a human Supervisor signing a payroll listing Flexible, uses judgement; but prone to human error
Automated Performed by the IT system System calculating gross-to-net pay automatically Consistent, no human error; but only as good as the programming
IT-dependent Manual Human uses system-generated output to perform a check Supervisor reviews a system-generated payroll report Combines both — relies on system integrity AND human judgement

IT General Controls (ITGCs)

These are broad controls over the entire IT environment. They don't directly prevent individual transaction errors, but they support all other automated controls. If ITGCs are weak, you cannot rely on automated controls.

  • Access controls — Who can access the system? (Passwords, user permissions)
  • Change management — Controls over changes to programs and data
  • Data backup and recovery — Ensuring data isn't lost
  • Physical security — Protecting servers and hardware
Section 04

Direct Controls

What Makes a Control "Direct"?

A direct control is one that directly addresses a specific control objective. It is the mechanism that, if working properly, achieves the goal described in the control objective.

📌 Key Point

In the exam, when asked for "direct controls," you must link each control to the specific risk or objective it addresses. A control is only "direct" in relation to a specific assertion or risk. The same control might not be relevant to a different risk.

Think of it as a lock-and-key relationship:

  • Risk / Objective = the lock (what needs to be secured)
  • Direct Control = the key (the mechanism that achieves security for that specific risk)

The Petra Co payroll scenario gives us several real examples. Let's break them down:

💡 How to Write a Direct Control in the Exam

Always include: WHO does WHAT, WHEN, and WHAT evidence is produced.

❌ Weak answer: "The supervisor checks the payslips."
✅ Strong answer: "The payroll supervisor selects a sample of payslips, reperforms the gross-to-net pay calculations, investigates any discrepancies, and signs the payslips as evidence of their review."

Section 05

Tests of Control (ToC)

What Does the Auditor Actually Do?

A test of control answers the question: "Is this control actually working in practice?"

Just because a company has a control doesn't mean it's being applied properly. The auditor needs evidence. Tests of control gather that evidence.

📌 ISA 330 Reminder

Under ISA 330 (Auditor's Responses to Assessed Risks), the auditor should perform tests of control when: (1) the risk assessment relies on controls operating effectively, or (2) substantive procedures alone cannot provide sufficient evidence at the assertion level.

The Four Testing Techniques

1. Inspection

Look at documents for evidence the control took place — signatures, authorisation stamps, tick marks, dates.

2. Observation

Watch the control being performed in real time. Useful for controls that leave no paper trail (e.g., watching cash packets being counted).

3. Re-performance

The auditor independently repeats the control themselves — e.g., recalculates payroll to verify the supervisor's check was correct.

4. Enquiry

Ask the client how the control works. But remember: enquiry alone is NEVER sufficient — it must be corroborated by another method.

The Direct Control → Test of Control Link

In the exam, when you identify a direct control, you must design a test that directly tests that specific control. The test must verify that the control was: (a) carried out, and (b) effective.

Full Worked Examples — Payroll Direct Controls & Tests
Direct ControlTest of ControlTechnique Used
Payroll supervisor reperforms gross-to-net calculations and signs payslips as evidence For a sample of payslips, inspect for the supervisor's signature confirming review was performed. Reperform the gross-to-net calculation and compare to payslip amounts to verify accuracy. Inspection Re-performance
Senior clerk reviews revised pay rates and signs the payroll listing Inspect the payroll listing for the period of the pay rate change and confirm the senior clerk's signature is present. Agree the revised rates on the listing to the authorised communication received from HR/management. Inspection
Two staff prepare and re-count cash packets; both sign the weekly payroll listing For a sample of weekly payroll listings, inspect for the signatures of both members of staff, confirming the segregation of duties was observed. Inspection
Clock card system automatically records hours worked; used to calculate pay For a sample of employees, agree the hours on clock card records to the hours used in the payroll calculation to confirm the system linkage is operating correctly. Inspection Re-performance
💡 Exam Technique — How to Write a Test of Control

Your test must always answer three things:
(1) What will you look at? (e.g., payslips, payroll listing, GRNs)
(2) How will you select it? (e.g., "for a sample of...")
(3) What are you checking for? (e.g., signature present, amounts agree, sequence complete)


❌ Weak: "Check the payroll."
✅ Strong: "For a sample of weekly payroll listings, inspect for the signatures of both members of staff responsible for preparing and re-counting the cash pay packets."

📌 Don't Confuse Test of Control with Substantive Procedure

A test of control checks whether a CONTROL worked. A substantive procedure checks whether the underlying FIGURE is correct.

Test of control: "Inspect payslip for supervisor's signature."
Substantive procedure: "Reperform the payroll calculation for a sample of employees and agree to the amount paid."

The exam often asks for one or the other — read the question carefully!

Section 06

Control Deficiencies

How to Spot a Deficiency

A control deficiency exists when a control is absent, inadequate, or is not operating effectively. When reading a scenario, ask yourself: "What could go wrong here that no one would catch?" Where you find no control (or a weak one), there is a deficiency.

🔍 The Deficiency Detection Checklist

When reading a scenario, look for these red flags:

  • No segregation of duties — one person does everything from start to finish with no check
  • No authorisation — things happen without approval from an appropriate person
  • No independent check / review — no one verifies what another person has done
  • No sequence check — documents aren't numbered, so gaps can't be identified
  • Over-broad access — too many people can access/change sensitive records
  • No periodic review — standing data (credit limits, pay rates, supplier files) is never revisited
  • Discretion without oversight — employees can make decisions (e.g., discounts) with no review

Common Deficiency Patterns — Tested in AA

AreaDeficiencyWhy It's a Problem
Sales Credit limits are only reviewed when the customer requests an increase Credit limits may become outdated; no proactive monitoring of customer risk; irrecoverable debts may arise undetected
Sales Sales staff can grant discounts up to 8% with no review undertaken Discounts may be given to friends/family, or used as kickbacks; revenue is understated and no one would know
Purchases All members of the purchasing department have full access to the supplier master file Any employee could set up a fictitious supplier and authorise payments — classic fraud risk
Purchases GRNs filed in warehouse but not matched to purchase invoices before payment Invoices could be paid for goods never received; no 3-way match
Payroll Pay rates entered by a clerk with no independent authorisation prior to change Unauthorised rate increases could be entered; overpayment would go undetected
General One person both initiates and approves a transaction No segregation of duties — fraud risk high; errors cannot be caught

Recommendations — How to Write Them

For every deficiency, you need a practical recommendation. The recommendation should directly fix the specific deficiency identified. It should be realistic and specific to the company's context.

Deficiency → Recommendation Examples
DeficiencyRecommendation
Sales staff grant discounts up to 8% with no review A regular review (e.g., monthly) of all discounts granted should be performed by the sales manager or finance director. A report of all discounts given should be generated from the system and reviewed for unusual patterns.
Credit limits only reviewed on customer request Credit limits should be reviewed periodically (e.g., annually) for all customers, or more frequently for customers with large balances or overdue accounts. The finance director should sign off all limit reviews.
All purchasing staff have full access to supplier master file Access to the supplier master file should be restricted to a designated administrator only — no one who also raises or approves purchase orders. Any changes to the file should require a second authorisation from a senior manager.
GRNs filed in warehouse without being matched to invoices A three-way matching process should be implemented: purchase order, GRN, and supplier invoice must all be matched before payment is authorised. Payables ledger clerks should perform this match and only process invoices where all three documents agree.
Section 07

Substantive Procedures — Purchases & Expenses

Even when controls are good, the auditor must perform substantive procedures — these directly verify the figures in the financial statements. For purchases and other expenses, the auditor is checking that:

  • Expenses are complete — all liabilities are recorded
  • Expenses are accurate — the amounts are correct
  • Expenses are valid/occurrence — they relate to real business transactions
  • Expenses are cut-off — recorded in the right period
  • Expenses are correctly classified — shown in the right line of the accounts
Standard Substantive Procedures for Purchases & Expenses
ProcedureAssertion Tested
Obtain a schedule of purchases/expenses and cast (add up) to agree to the trial balance Completeness, Accuracy
For a sample of purchase invoices, agree the amount per invoice to the payables ledger entry Accuracy, Occurrence
For a sample of GRNs, trace to the corresponding purchase invoice and payables ledger — confirm all goods received have been recorded as a liability Completeness
For a sample of purchase invoices, inspect to confirm they are authorised by an appropriate manager Occurrence, Authorisation
Test cut-off: review invoices received just before and just after year end; confirm recorded in the correct period Cut-off
Review for any unusual or one-off expenses; obtain explanation from management and inspect supporting documentation Occurrence, Accuracy
Obtain a schedule of accruals; agree to supporting documentation (e.g., invoices received after year end for services in the period) Completeness, Accuracy
Compare expense categories as a percentage of revenue to prior year and investigate significant variances (analytical procedure) Completeness, Accuracy
Section 08

Key Exam Techniques for Questions Like Petra

Part (a) — List Control Objectives

You need 4 distinct objectives for the sales system. Each should be one clear sentence starting with "To ensure that..." or "To prevent...". Cover the whole cycle: order → credit check → dispatch → invoicing → collection. Avoid repeating the same idea in different words.

Part (b) — Direct Controls & Tests of Control (Payroll)

The question asks for 3 direct controls and a test of control for each. The marks are split equally — 1 mark per direct control, 1 mark per test = 6 marks total.

For each control: describe clearly what the control does and why it is relevant.
For each test: say what document you inspect, how you select the sample, and what you verify.

Part (c) — 5 Deficiencies + 5 Recommendations (Sales & Purchases)

10 marks split equally = 1 mark per deficiency + 1 mark per recommendation. Cover both systems — don't just focus on one.

For deficiencies: describe what is missing AND explain why it is a problem (what risk arises).
For recommendations: be specific and practical — don't just say "implement controls."

Part (d) — Substantive Procedures for Purchases

4 marks. Write 4 clear, distinct procedures. Each procedure should state: what you obtain / select → what you agree it to → what you are verifying. Use the word "obtain," "inspect," "agree," "cast," "review," "trace."

A Final Word on Answering Style

The Golden Rule for AA Written Questions

Always relate your answer back to the scenario. Generic textbook answers score poorly. If the question is about Petra Co's payroll system, your answer must reference clock cards, cash pay packets, payroll supervisor, etc. The examiner is testing whether you can apply your knowledge, not just recall it.


Use the facts in the scenario as your anchor — every point you make should connect to something specific mentioned about Petra Co.
