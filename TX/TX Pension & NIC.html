Pensions – Overview & Syllabus Scope

📋 Syllabus Reference

Syllabus Area B7a: "Explain and compute the relief given for contributions to personal pension schemes, and to occupational pension schemes." — Level [2]: Explain & Compute.

Why Pensions Matter in Tax

Pension contributions are one of the most powerful tax-planning tools available to individuals. Contributions attract income tax relief — meaning the money goes into a pension before tax is deducted (in effect), reducing your taxable income and your overall income tax bill.

For the TX exam, you need to understand two types of pension schemes and how tax relief works on contributions made by both employees/individuals and employers.

Scheme Type Who contributes? How is relief given? Exam Focus
Occupational Pension Scheme (Employer-run) Employee + Employer Employee contributions deducted from gross pay before tax ✅ High
Personal Pension Scheme (Individual-run) Individual Basic rate relief at source (gross-up method) ✅ High
🚫 Excluded from Syllabus
  • Conditions for HMRC approval of pension schemes
  • The £10,000 anti-avoidance annual allowance limit (the tapering down to £10,000 minimum IS examinable)
  • The threshold income level below which tapering does not apply
02

Occupational Pension Schemes

What is an Occupational Pension Scheme?

An occupational pension scheme (OPS) is set up and run by an employer for the benefit of its employees. Both the employee and the employer can make contributions.

How Tax Relief Works for Employees

Employee contributions to an OPS are paid from gross salary before income tax is applied. This means the contribution is deducted when calculating taxable employment income. The employee benefits automatically at their marginal rate of tax.

Employment Income (gross) £X,XXX Less: Pension contribution (OPS) (£ XXX) ─────────────────────────────────────── Taxable employment income £X,XXX // No grossing up needed — relief is automatic

Employer Contributions

Contributions made by the employer are:

  • Deductible as a business expense for the employer (reducing corporation tax or self-employment profits)
  • Not a taxable benefit for the employee — employer pension contributions do not form part of the employee's employment income
  • Not subject to Class 1 NIC
✅ Key Point

For OPS, employer contributions are ignored in the employee's tax computation. They are not a benefit-in-kind. Only employee contributions affect the employment income figure.

✎
Example 1 — Occupational Pension: Employee Relief
Basic rate taxpayer

Amelia has a gross salary of £45,000. She pays 5% (£2,250) into her employer's occupational pension scheme. Her employer also contributes 4% (£1,800).

Tax saving from OPS contribution: £2,250 × 20% = £450

03

Personal Pension Schemes

What is a Personal Pension Scheme?

A personal pension scheme (PPS) is taken out by an individual directly with a pension provider (e.g., an insurance company). It is not linked to an employer.

Personal pensions are particularly relevant for:

  • Self-employed individuals
  • Employees who want additional pension savings beyond their OPS
  • Individuals with no employment income (up to the £3,600 limit)

The "Gross-Up" Method — Relief at Source

This is the most commonly tested aspect. When a personal pension contribution is made, the individual pays a net amount and HMRC tops it up with basic rate (20%) tax relief automatically. The pension provider receives the gross amount.

Individual pays to pension provider (net): £8,000 HMRC adds basic rate relief (20/80 × £8,000): £2,000 ──────────────────────────────────────────────────── Gross pension contribution: £10,000 // Key: Gross = Net ÷ 0.80 OR Net × 100/80
💡 Important Formula

Gross contribution = Net amount paid ÷ 0.80

Alternatively: Net amount × 100/80

The exam will often give you either the net or gross amount — make sure you use the correct one in the income tax computation.

Effect on Income Tax Computation

The gross contribution extends the basic rate band. Instead of deducting the pension from income, we extend the basic rate threshold upwards by the gross contribution amount. This ensures higher-rate taxpayers also get relief at their marginal rate.

Normal basic rate band: £37,700 Add: Gross pension contribution: + £10,000 ───────────────────────────────────────── Extended basic rate band: £47,700 // Higher rate tax begins at £47,700 instead of £37,700
✎
Example 2 — Personal Pension: Higher Rate Taxpayer
The gross-up and band extension method

Benjamin has total income of £55,000. He pays £8,000 net into a personal pension scheme.

Step 1: Calculate gross contribution: £8,000 ÷ 0.80 = £10,000 gross

Step 2: Extend basic rate band: £37,700 + £10,000 = £47,700

🎯
Exam Tip: The basic rate relief of £2,000 (the HMRC top-up) is already received via the pension provider. The income tax computation captures the additional higher-rate relief. This is why we extend the band rather than deduct from income.

Minimum Contribution (No Earnings Rule)

Even if someone has no earnings at all, they can still make a personal pension contribution of up to £3,600 gross per year (i.e., pay £2,880 net and HMRC tops up to £3,600).

This is useful for, e.g., a spouse/partner with no income — they can still benefit from basic rate relief at source.

04

Annual Allowance & Contribution Limits

The Annual Allowance

The annual allowance is the maximum gross pension contribution that can be made in a tax year across all pension schemes (combined) while still receiving full tax relief.

Tax YearAnnual AllowanceNotes
2022–23£40,000Pre-increase year
2023–24£60,000Increased — applies for June 2026 exam
2024–25£60,000Standard for FA2025
2025–26£60,000Standard for FA2025 / J26 exam

Earnings Limit

The gross pension contribution eligible for relief is capped at 100% of the individual's UK earnings for the tax year (or £3,600 if that is higher — see minimum rule above).

📌 Key Rule

Tax relief is limited to the lower of:

  • The annual allowance: £60,000
  • 100% of the individual's relevant UK earnings (employment income, trading income, etc.)

If contributions exceed these limits, the excess is subject to an annual allowance charge.

Tapering of the Annual Allowance

For high earners, the annual allowance is gradually reduced ("tapered"). The following figures are from the FA2025 tax tables:

ItemAmount
Standard annual allowance£60,000
Minimum annual allowance (tapered floor)£10,000
Income limit (threshold for tapering)£260,000

For every £2 of "adjusted income" above £260,000, the annual allowance reduces by £1. The minimum it can be tapered to is £10,000.

Example: Adjusted income = £280,000 → Excess over £260,000 = £20,000 → Reduction = £10,000 → Annual allowance = £60,000 − £10,000 = £50,000

🎯
Exam Tip: The syllabus EXCLUDES the threshold income level below which tapering doesn't apply. It also EXCLUDES the £10,000 limit itself as a calculation — but the tapering down to a minimum of £10,000 is examinable. Focus on: standard allowance = £60,000, tapered down by £1 per £2 over £260,000, minimum = £10,000.

Carry Forward of Unused Allowance

Individuals can carry forward unused annual allowance from the three preceding tax years to make larger contributions in the current year without an annual allowance charge. The current year's allowance must be used first before carrying forward.

This is a planning tool examined in Section B7(c) of the syllabus under exemptions and reliefs.

05

Tax Relief on Contributions — Summary

Adjusted Net Income & Personal Allowance Interaction

Personal pension contributions (gross) reduce the individual's adjusted net income (ANI). This is important because ANI determines:

  • Whether the personal allowance is tapered (ANI > £100,000)
  • The child benefit income tax charge (ANI between £60,000 and £80,000)
  • The savings nil rate band available
✎
Example 3 — Pension & Personal Allowance Trap
High earner restoring personal allowance via pension contribution

Charlotte has employment income of £110,000. She pays £8,000 net (£10,000 gross) into a personal pension.

Without pension contribution:

  • ANI = £110,000 → excess over £100,000 = £10,000
  • Personal allowance reduced by £10,000/2 = £5,000 → PA = £12,570 − £5,000 = £7,570

With pension contribution (gross £10,000):

  • ANI = £110,000 − £10,000 = £100,000
  • ANI = £100,000 → no reduction → full PA = £12,570

Effective tax saving: £5,000 × 40% = £2,000 extra saving by restoring PA!

🎯
Exam Tip: Pension contributions can be a key tool to reduce ANI below £100,000 to restore the personal allowance. This generates an effective marginal tax rate of 60% in the £100,000–£125,140 band, making pension contributions extremely valuable here.

National Insurance Contributions — Overview

📋 Syllabus Reference

Syllabus Area B6a & B6b: "Explain and compute national insurance contributions payable: Class 1 and 1A NIC [2], Class 4 NIC [2]. Understand the annual employment allowance [2]."

Excluded: Calculation of directors' NIC on a month-by-month basis. Exemption from employer's Class 1 NIC for employees under 21 / apprentices under 25. Group aspects of employment allowance. Class 2 NIC.

What Are NICs?

National Insurance Contributions (NICs) are a separate tax from income tax, paid by employees, employers, and the self-employed. They fund state benefits including the State Pension, NHS, and unemployment support.

NICs are assessed on a weekly or annual basis (TX uses annual thresholds for simplicity).

Classes of NIC — Summary

Class 1 NIC — Employee (Primary)

FA2025 Rates (from Tax Tables J26–J27)

Earnings Band (Per Year) Rate Notes
£1 – £12,5700%Below Primary Threshold (PT) — NIL
£12,571 – £50,2708%Main rate band
£50,271 and above2%Above Upper Earnings Limit (UEL) — reduced rate
📌 Key Thresholds (FA2025)
  • Primary Threshold (PT): £12,570 — earnings below this are NIC-free for the employee
  • Upper Earnings Limit (UEL): £50,270 — above this, the rate drops to 2%

Note: The PT aligns with the personal allowance (£12,570). This makes computations cleaner.

What Counts as Earnings for Class 1?

Class 1 NIC is charged on cash payments including:

  • Gross salary and wages
  • Bonuses and commission
  • Holiday pay
  • Statutory sick pay / maternity pay (treated as earnings)

Not included in Class 1 earnings:

  • Benefits-in-kind (company cars, private medical insurance, etc.) — these are subject to Class 1A instead
  • Reimbursed business expenses (where properly evidenced)
  • Employer pension contributions
  • Employee pension contributions to OPS also reduce Class 1 NIC base
Employee Class 1 NIC Calculation: Earnings between £12,571 and £50,270: (£50,270 − £12,570) × 8% = £37,700 × 8% = £3,016 Earnings above £50,270 (e.g., £60,000): (£60,000 − £50,270) × 2% = £9,730 × 2% = £195 Total Class 1 Employee NIC = £3,016 + £195 = £3,211 // Always check whether salary is above £50,270!

⚠️ Important FA2025 Change

For FA2025 (J26 exam), the employer's secondary threshold is £5,000 (reduced from £9,100 in prior years) and the rate is 15% (increased from 13.8%). This is a significant change — make sure you use the correct figures from the FA2025 tax tables.

Also note: there is NO upper earnings limit for employer NIC. The 15% rate applies to all earnings above £5,000 without cap.

Employer Class 1 NIC Calculation: For earnings of £38,000: (£38,000 − £5,000) × 15% = £33,000 × 15% = £4,950 For earnings of £75,000: (£75,000 − £5,000) × 15% = £70,000 × 15% = £10,500 // No UEL for employer — 15% continues indefinitely above £5,000

Class 1A NIC — Benefits-in-Kind

Class 1A NIC is charged by the employer only on the value of taxable benefits-in-kind provided to employees. The rate is 15% (same as Class 1 employer rate for FA2025).

📌 Key Points — Class 1A NIC
  • Paid by the employer only — employees do not pay Class 1A NIC
  • Applies to all taxable benefits-in-kind: company cars, fuel, private medical insurance, etc.
  • Rate: 15% of the taxable benefit value
  • Class 1A NIC is a deductible expense for the employer (reduces corporation tax)
  • It is not payable through payroll — paid annually in July after the tax year end
Class 1A NIC = Total taxable benefits-in-kind × 15% Example: Company car benefit = £4,500 Private medical = £800 Total benefits = £5,300 Class 1A NIC = £5,300 × 15% = £795 // Employer pays this; employee pays income tax on the benefits
✎
Example 7 — Class 1A NIC on Company Car
Petrol car benefit + Class 1A calculation

Grace is provided a company petrol car with a list price of £20,000 and CO₂ emissions of 110g/km. She also receives private medical insurance worth £600 per year.

Step 1: Car benefit percentage

  • Base level = 55g/km = 17%
  • Emissions above 55g: (110 − 55) = 55g/km above → 55 ÷ 5 = 11 additional percentage points
  • Car benefit % = 17% + 11% = 28%

Step 2: Car benefit value

  • £20,000 × 28% = £5,600

Class 4 NIC — Self-Employed

Overview

Class 4 NIC is paid by self-employed individuals on their trading profits. It is calculated annually alongside the income tax self-assessment return. Unlike employees, the self-employed do not pay Class 1 NIC on their trading profits.

FA2025 Rates (from Tax Tables J26–J27)

Profit Band (Per Year) Rate Notes
£1 – £12,5700%Below Lower Profits Limit (LPL) — NIL
£12,571 – £50,2706%Main rate band
£50,271 and above2%Above Upper Profits Limit (UPL) — reduced rate
📌 Compare Class 1 vs Class 4

The thresholds for Class 4 NIC mirror the Class 1 employee NIC thresholds (PT = £12,570, UEL/UPL = £50,270). However, the rates are lower:

  • Employee Class 1: 8% (main), 2% (above UEL)
  • Self-employed Class 4: 6% (main), 2% (above UPL)

This rate difference recognises that the self-employed do not receive the same level of state benefit entitlement.

Class 4 NIC = (Trading profits − £12,570) × 6% + (Profits above £50,270) × 2% Example: Trading profits = £40,000 = (£40,000 − £12,570) × 6% = £27,430 × 6% = £1,646 Example: Trading profits = £65,000 = (£50,270 − £12,570) × 6% + (£65,000 − £50,270) × 2% = £37,700 × 6% + £14,730 × 2% = £2,262 + £295 = £2,557
✎

Employment Allowance

What is the Employment Allowance?

The employment allowance is a reduction available to eligible employers which reduces their Class 1 employer NIC liability.

ItemFA2025 Amount
Employment Allowance£10,500 per year
📌 Key Rules
  • Reduces the employer's Class 1 secondary NIC liability
  • Available to most employers — but NOT available to single-director companies where the director is the only employee
  • The allowance is claimed via the payroll (Real Time Information submissions) and offsets NIC liability throughout the year
  • Only one employment allowance per group of companies (group aspects are excluded from the TX syllabus)
  • It is not refundable — can only be used to reduce employer NIC to nil
✎
Example 10 — Employment Allowance
Reducing employer Class 1 NIC liability

A small employer has 3 employees with total gross salaries of £80,000.

Item£
Total gross salaries80,000
Employer Class 1 NIC: (£80,000 − £5,000) × 15%11,250
Less: Employment allowance(10,500)

The employment allowance saves this employer £10,500 in employer NIC — a significant reduction for small businesses.

🎯
Exam Tip: Always check whether the employment allowance is available in a question. If a company has only one employee who is also the sole director, the allowance is not available. If the allowance is available, deduct it from employer Class 1 NIC after calculating the gross liability.

Common Exam Mistakes to Avoid

⚠️ Common Errors
  1. Using net vs gross pension: Always gross up PPS contributions before extending the band. £8,000 net ≠ £8,000 gross — it's £10,000 gross.
  2. Deducting PPS from income: Personal pensions do NOT reduce income — they extend the basic rate band. OPS contributions DO reduce income.
  3. Forgetting no upper limit for employer NIC: Employee Class 1 reduces to 2% above £50,270. Employer Class 1 stays at 15% with no upper limit.
  4. Including employer pension in employee income: Employer pension contributions to OPS are NOT employment income and NOT a benefit-in-kind.
  5. Applying Class 1A to cash earnings: Class 1A only applies to benefits-in-kind, not cash salary.
  6. Forgetting employment allowance: If a question asks for employer NIC and mentions a small employer, check whether the employment allowance applies and deduct £10,500.
  7. Applying personal allowance to Class 4: Class 4 NIC uses its own lower profits limit (£12,570), not the personal allowance calculation.

Key Decision Tree for Pensions

Is it an OPS contribution? YES → Deduct from gross employment income directly → Also reduces Class 1 NIC-able earnings → No grossing up needed Is it a PPS contribution? YES → Gross up: Net ÷ 0.80 → Extend basic rate band by gross amount → Does NOT reduce income → Reduces ANI (relevant for PA tapering, child benefit charge) → Check annual allowance: gross ≤ lower of £60,000 / 100% earnings

Key Decision Tree for NIC

Is the person employed? YES → Class 1 Primary (Employee): 8% on £12,571–£50,270; 2% above Class 1 Secondary (Employer): 15% on all above £5,000 Class 1A (Employer): 15% on benefits-in-kind Employment Allowance: reduces employer Class 1 (if eligible) Is the person self-employed? YES → Class 4: 6% on £12,571–£50,270; 2% above £50,270 Class 2: EXCLUDED from syllabus
🎯
Final Exam Tip: For Section B questions on NIC and pensions, show all workings clearly in a structured format. The marker rewards a logical approach — even if your final number is slightly off, intermediate marks are awarded for correct method. Always label which class of NIC you are calculating.
