Tax-Adjusted Trading Profit

1.1 What Is the Tax-Adjusted Trading Profit?

A sole trader's accounts are prepared under accounting rules (e.g. including depreciation, private expenses). These are NOT the same as the rules HMRC uses to tax you. The process of converting the accounting profit into the taxable trading profit is called the adjustment of profits.

The key principle underlying every adjustment is the "wholly and exclusively" rule: an expense is only deductible for tax purposes if it is incurred wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the trade. If it has a private element, only the business portion (if identifiable) is allowed.

The Core Rule: An expense is allowable only if it is incurred wholly and exclusively for the purposes of the trade (ITA 2007 / ITTOIA 2005). The expense must also be revenue in nature — capital expenditure is never deductible as a revenue expense (it gets capital allowances instead).

1.2 The Pro Forma — How to Lay Out an Adjustment

Always start with the net profit per the accounts. You then make two types of adjustment:

  • Add back any expense that is disallowable (i.e. it has been deducted in the accounts but HMRC won't allow it).
  • Deduct any item that is allowable for tax but has not been deducted in the accounts (principally capital allowances, which replace depreciation).

1.3 Detailed Tutorial — Disallowable Items Explained

A) Depreciation

Depreciation is the accounting way of spreading the cost of a fixed asset over its useful life. HMRC does not accept depreciation as a deduction because tax relief for capital expenditure is given through the capital allowances system instead (see Chapter 2). Therefore, whenever depreciation appears in accounts, it must be added back in full.

Example
Depreciation charged in accounts = £5,000. Capital allowances computed = £4,200.

You add back £5,000 (depreciation) AND deduct £4,200 (capital allowances). These are two separate adjustments — you never net them off.

B) Private Use of Expenses (Owner/Sole Trader)

If a sole trader uses something partly for personal purposes (e.g. a car, home phone, a room at home), only the business proportion is deductible. The private element must be added back. This only applies where private use by the owner is involved. If an employee uses a company car privately, no adjustment is made to trading profit — the employee is instead charged a benefit-in-kind.

Example — Motor Expenses
Idris drives 13,000 business miles out of 20,000 total miles. Motor expenses in accounts = £9,340.

Business proportion = 13,000/20,000 = 65%.

Allowable amount = £9,340 × 65% = £6,071.

Private element to add back = £9,340 × 35% = £3,269.

Note: For capital allowances, the car is put in a separate private use column. The WDA is computed first, then multiplied by the business proportion to give the actual allowance (65% in this case).

C) Capital Expenditure in the P&L

Sometimes a sole trader incorrectly charges capital expenditure (e.g. buying new equipment, building improvements) through the income statement rather than capitalising it. For tax purposes, capital expenditure is never deductible as a revenue expense. It must be added back. Capital allowances may then be available instead.

⚠️ Capital vs Revenue — Key Distinction

Revenue expenditure: maintains an existing asset, or relates to trading operations (e.g. repainting existing offices — allowable). Capital expenditure: creates a new asset or provides an enduring benefit (e.g. building a new extension — not deductible, but SBA may apply). Legal fees for buying/selling a capital asset = capital. Legal fees for renewing a short lease (≤50 years) = revenue and allowable.

D) Entertaining

Entertaining customers, suppliers, or any external parties is never deductible — add it all back. The only exception is staff entertaining (e.g. the annual office party), which is fully allowable.

Common Confusion — VAT vs Income Tax

For VAT purposes, input VAT can be recovered on entertaining overseas customers. But for income tax / corporation tax purposes, ALL customer and supplier entertaining is disallowed — including overseas customers. Students often confuse these two rules.

E) Gifts to Customers

Gifts to customers are a disallowable form of entertaining UNLESS all three conditions are met:

  • The gift costs less than £50 per recipient per year
  • The gift is not food, drink, tobacco or a voucher exchangeable for goods
  • The gift carries a conspicuous advertisement for the business
Example — Gifts
Pens with company logo at £12 each — allowable (under £50, not food/drink, carries ad). Food hampers at £75 each — disallowable (food item). Clocks at £60 each — disallowable (over £50).

F) Fines and Penalties

Fines (e.g. parking fines, speeding fines, HMRC penalties) are never deductible. The rationale is that allowing a tax deduction for breaking the law would undermine the purpose of the penalty. Add back in full.

G) Charitable Donations

Qualifying charitable donations (paid under Gift Aid) are not deductible in computing trading profit — they are instead deductible from total income at a later stage. Add them back. Exception: a donation is allowable as a trading expense if it is wholly and exclusively for trade purposes and can be shown to be genuine advertising (e.g. sponsoring a local community event where the business logo appears prominently).

H) Legal and Professional Fees

This area requires careful analysis because legal fees can be either revenue (allowable) or capital (disallowable):

✅ Allowable Legal Fees
  • Renewal of a short lease (≤50 years)
  • Debt collection / recovery
  • Defending a trading lawsuit
  • Employment contract disputes
  • Routine accountancy and tax compliance
❌ Disallowable Legal Fees
  • Acquisition of a capital asset
  • Disposal of a capital asset
  • Acquiring a new long lease (>50 years)
  • Business incorporation
  • Share capital issues

I) Bad Debts

The correct treatment of bad debts is nuanced:

  • Specific write-off of a trade debt — allowable (the debt has been identified as irrecoverable)
  • Recovery of a previously written-off debt — add back to income (a taxable receipt)
  • General provision (e.g. "2% of debtors may go bad") — disallowable, add back. HMRC requires specific identification.
  • Increase in general provision — disallowable, add back. Decrease in general provision — the original provision was disallowed, so the release is also non-taxable (deduct from profit).

1.4 Allowable Items — What You CAN Deduct

✅ Always Allowable
  • Staff salaries, wages and bonuses
  • Employer pension contributions
  • Staff entertaining and staff welfare
  • Business insurance premiums
  • Rent of business premises
  • Business telephone and internet
  • Stationery and office supplies
  • Revenue repairs and maintenance
  • Patent royalties for trade purposes
  • Specific bad debt write-offs
  • Qualifying gift to customers (see above)
✅ Conditionally Allowable
  • Motor expenses — business proportion only (for owner's private use car)
  • Home office — reasonable proportion of bills
  • Charitable donations — only if genuine advertising
  • Legal fees — revenue nature only
  • Subsistence — if away from usual base of operations

1.5 Pre-Trading Expenditure

If someone spends money before they start trading — perhaps fitting out premises, buying initial stock, or marketing costs — they might worry they'll lose the tax relief. HMRC provides specific relief:

The Rule: Expenditure incurred within seven years before the date the trade commences is treated as if it were incurred on the first day of trading, provided it would have been revenue-deductible had the trade been carried on at the time it was incurred.
Example — Pre-Trading Expenditure
Wretched Ltd commenced trading on 1 August 2025. Advertising expenditure of £7,990 was incurred in April 2025 — three months before trading commenced.

Since the advertising was incurred within seven years of starting to trade, it is treated as incurred on 1 August 2025 (first day of trading) and is deductible. The pre-trading expenditure is deducted as if it arose in the first accounting period.

1.6 The Badges of Trade (SCOTEM)

Whether a person is "trading" (and therefore subject to income tax on profits) rather than simply making an investment gain (subject to CGT) is determined by reference to the badges of trade. No single badge is conclusive — all must be weighed together. The mnemonic SCOTEM covers them:

BadgeWhat It MeansPointing Towards Trade When...

Transactions to make profitProfit motiveClear intention to make a profit; organised commercial manner

📌 Exam Technique — Applying SCOTEM

In the exam, if you are asked to apply the badges of trade, do not just list them — apply each badge to the facts given and state whether it points towards or against trading activity. Then give a conclusion. You will score marks for each badge discussed in the context of the question.

Capital Allowances

2.1 Why Capital Allowances Exist

Depreciation is never deductible for tax. Instead, the tax system provides its own relief for capital expenditure — called capital allowances. These are calculated using standardised rates set by HMRC and are deducted from the tax-adjusted trading profit. Capital allowances are therefore more generous or less generous than accounting depreciation depending on the asset.

Capital allowances apply to plant and machinery (P&M). "Plant and machinery" is not defined in statute but case law and practice means it includes: machinery, equipment, computers, vehicles, fixtures & fittings, and integral building features.

2.2 The Pools System

Assets are placed into pools. A pool is a running total of the written-down value (TWDV) of qualifying assets. Each year, the TWDV at the start of the year is adjusted for additions and disposals, and then writing down allowances (WDA) are applied to the remaining balance.

2.3 The Annual Investment Allowance (AIA)

The AIA gives a 100% deduction in the year of purchase for most plant and machinery. This is extremely valuable — it means the full cost of qualifying expenditure can be deducted in year one rather than over many years. The key rules are:

  • AIA limit = £1,000,000 per annum. Time-apportioned for short accounting periods (e.g. 9-month period: £1,000,000 × 9/12 = £750,000).
  • AIA applies to additions in both the main pool and special rate pool.
  • Cars never qualify for AIA — they have their own rules.
  • AIA should be claimed on special rate pool additions first (because those would otherwise only get 6% WDA). After that, claim on main pool additions.

Why Claim AIA on SRP First?

If you have £50,000 of main pool additions and £20,000 of special rate pool additions, and the AIA limit is sufficient to cover both, then claim all £20,000 SRP first (saving from 6% → 100%) and all £50,000 main pool (saving from 18% → 100%). If AIA is limited, always maximise relief by covering the slowest pool first.

2.4 First Year Allowances (FYA)

A 100% FYA is available on new, zero-emission cars only (CO₂ = 0g/km). Key rules:

  • The car must be brand new — a second-hand zero-emission car does NOT qualify (it gets 18% WDA instead).
  • FYA is never time-apportioned, even if the accounting period is shorter than 12 months.
  • FYA is 100% — the full cost is deducted in year one.

2.5 Writing Down Allowances (WDA)

WDA is applied to the remaining balance of each pool after AIA and FYA claims, and after adjusting for disposals. The rate depends on the pool:

Time Apportionment of WDA: WDA is time-apportioned for short accounting periods. For example, if the period is 9 months: WDA = 18% × 9/12. The small pool WDA of £1,000 is also time-apportioned. Exception: FYA is never time-apportioned.

2.6 Cars — Special Rules

Cars have their own regime based entirely on CO₂ emissions. Cars can NEVER get AIA. The rules are:

Private Use Cars — The Separate Column Rule

When a sole trader (the owner) uses a car partly for private purposes, that car MUST be shown in a separate private use column. The WDA is computed on the full balance, then multiplied by the business use percentage to give the allowable amount. The difference remains in the column and is carried forward.

⚠️ Critical Distinction — Owner vs Employee

Private use by the owner of a sole trader: car goes in a separate column, WDA is restricted to business proportion. Private use by an employee of a company: NO restriction on capital allowances — the employee simply pays tax on the car benefit-in-kind instead.

2.7 Balancing Adjustments

Balancing adjustments arise when assets are disposed of. There are two types:

  • Balancing allowance: arises when proceeds are LESS than the TWDV. The shortfall = a deduction from profits.
  • Balancing charge: arises when proceeds EXCEED the TWDV. The excess = an addition to profits (i.e. tax is clawed back).
Important: For the main pool and special rate pool, a balancing allowance only arises on cessation of trade (when the pool is closed). If assets are sold mid-trade but other assets remain in the pool, there is no balancing allowance — you just carry the remaining balance forward. However, a balancing charge can arise mid-trade if disposal proceeds exceed the pool balance.
Private use and short life asset columns: These DO get balancing adjustments on disposal (because they are treated as individual columns, not pooled). The TWDV of the column is compared to the proceeds (capped at original cost) when the asset is sold.

2.8 Short Life Assets (SLA)

A short life asset election allows certain assets to be placed in their own column rather than pooled. The benefit is that you get a balancing allowance when the asset is eventually sold, giving faster tax relief if the asset loses value quickly. The rules:

  • Election must be made within 2 years of the end of the accounting period in which the asset was acquired.
  • If the asset is not disposed of within 8 years, the TWDV is transferred into the main pool at the end of the 8th year.
  • Cars cannot be short life assets.

2.9 Structures and Buildings Allowance (SBA)

The SBA provides tax relief for the cost of constructing or renovating commercial buildings. Key rules:

  • Rate: 3% per annum on qualifying expenditure (straight-line, not reducing balance).
  • Available from the date the building is first brought into use — not from when it's acquired.
  • Qualifying expenditure includes: building/construction cost + qualifying renovation costs. Land cost never qualifies.
  • No balancing adjustment on disposal — if the building is sold, the buyer continues claiming SBAs on the original cost.
  • SBA is always calculated separately from plant & machinery capital allowances.
  • SBA is time-apportioned based on when the building is brought into use during the accounting period.
Example — SBA
Ashura purchases and brings into use a commercial building on 1 July 2025 (mid-way through her 9-month period ended 5 April 2026). Cost of structure: £97,600. Land: £30,000.

Qualifying expenditure = £97,600 (land excluded).

Annual SBA = £97,600 × 3% = £2,928.

Period available = 1 July 2025 to 5 April 2026 = 9 months (the full accounting period length).

SBA deduction = £2,928 × 9/12 = £2,196.

Cash Basis of Accounting

3.1 What Is the Cash Basis and Who Can Use It?

The cash basis is a simplified method of computing taxable trading profit. Instead of recognising income when earned and expenses when incurred (the accruals basis), the cash basis only recognises income when actually received and expenses when actually paid.

Since FA2024, the cash basis is the default method for sole traders and unincorporated businesses — i.e. it applies automatically unless the trader elects out. Partnerships can also use the cash basis. Companies cannot.

Entry Condition: Cash basis is available where cash receipts do not exceed the cash basis threshold (given in the tax tables in the exam). A trader who exceeds the limit must switch to the accruals basis. A trader on the cash basis can elect out and use the accruals basis if preferred.

3.3 HMRC Approved Mileage Rates — Cars Under Cash Basis

Under the cash basis, a trader who uses a car for business purposes cannot claim actual car costs (petrol, insurance, repairs) or capital allowances. Instead, they must use HMRC's approved mileage rates:

First 10,000 business miles in the year: 45p per mile
Remaining business miles: 25p per mile
Example — Mileage Under Cash Basis
Idris drives 13,000 business miles in the year. Calculate the deductible car expense under the cash basis.

First 10,000 miles × 45p = £4,500

Remaining 3,000 miles × 25p = £750

Total deductible car expense = £5,250

The car's purchase cost of £9,000 and running costs of £9,340 are completely ignored under the cash basis — replaced entirely by the mileage deduction above.

3.4 HMRC Flat Rate Adjustment for Business Premises

Where a sole trader lives and works at the same premises (e.g. a bed & breakfast owner, a childminder), they may deduct their full household costs from trading profit. But a flat rate private use adjustment must then be added back to account for their personal consumption. These are given in the tax tables in the exam.

Tutorial Note

Under the cash basis, you first deduct the actual household costs in full as a trading expense, and then add back the flat rate as a "private use adjustment". The net deduction is therefore: actual costs paid − flat rate adjustment. The flat rate covers ALL private usage of food, utilities and household goods — you do not need to work out actual private proportions.

3.5 Capital Expenditure Under the Cash Basis

One of the biggest advantages of the cash basis is that capital expenditure on plant and machinery (other than cars) is deductible in full in the year it is paid. This eliminates the need for a capital allowances computation on most assets. However:

  • Cars — never deductible under cash basis (use mileage rates instead).
  • Land and buildings — not deductible under the cash basis.
  • Financial instruments / investments — not deductible.

Partnerships

4.1 The Fundamental Principle

A partnership is not a taxable entity in its own right. Instead:

  • The partnership computes one single tax-adjusted trading profit for the accounting period (using all the rules in Chapters 1–3).
  • That profit is then allocated between the partners according to the profit-sharing agreement.
  • Each partner then declares their own individual share as trading income on their own personal tax return, applying their own personal allowance, tax rates, and any loss relief.

This means partners are taxed as if they were individual sole traders — the partnership itself pays no tax.

4.2 Allocating Profits — The Profit-Sharing Agreement

The profit-sharing agreement typically provides for three elements (deducted in order before splitting the balance):

1

Salaries: Partners may receive a fixed salary before profit is shared (compensating a partner who works more). This is NOT a tax deduction — it is just an allocation mechanism. Salaries are time-apportioned if a change occurs mid-period.

2

Interest on capital: Partners may receive a return on their capital account balance (e.g. 4% × capital balance). This is also just an allocation, not a deductible expense.

3

Balance shared in agreed ratio: The remaining profit (after salaries and interest on capital) is split in the profit-sharing ratio (PSR).

4.3 Changes in the Profit-Sharing Ratio Mid-Period

If the PSR changes during an accounting period, the period is time-apportioned — split into two sub-periods at the date of change. Each sub-period's profit is calculated (total profit × fraction of year) and then allocated using the respective PSR for that sub-period.

Tutorial Note — Why Time-Apportion?

The exam frequently tests the scenario where a salary or PSR changes during a year. The correct technique is to split the year at the change date, compute the profit for each sub-period (as a fraction of the total), apply the relevant PSR to each sub-period, and add the two sub-periods together. The salary must also be time-apportioned (£24,000 × 5/12 = £10,000 for the 5-month period).

4.4 Change in Partnership Membership

When a partner joins or leaves mid-accounting period, the principle is identical — time-apportion the profit at the date of change, allocate each sub-period under the respective PSR, and compute each partner's individual share. Each partner is then taxed on their own share of profits as if they were a sole trader.

4.5 Partners and Loss Relief

If the partnership makes a loss, each partner receives their individual share of the loss (allocated using the same PSR). Each partner can then independently choose which loss relief claim to make — partners in the same partnership do NOT have to choose the same option. The available loss relief claims are the same as for sole traders (see Chapter 5).

Trading Losses for Individuals

5.1 Overview — Why Loss Relief Matters

When a sole trader's tax-adjusted trading profit is negative, the result is a trading loss. HMRC provides several ways to obtain tax relief for this loss. Understanding how each relief works, when it can be claimed, and why one may be better than another is essential for the exam.

5.2 Carry Forward Relief (s83)

This is the default relief — if a trader takes no action, their trading loss is automatically carried forward and set against the first available future trading profits from the same trade. Key points:

  • Can be carried forward indefinitely — no time limit.
  • Can only be set against profits from the same trade — if the trader starts a new business, the old loss cannot be carried into it.
  • No cap applies — the full carried-forward loss is set against the first profits, even if this wastes the personal allowance.
  • A formal claim must be made within 4 years of the tax year in which the loss arose.
📌 When Is Carry Forward Beneficial?

Carry forward gives relief at the lowest tax rate applicable in the future (often basic rate 20%). It is slower than s64 current/prior year relief. However, it is particularly beneficial if the trader has no other income in the current/prior years (so a s64 claim would waste the personal allowance), or if future profits will be taxed at a higher rate.

5.3 Current Year and Prior Year Relief (s64)

A trader can elect to set a trading loss against their total income (including employment income, property income, savings income) rather than waiting for future trading profits. This is faster relief and may be at higher tax rates.

How It Works

  • A current year claim sets the loss against total income for the tax year in which the loss arises (the loss year).
  • A prior year claim sets the loss against total income for the tax year immediately before the loss year.
  • The two claims are independent — a trader can claim one without the other, or both.
  • If both are claimed, you can choose which year to use first (current or prior — whichever gives more tax relief).

The Income Tax Reliefs Cap

A critical restriction applies when claiming s64 relief against income that is not trading profits from the same trade. In such cases, the deduction is capped at the greater of £50,000 or 25% of Adjusted Total Income (ATI) for that year.

Understanding the Cap — Two Part Test

Step 1: Calculate 25% of ATI (ATI = total income before the loss relief claim).

Step 2: Compare to £50,000.

Step 3: The cap is the higher of the two figures.

Step 4: The deduction cannot exceed this cap, UNLESS the loss is being set against trading profits from the same trade (in which case there is no cap).

Example: If ATI = £80,000, then 25% × £80,000 = £20,000. Higher of £20,000 and £50,000 = £50,000. So at most £50,000 of the loss can be relieved against other income in that year.

📝 Worked Example — s64 Current + Prior Year Relief
Dill — Tax year 2025/26. Trading loss = £58,000. Employment income = £361,990. No trading income in 2025/26 (loss year).

Cap = greater of: 25% × £361,990 = £90,498, or £50,000. So cap = £90,498.

Loss = £58,000 — this is below the cap, so the full £58,000 can be relieved.

⚠️ s64 May Waste Personal Allowance

If the loss claim reduces total income below £12,570 (the personal allowance), the personal allowance is partially or fully wasted. The trader still pays no more tax (net income cannot go below zero), but they lose the benefit of the personal allowance. When choosing whether to claim s64, consider if relief would be wasted this way, and compare to the carry-forward option where the PA might be preserved.

5.4 Relief Against Chargeable Gains (s261B)

After making a s64 claim against total income (which is a prerequisite), any remaining loss can be extended to chargeable gains in the same year. This is a two-stage process:

1

First, claim s64 against total income in the same tax year (this is mandatory before gains can be used).

2

Any remaining loss can then be set against net chargeable gains (gains after deducting capital losses but before the Annual Exempt Amount of £3,000).

3

The AEA is applied after the trading loss — this may be wasted if the trading loss absorbs all gains.

📌 Is Relief Against Gains Worthwhile?

Relief against gains saves CGT at 18% (basic rate) or 24% (higher rate). This is less than the 40%/45% income tax saving on income relief. However, if total income has been reduced to zero by a s64 claim and there are still gains to offset, the gains relief may still be the best use of remaining losses. Always show a loss memorandum to track amounts used and remaining.

5.5 Early Years Loss Relief (s72)

This relief is specifically designed for new traders who often incur start-up losses. It allows the loss to be carried back further than the usual one prior year:

  • Available for losses arising in the first four tax years of a trade.
  • The loss is carried back against total income of the three preceding tax years, applied on a FIFO basis (earliest year first).
  • The same cap applies: greater of £50,000 or 25% of ATI.
📝 Worked Example — Early Years Relief
Sabine started trading 1 April 2024. Loss in year ended 31 March 2026 (tax year 2025/26) = £25,000. Sabine was employed until 31 March 2024 earning £45,000 p.a. plus savings income £3,000 p.a. She wants relief as early as possible.

The loss arises in tax year 2025/26. This is year 2 of trading (2024/25 was year 1). Early years relief is available.

Carry back to the 3 preceding tax years on a FIFO basis (earliest first):

  • 2022/23 — total income = £48,000. Set loss here first: relieve £25,000 (loss exhausted).
  • 2023/24 and 2024/25 — no further loss to relieve.

Result: The full £25,000 loss is relieved in 2022/23, saving income tax at up to 40% on employment income from three years ago.

5.6 Terminal Loss Relief (s89)

When a trader permanently ceases to trade, any loss in the final 12 months (the "terminal loss") can be carried back against trading profits of the preceding 3 tax years on a LIFO basis (latest year first).

  • The terminal loss = trading loss in the last 12 months of trading (may span two tax years).
  • Carry back is against trading profits only (not total income, unlike s64).
  • No cap applies to terminal loss relief.
  • Applied LIFO — most recent preceding year first, then going further back.
📝 Worked Example — Terminal Loss Relief
Carol ceased trading on 31 March 2026. Trading loss in year ended 31 March 2026 = £44,000. Prior trading profits: 2024/25 = £19,000, 2023/24 = £28,000, 2022/23 = £12,000. Dividend income £5,000 every year.

Terminal loss = £44,000 (loss in the last 12 months = 1 April 2025–31 March 2026).

Carry back LIFO against trading profits only (dividends excluded):

Tutorial Note

In 2023/24, only £25,000 (not the full £28,000) is offset — the loss was exhausted at £25,000. The amount offset in 2023/24 is therefore £25,000, leaving £3,000 of trading profits remaining taxable in 2023/24. Terminal loss relief only applies against trading profits, so the dividend income in each year is completely unaffected.

5.7 Choosing the Best Loss Relief — Factors to Consider

The Four Factors — Always Discuss These in the Exam

  • 1. Tax rate: Preference for relief at the highest possible rate (40%/45% income tax > 20% income tax > 24% CGT > 18% CGT).
  • 2. Timing: Current/prior year or early years relief gives faster relief than carry forward. Faster relief = time value of money benefit (tax saved sooner).
  • 3. Personal allowance wastage: If a s64 claim reduces net income below £12,570, the personal allowance (or part of it) is wasted. Consider whether it is worth the tax saving.
  • 4. AEA wastage: Setting a loss against gains may waste the £3,000 AEA (Annual Exempt Amount for CGT).

5.8 The Loss Memorandum — Always Show This

In an exam, whenever you are working through a loss question, always prepare a loss memorandum that tracks: total loss, amounts claimed in each year, and the balance remaining. This prevents errors and earns marks even if your computations contain errors elsewhere.

Tax Administration for Individuals

6.1 The Self-Assessment System

Under self-assessment, each individual taxpayer is responsible for calculating their own tax liability and filing their own tax return. HMRC issues a notice to file to taxpayers who need to complete a return (including all sole traders and partners). The taxpayer must then file the return by the due date and pay any tax owed.

6.3 Payments on Account (POAs)

Many self-employed individuals must make advance payments towards their current year's tax liability. These are called payments on account. The system works as follows:

1

Calculate the "relevant amount" for the prior year = income tax liability + Class 4 NIC for that year, MINUS any amounts collected at source (e.g. PAYE, tax deducted at source from savings interest). CGT is excluded from the relevant amount.

2

If the relevant amount exceeds £1,000 AND is more than 20% of the total tax liability (i.e. more than 80% was NOT collected at source), then POAs are required for the following year.

3

Each POA = 50% of the prior year's relevant amount. Two POAs are required each year.

4

The balancing payment (or repayment) is due on 31 January after the tax year — this also includes any CGT owed.

📝 Worked Example — Payments on Account
Sophie Shape — Tax year 2025/26. Prior year (2024/25) income tax = £5,240. Prior year Class 4 NIC = £1,820. PAYE deducted = £0. CGT liability 2025/26 = £5,277. 2025/26 income tax = £6,100. 2025/26 Class 4 NIC = £1,910.

1st POA for 2026/27 (also due 31 Jan 2027) = (£6,100 + £1,910) × 50% = £4,005.

Total payable on 31 January 2027 = £6,227 + £4,005 = £10,232.

Reducing Payments on Account

A taxpayer can apply to reduce their POAs if they expect their current year's liability to be lower than the prior year. However, if the POAs are reduced below what turns out to be the actual liability, interest is charged on the shortfall from the original due date. If the reduction was made fraudulently or negligently, a tax-geared penalty is also charged.

Tutorial Note — What Is a Discovery Assessment?

A discovery assessment is used when HMRC discovers that a taxpayer has underpaid tax after the enquiry window has closed. The time limit depends entirely on the taxpayer's behaviour — not on how large the error is. Deliberate concealment (e.g. hiding income) gives HMRC 20 years to act. Careless errors (e.g. failing to check a return properly) give 6 years. Innocent errors (where the taxpayer was not at fault) allow only 4 years.

6.7 Late Payment Interest and Penalties

Paying tax late is separate from filing late. Two charges may apply:

Interest on Late Payment

Interest is charged automatically from the due date until the date of payment. There is no minimum threshold — any late payment incurs interest. The rate is currently 8.5% per annum.

Interest = Amount unpaid × 8.5% × (number of days late ÷ 365)
📝 Example — Interest Calculation
Rajesh paid his balancing payment of £1,200 on 30 June 2027. It was due 31 January 2027. How long was the payment late?

31 January 2027 to 30 June 2027 = 5 months late.

Interest = £1,200 × 8.5% × 5/12 = £43.

Late Payment Penalties (Surcharges)

In addition to interest, separate penalties apply if the balancing payment remains unpaid:

📝 Example — Interest + Penalty
Rajesh paid £1,200 (balancing payment for 2025/26) on 30 June 2027. Due date was 31 January 2027.

Late by 5 months → more than 30 days but less than 6 months.

Interest = £1,200 × 8.5% × 5/12 = £43.

Penalty (more than 30 days late) = £1,200 × 5% = £60.

Total additional charges = £43 + £60 = £103.

📌 Interest vs Penalty — Remember This Distinction

Interest runs automatically from day one of being late. The 5% penalty only kicks in once the payment is MORE than 30 days late. Always show these as two separate calculations — markers award marks for each.

An unprompted disclosure is where the taxpayer voluntarily comes forward before HMRC has started any enquiry. This attracts a lower minimum penalty — rewarding proactive honesty.
