Chapter map: what this chapter is really about

Syllabus A1–A3, H3–H4 · Main capabilities A and H
In simple wordsAn organisation is only as good as the people who lead it, the culture they create, the ethics they live by and the talent they attract and grow. This chapter is the "people side" of strategy. Strategy is the plan; leadership, culture, ethics and talent decide whether the plan actually happens.

ACCA's own description of capability A says candidates must "set the tone from the top and promote a positive culture". Capability H says they must manage "talent and other business resources". So the examiner wants you to think like a CEO or senior adviser, not a textbook.

The role of effective leadership & key leadership traits

A1a · Leadership in formulating and implementing strategy and change
Step 1 · In simple wordsLeadership is getting people to want to move in a direction. Management is making sure the work gets done efficiently once that direction is set. A good organisation needs both, but strategy and change need leadership most, because people have to let go of the old way.

Leadership vs management (Kotter)

Leaders…Managers…
Set direction: create a vision and strategyPlan and budget: set targets, timetables and allocate resources
Align people: communicate the vision so people understand and commitOrganise and staff: create structures, assign jobs, delegate
Motivate and inspire: energise people to overcome barriersControl and problem-solve: monitor results against plan, correct deviations
Produce changeProduce order and consistency

The role of the leader in strategy

Formulate

Set mission and vision, scan the environment, choose where to compete. Leaders must be open to bad news and challenge.

Communicate

Translate strategy into a simple story every employee understands ("why", "what", "what it means for me").

Implement

Allocate resources, pick the right people, remove blockers, model the new behaviours.

Sustain

Set the tone from the top, embed culture and ethics, hold people accountable, review and adapt.

Key leadership traits effective in strategy and change

TraitWhy it matters for strategy / changeWhat it looks like in practice
VisionPeople follow a clear picture of the future, not a spreadsheet"In three years every patient can book online and be seen within 7 days"
Integrity & honestyTrust is the currency of change; staff won't take risks for a leader they don't trustAdmits mistakes; same message to board, staff and regulators

Step 2 · Real business exampleMicrosoft (2014 onwards). The new CEO changed the culture from "know-it-all" to "learn-it-all", moved the strategy from Windows to cloud, and partnered with former rivals. His traits were vision (cloud-first), empathy (a calmer, more collaborative tone), and decisiveness (writing off the phone business). The strategy only worked because the leadership changed the culture first.

Healthcare example. A hospital CEO launching a new electronic medical record shadows nurses on night shifts, explains why (patient safety, fewer duplicate tests), and empowers "super-users" on each ward. Same system, very different adoption rate from a CEO who just emails a go-live date.
Step 3 · How it appears in the examUsually through a weak leader in the case: a founder-CEO who dominates the board, ignores advice, or a new CEO who announces change by email. The task asks you to "assess the leadership" or "advise on the qualities required" to implement a strategy.
Scenario for the model answerMedLine Clinics is a private chain of 14 clinics. The board has approved a move to telemedicine. The CEO, Mr Hart, founded the business 20 years ago. He announced the change in a two-line email, has not met clinic staff since, and told the board "doctors will do what they're told". Three senior doctors have resigned this month.
Step 4 · Model answer extract (assess the CEO's leadership, 6 marks)

Lack of communication of the vision. A two-line email does not explain why telemedicine matters to patients or how doctors' work will change. Without a clear vision, staff fill the gap with fear, which is likely to explain the three resignations. Mr Hart should hold face-to-face sessions in each clinic explaining the patient benefits and timetable. 1–2

Low emotional intelligence. Telling the board that "doctors will do what they're told" shows he does not recognise doctors' professional autonomy. Doctors are a high-power group whose co-operation is essential. A coercive approach risks further resignations of scarce clinical staff. 1–2

No guiding coalition. He is acting alone. He should appoint a respected clinical lead to champion telemedicine and involve doctors in designing the new consultation process, which increases ownership. 1–2

Step 5 · Examiner expectations
  • Use the case evidence as your headings (email, resignations, quote), not a list of textbook traits.
  • For each point: trait → evidence → consequence → recommendation.
  • Show scepticism: question whether the CEO is the right person to lead the change, politely.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Listing 10 traits with definitions and no case link (scores almost nothing).
  • Confusing leadership with management: "the CEO should set a budget" is a management answer.
  • Being rude about a director: say "the CEO's approach appears to…", not "the CEO is incompetent".
Step 7 · Memory tipLeaders: Direction, Alignment, Motivation (DAM the river of change). Managers: Plan, Organise, Control. Traits for change: "VICE-DREAM": Vision, Integrity, Communication, Emotional intelligence, Decisiveness, Resilience, Empowerment, Adaptability, Market awareness.

Leadership theories you can use as tools

A1a / A2b · Trait, style, contingency, transactional vs transformational
Step 1 · In simple wordsTheories try to answer "what makes a good leader?" Early theories said born with traits. Then behaviour/style (what leaders do). Then it depends on the situation. Modern thinking says strategic change needs transformational leaders who inspire, backed up by transactional discipline.
ApproachKey ideaUse in SBL
Trait theoryLeaders share personal qualities (intelligence, confidence, drive, integrity)Assessing a CEO candidate or a weak leader. Limitation: traits alone don't predict success.
Style theory (e.g. autocratic, democratic, laissez-faire)Leaders can choose a style: tell, sell, consult, joinAutocratic fits a crisis; democratic suits knowledge workers (doctors, engineers); laissez-faire suits experts but risks drift
Contingency / situationalBest style depends on the task, the team's maturity and the leader's powerJustify why a different style is needed now (e.g. turnaround vs steady growth)
Transactional leadershipExchange: rewards for performance, punishment for failure; focus on targets and controlGood for stable operations, compliance, cost control
Transformational leadershipInspires people to go beyond self-interest for a shared visionBest for strategic change, innovation, culture change

The four "I"s of transformational leadership (Bass)

Idealised influence

Role model; "walks the talk"; earns trust and respect.

Inspirational motivation

Clear, exciting vision; optimism; high expectations.

Intellectual stimulation

Encourages questioning, new ideas, innovation.

Individualised consideration

Coaches and supports each person's development.

Step 2 · Real business exampleA government health authority facing a cyber-attack needs a directive, transactional style for the first 72 hours (clear orders, checklists). Six months later, rebuilding its digital strategy needs a transformational style to get clinicians and IT co-designing new systems. Same organisation, different situation, different style: that is contingency thinking.
Step 3 · How it appears in the examRarely asked by name. Instead: "Advise the board on the leadership style most appropriate to manage the proposed change." Use the theories as language inside an applied answer.
Scenario for the model answerA family-owned FMCG company wants to launch a range of healthy snacks to reverse falling sales. The managing director runs the business through weekly sales targets and bonuses. Product ideas from staff are rarely heard because "the MD decides".
Step 4 · Model answer extract (4 marks)

The MD's style is transactional: weekly targets and bonuses drive short-term sales of existing products, which has worked in a stable market. However, launching a new healthy range requires creativity and risk-taking. Because "the MD decides", staff with market insight are unlikely to share ideas, so the company may miss consumer trends. 2

The MD should adopt a more transformational style: set an inspiring vision (e.g. "the healthiest snack brand in the region"), stimulate ideas through cross-functional innovation teams, and recognise innovation, not just sales volume. Transactional controls should remain for cost and quality. 2

Step 5 · Examiner expectationsMatch the style to the situation in the case and justify it. Recognise that a blend is often best.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Saying "transformational is always best": in a crisis or for safety compliance, directive is often right.
  • Describing theories historically (who wrote what in which year). No marks.
Step 7 · Memory tipTransactional = deal ("do X, get Y"). Transformational = dream (4 I's: Influence, Inspire, Intellect, Individual).

Entrepreneurship & intrapreneurship

A1b · Exploit strategic opportunities and innovate successfully
Step 1 · In simple wordsEntrepreneurship: someone spots an opportunity, takes a personal risk and starts a new business. Intrapreneurship: an employee behaves like an entrepreneur inside an existing organisation, using its resources to create a new product, service or process. The company carries the financial risk; the employee brings the energy and idea.
EntrepreneurIntrapreneur
WhereOwn new ventureInside an existing organisation
Risk borne byThe individual (own money, reputation)Mainly the organisation
ResourcesMust raise themUses company brand, cash, customers, data
Main obstacleFinance, market accessBureaucracy, risk-averse culture, "not invented here"
RewardOwnership, profitRecognition, bonus, career, sometimes equity in a spin-off

Typical entrepreneurial traits

Opportunity spotting, risk tolerance, self-belief, persistence, creativity, comfort with uncertainty, ability to sell an idea and acquire resources.

How leaders encourage intrapreneurship

  • Time and space: e.g. dedicated innovation time, hackathons, internal "Dragon's Den" pitches.
  • Seed funding with light approval for small experiments.
  • Tolerate failure: treat failed pilots as learning, not as grounds for punishment.
  • Flatter, faster decisions: fewer sign-offs; a sponsor with authority.
  • Rewards and recognition linked to innovation, not just meeting budget.
  • Governance and risk limits: clear boundaries so innovation doesn't create compliance or patient-safety risk.
Step 2 · Real business examples
  • 3M Post-it notes came from an employee using "15% time" to solve a personal problem (bookmarks falling out of a hymn book).
  • Sony PlayStation began as an internal engineer's project that senior managers initially resisted.
  • Healthcare: a nurse proposes a "virtual ward" where stable patients are monitored at home with wearable devices, freeing beds. The hospital funds a 3-month pilot on one ward; it scales after showing a fall in bed-days.
Step 3 · How it appears in the examA case where a mature organisation is losing to nimble start-ups, or a staff member has an innovative idea that management ignores. Task: "Discuss how the company could encourage intrapreneurship" or "Evaluate the CEO's entrepreneurial qualities".
Scenario for the model answerCityBank is a 60-year-old bank losing younger customers to mobile-only fintechs. A junior IT analyst built a budgeting app prototype at home; her manager rejected it because "it is not in this year's IT plan". Any new project above $10,000 requires five levels of approval.
Step 4 · Model answer extract (6 marks)

Remove approval barriers for small experiments. Five approval levels for $10,000 means ideas die before they are tested, while fintechs can launch features in weeks. CityBank should create an innovation fund where small pilots (e.g. under $50,000) are approved by one sponsor within two weeks. 2

Create channels for ideas. The analyst's prototype was rejected purely because it was not in the plan, so the bank is losing exactly the digital creativity it needs to win younger customers. A quarterly pitch event judged by the digital director would let ideas reach decision makers. 2

Reward and protect intrapreneurs. If the analyst sees no recognition, she may join a fintech. CityBank should give successful intrapreneurs visible recognition and bonuses, and treat failed pilots as learning. Risk and compliance should review pilots early so innovation stays within regulatory limits. 2

Step 5 · Examiner expectationsPractical mechanisms (fund, process, reward, culture) linked to the specific barrier in the case. Balance innovation with control.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Defining entrepreneurship at length and not recommending anything.
  • Ignoring risk: in banks and hospitals, "move fast and break things" is not acceptable.
Step 7 · Memory tipIntrapreneur = "entrepreneur with a company credit card". To unlock them: T-F-R-E-E: Time, Funding, Reward, Error tolerance, Easy approval.

Ethical & professional values underpinning governance

A1c · Applying ethical values in the context of governance and leadership
Step 1 · In simple wordsRules can never cover every situation. Good governance therefore rests on a set of values that leaders should live by. When a case describes a director's behaviour, test it against these values. Each one is a ready-made heading.
ValueMeaningBreach looks like…
FairnessTreat all stakeholders (including minority shareholders, staff, patients) even-handedlyDirectors awarding themselves bonuses while freezing staff pay
Openness / transparencyDisclose information freely unless there is a good reason not toHiding a product safety issue or a major contract loss from shareholders
IndependenceFreedom from conflicts that could bias judgement (key for NEDs and auditors)A "NED" who is the CEO's brother-in-law or former audit partner
Probity / honestyTelling the truth; not misleadingInflating revenue forecasts in a funding bid
ResponsibilityAccepting your duties and the consequences of your decisionsBlaming junior staff for a failure the board ignored
AccountabilityBeing answerable to stakeholders and accepting scrutinyCEO refusing to explain decisions to the audit committee
ReputationProtecting the organisation's standing; it follows from the other valuesBeing linked to bribery or unsafe working conditions in suppliers
JudgementMaking balanced, informed decisions in the organisation's best interestRushing an acquisition without due diligence
IntegrityStraightforward, honest, consistent in word and action (the "umbrella" value)Saying one thing to regulators and another to staff
Step 2 · Real business exampleVolkswagen emissions scandal (2015). Software was used to cheat emissions tests. This breached probity (deceiving regulators), openness (concealment), responsibility (engineers blamed) and caused huge reputation damage and billions in fines. A culture of fear of missing targets made it possible, which shows how values, culture and leadership connect.
Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Evaluate the behaviour of the chairman against the ethical values expected of a director." Use 4–5 of the values as headings that fit the evidence.
Scenario for the model answerThe chairman of a listed hospital group appointed his close friend as a non-executive director without a nomination committee process. He also delayed announcing that a regulator had suspended surgery at one hospital until after he had sold 30% of his own shares.
Step 4 · Model answer extract (6 marks)

Independence and fairness. Appointing a close friend without a nomination committee means the new NED is unlikely to challenge the chairman. This weakens board scrutiny and is unfair to shareholders who rely on independent oversight. The appointment should be reviewed by the nomination committee. 2

Openness and probity. Delaying disclosure of the surgery suspension kept price-sensitive information from the market. 1 Integrity and fairness. Selling 30% of his shares before the announcement suggests he used inside information for personal gain at other shareholders' expense, which may be illegal insider dealing. 1–2 The board should obtain legal advice, report to the regulator and consider whether he can remain as chairman. 1

Step 5 · Examiner expectationsName the value, show the evidence, say why it matters (to which stakeholder), recommend action. Scepticism marks for spotting the timing of the share sale.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Using every value for every fact: choose the best-fitting 1–2.
  • Moralising ("this is very bad") instead of analysing consequences.
Step 7 · Memory tip"FOIP RARJI": Fairness, Openness, Independence, Probity, Responsibility, Accountability, Reputation, Judgement, Integrity. Or picture a judge saying: "Fair, Open, Independent, Honest… and Responsible, Accountable, Reputable, Judicious, with Integrity."

Leadership in defining and managing organisational culture

A2a · Tone from the top, Schein's three levels, Handy's cultures
Step 1 · In simple wordsCulture is "the way we do things around here": the shared beliefs, habits and unwritten rules that guide what people do when no one is watching. Leaders shape culture more than anyone, because staff copy what leaders do, reward and tolerate, not what they say.

Schein's three levels of culture

1. Artefacts (visible)

What you can see and hear: dress code, office layout, logos, language, ceremonies, how meetings run.

2. Espoused values (stated)

What the organisation says it believes: mission statement, code of conduct, "patients first".

3. Basic assumptions (hidden)

Deep, taken-for-granted beliefs that really drive behaviour: "senior doctors are never questioned", "hitting the number is all that matters".

Why this mattersIf espoused values ("we value safety") differ from basic assumptions ("never stop the production line"), staff follow the assumptions. In the exam, spot this gap: it is a classic scepticism point.

How leaders shape culture (the "tone from the top")

  • What leaders pay attention to and measure: if the board only discusses revenue, staff learn only revenue matters.
  • How leaders react to crises and mistakes: blame culture vs learning culture.
  • Role modelling: a CEO who flies first class while cutting staff travel sends a stronger message than any memo.
  • Reward and promotion criteria: who gets promoted tells everyone what is really valued.
  • Recruitment and exit: hiring for values, removing toxic high performers.
  • Stories, symbols and rituals leaders create (see the cultural web).

Handy's four cultures (useful vocabulary)

CulturePictureFeaturesTypical example
Power (Zeus)Spider's webPower at the centre; fast decisions; depends on one personFounder-led family business
Role (Apollo)Greek templeRules, procedures, job descriptions; stable but slowGovernment department, large bank
Task (Athena)Net / matrixTeams form around projects; expertise valued; flexibleConsultancy, tech product teams

Step 2 · Real business exampleBoeing 737 MAX. Investigations described a shift from an engineering-led culture to one dominated by cost and schedule pressure, where safety concerns were not escalated effectively. The espoused value was safety; the lived assumption was "deliver on time and on cost". Two fatal crashes followed.

Positive example: hospitals that adopt a "just culture" (no blame for honest errors, accountability for reckless behaviour) see more incident reporting, which lets them fix problems before patients are harmed.
Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Discuss the importance of the leadership in shaping the culture at X" or a case where staff behaviour (e.g. hiding errors) is clearly caused by leaders' behaviour.
Scenario for the model answerAt Northgate Hospital, the code of conduct says "patient safety is our first priority". In the last year, two nurses who reported medication errors were disciplined, and the CEO's monthly staff email focuses only on waiting-time targets and budget. Incident reports have fallen by 40% although patient complaints have risen.
Step 4 · Model answer extract (5 marks)

There is a gap between Northgate's espoused value ("patient safety first") and its actual assumptions. Disciplining nurses who reported errors teaches staff that reporting is dangerous. 1 The 40% fall in incident reports, while complaints rise, is therefore unlikely to mean fewer errors. It more likely means errors are being hidden, which increases the risk of serious patient harm. 1–2

The CEO's emails focus only on targets and budget, so leadership attention signals that safety is secondary. 1 The CEO should introduce a just-culture policy, publicly thank staff who report incidents, and add safety indicators to every board and staff communication. 1

Step 5 · Examiner expectationsLink leader behaviour → staff behaviour → business risk. Interpret numbers sceptically (fewer reports ≠ fewer errors).
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Recommending "change the culture" without saying how.
  • Recommending only a new code of conduct: codes change espoused values, not assumptions.
Step 7 · Memory tipSchein = an iceberg: Artefacts above water, Values at the waterline, Assumptions deep below (and they sink ships). Staff follow what leaders Do, Reward and Tolerate, not what they say.

The cultural web: analysing culture and recommending change

A2c · Johnson, Scholes & Whittington · A very high-frequency exam model
Step 1 · In simple wordsThe cultural web breaks culture into six visible elements that together hold in place the paradigm: the core set of assumptions at the centre. To change culture, you must change the six elements; otherwise they keep pulling the paradigm back.
StoriesWhat people tell each other: heroes, villains, legends, past successes and failures
SymbolsLogos, offices, titles, cars, language, dress: signs of status and identity
Power structuresWho really has influence (may not match the org chart)
Rituals & routines"The way we do things": meetings, ceremonies, habits, unwritten rules
PARADIGMThe core assumptions everyone takes for granted
Organisational structureFormal reporting lines, hierarchy, centralisation
Control systemsWhat is measured, rewarded and punished: budgets, KPIs, bonuses, appraisals

How to use it in the exam (the three-step method)

1. Current webUse case facts to describe each element today
→
2. ProblemExplain why each element blocks the new strategy
→
3. Desired webRecommend specific changes to each element
Step 2 · Worked example: a government hospital moving to patient-centred, digital care
ElementCurrent (from case)Why it blocks strategyRecommended change
StoriesStaff retell how a consultant "saved" the hospital by refusing a new IT system in 2015Celebrates resistance to technologyPublicise stories of clinicians whose patients benefited from digital tools
SymbolsConsultants have reserved parking and a separate dining room; patients are called "beds"Reinforces hierarchy and depersonalises patientsRemove status symbols; adopt patient-centred language ("Mrs Ali", not "bed 12")
PowerSenior consultants veto changes informally, regardless of managementStrategy fails without their supportMake respected consultants clinical champions with real roles in the programme
StructureRigid departmental silos; each specialty has its own budgetPatient journeys cross departments; nobody owns the whole pathwayCreate cross-department pathway teams (e.g. a diabetes pathway lead)
Control systemsDepartments judged only on staying within budgetNo incentive to improve patient experienceBalanced KPIs: patient satisfaction, waiting time, readmissions, plus budget
Rituals & routinesWard rounds at fixed times for doctors' convenience; paper forms "because we always have"Patients wait; digital records ignoredRedesign routines around patients; celebrate "paper-free ward" milestones
Paradigm"Doctors know best; the hospital serves clinicians"Opposes patient-centred care"We design care around the patient, using data"
Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Using the cultural web, analyse the culture of X and recommend changes needed to support the new strategy" (12–18 marks). Facts are scattered across exhibits: a CEO interview, staff survey, email, newspaper article. Hunt for them.
Scenario for the model answer

SwiftPost is a former state postal service, now partly privatised. The board wants to become an e-commerce parcel delivery company. Facts from the case:

  • Long-serving staff talk proudly about the "golden age" when SwiftPost had a legal monopoly on letters.
  • Senior managers have large private offices on the top floor of head office; they are addressed as "Sir".
  • The postal workers' union must approve any change to shift patterns, and has blocked weekend deliveries twice.
  • There are nine layers of management between a delivery driver and the CEO.
  • Managers' bonuses are based on the number of letters delivered on time; parcels are not measured.
  • Every delivery route has been the same for 15 years; staff finish at 2pm.
Step 4 · Model answer (cultural web, 12 marks; extract shows 5 elements)

Stories

Staff speak proudly of the "golden age" of the letter monopoly. This shows an identity built on protected letters, not competitive parcels. Staff may not believe e-commerce is "real postal work". Recommendation: leaders should create new stories by celebrating early parcel successes, e.g. a team that achieved record same-day deliveries. 2

Symbols

Top-floor private offices and the title "Sir" signal hierarchy and distance from front-line drivers, who are the people e-commerce customers actually see. Recommendation: open-plan management areas, first-name culture and senior managers spending days on delivery rounds. 2

Power structures

The union has real power: it has blocked weekend deliveries twice, and weekend delivery is essential for online shoppers. Recommendation: involve the union early, negotiate a partnership deal (e.g. premium pay for weekend shifts, job security commitments) rather than imposing change. 2–3

Organisational structure

Nine layers of management slow decisions; e-commerce clients like online retailers expect quick responses. Recommendation: delayer to around five levels and create a dedicated parcels business unit with its own leader. 2

Control systems

Bonuses reward letters delivered on time, so managers rationally ignore parcels, the very growth area. Recommendation: introduce parcel KPIs (delivery within promised slot, first-time delivery rate, customer rating) and link bonuses to them. 2

Paradigm (conclusion)

Overall the paradigm is "we are a protected public letter service". It must shift to "we are a competitive, customer-focused delivery business". Changing control systems and union agreements first will give the quickest results, because they change daily behaviour. 1–2 Evaluation

Step 5 · Examiner expectations
  • Every element must be fed by a case fact. If the case has no evidence for one element, say so briefly or skip it.
  • Examiners repeatedly say weak candidates only describe the current culture. Marks come from implications for the strategy and specific changes.
  • Finish with the paradigm shift and priorities: that's where evaluation marks sit.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Putting the same fact under two elements.
  • Confusing rituals (habits/ceremonies) with control systems (measurement and reward).
  • Confusing structure (formal chart) with power (who really influences).
  • Forgetting the "desired web" when the requirement says "recommend".
Step 7 · Memory tip"Silly Sheep Pick Odd Coloured Rugs": Stories, Symbols, Power, Organisational structure, Control systems, Rituals & routines → all around the Paradigm. Method: Now → Problem → Future.

Impact of culture on purpose and strategy

A2d · Culture as enabler or blocker, strategic drift, national culture
Step 1 · In simple words"Culture eats strategy for breakfast." A strategy that fits the culture is implemented almost automatically. A strategy that fights the culture will be quietly resisted, delayed or diluted, even if it is brilliant on paper. Culture also shapes which strategies leaders even consider.

Four ways culture affects strategy

1. Filters what leaders see

The paradigm decides which environmental signals are noticed. A bank proud of its branches may dismiss mobile banking as "a fad".

2. Shapes strategic choice

A risk-averse culture favours market penetration over diversification; an innovative culture favours new products.

3. Determines implementation speed

Aligned culture = fast execution; misaligned = resistance, delays, cost overruns.

4. Defines purpose and ethics

Culture decides whether purpose statements ("patients first") are lived or just posters. It drives ethical behaviour and reputation.

Strategic drift

Strategic drift happens when an organisation's strategy gradually moves away from the needs of its changing environment because the culture keeps repeating what worked before. It has four phases:

1. Incremental changeSmall tweaks; strategy still fits
→
2. DriftEnvironment moves faster; culture resists
→
3. FluxResults fall; confusion; internal arguments
→
4. Transformation or deathRadical change or failure
Step 2 · Real business examples
  • Kodak invented the digital camera but a culture built on film profits and chemical expertise kept it focused on film. Classic strategic drift → bankruptcy (2012).
  • Nokia: a culture of internal fear and over-confidence in hardware meant smartphones' software threat was underestimated.
  • Healthcare: a health authority wants to shift spending from hospitals to preventive community care, but its culture (and status) is built around hospital specialists. Without changing the culture, money keeps flowing to hospitals.

National culture: Hofstede (useful for cross-border cases)

Not named in the syllabus, but helpful when a case involves overseas expansion, a multinational merger or a foreign workforce.

Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Assess how the culture of X could affect the implementation of the proposed acquisition / digital strategy". Post-merger cultural clash is a favourite.
Scenario for the model answerGreenLeaf, a 120-year-old, highly bureaucratic tea company (decisions take months; strict procedures; long-serving staff), plans to acquire QuickBrew, a five-year-old ready-to-drink tea start-up with 40 young staff, flexible hours and decisions made in daily stand-up meetings. Step 4 · Model answer extract (6 marks)

Responsible leadership, the public interest & public value

A3a · Critically evaluate responsible leadership and creating public value
Step 1 · In simple wordsResponsible leadership means leading in a way that creates value not only for owners but for society: staff, customers, communities and the environment, today and in the future. Accountants have a special duty to act in the public interest: the collective well-being of the community that relies on trustworthy financial information and fair organisations.

Features of responsible leadership

  • Considers all stakeholders, not just shareholders (links to Mendelow and CSR from Governance).
  • Takes a long-term view (sustainability, not just this year's bonus).
  • Acts ethically even when no law requires it.
  • Is transparent and accountable for impacts on society and the environment.
  • Builds trust, which lowers the cost of capital, attracts talent and protects the licence to operate.

Public value (Mark Moore's strategic triangle)

Public sector leaders don't maximise profit; they create public value: outcomes citizens value (health, safety, education) delivered efficiently and fairly. A public sector strategy succeeds only if it satisfies all three corners:

Public value

Does it deliver outcomes citizens really value? (e.g. fewer deaths from heart disease)

Legitimacy & support

Does it have political, legal and public support (the "authorising environment": ministers, regulators, citizens, media)?

Operational capacity

Does the organisation have the money, staff, skills and systems to deliver it?

Critical evaluation (the exam says "critically evaluate")

Arguments for responsible leadership

  • Protects reputation and licence to operate
  • Attracts talent and ethical investors (ESG funds)
  • Reduces regulatory and litigation risk
  • Long-term value creation (shared value)

Challenges / criticisms

  • Stakeholder interests conflict: whose interest wins?
  • "Public interest" is vague and hard to measure
  • Short-term shareholder pressure and bonus schemes
  • Risk of greenwashing: talking responsibility without acting
Step 2 · Real business examplePublic value: a city launches free bowel-cancer screening. Public value = earlier diagnosis, lives saved. Legitimacy = backed by the health ministry and public campaigns. Operational capacity = enough labs and specialists. If labs are short-staffed, results take months: public value falls and public support erodes, even though the policy is "right".

Public interest failure: the collapse of Carillion (UK, 2018) hit public services, suppliers, pensioners and jobs. Investigations criticised aggressive accounting, dividend payments while pension deficits grew, and weak challenge by advisers. Leaders served short-term shareholder returns, not the public interest.
Step 3 · How it appears in the examPublic sector cases (hospitals, government agencies) or a private company providing public services. "Evaluate whether the CEO has acted in the public interest" or "Discuss how the new strategy creates public value".
Scenario for the model answerA government health department plans to close three small rural clinics and build one large "super clinic" in the regional city. The plan saves $6m a year and the new clinic will offer specialist scans. Rural residents (many elderly, without cars) would travel up to 70km. Local politicians and the media oppose the plan. The department has not yet hired the 30 extra staff the super clinic needs.
Step 4 · Model answer extract (Moore's triangle, 8 marks)

Public value. The super clinic offers specialist scans not currently available locally, which could improve early diagnosis. The $6m annual saving could be reinvested in services. 1 However, elderly residents without cars may stop attending appointments when travel rises to 70km, so health outcomes for the most vulnerable could get worse. Public value is therefore uncertain unless transport is provided. 2

Legitimacy and support. Opposition from local politicians and the media threatens the authorising environment. Ministers may reverse the plan under pressure, wasting money already spent. 1–2 The department should consult residents and present evidence on clinical outcomes. 1

Operational capacity. 30 extra staff are not yet hired. In a period of health-worker shortages, opening without them would create long waits, destroying the value the project promised. 1

Conclusion. The plan is not yet ready. The department should keep one rural clinic open as a satellite or fund a free shuttle service, and recruit staff before closing any clinic. 1–2 Evaluation

Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Judging a public body only on cost savings.
  • Not reaching a conclusion when asked to "evaluate".

Conflicts of interest, ethical conflicts & how to resolve them

A3c · Reasons for conflicts; recommend resolutions · Kohlberg, Tucker, AAA model, whistleblowing
Step 1 · In simple wordsA conflict of interest exists when a person's private interest (money, family, friendship, career) could influence a decision they make for the organisation. An ethical conflict / dilemma is when two duties or values pull in opposite directions (e.g. loyalty to employer vs duty to the public). The skill is to resolve them through a structured, transparent process.

Why conflicts arise in organisations

Personal interests

Shareholdings, family members in suppliers, gifts, side businesses, job offers from contractors.

Incentives & targets

Bonuses tied to short-term profit; unrealistic budgets; career pressure.

Competing stakeholder claims

Cost savings vs patient safety; shareholders vs employees; profit vs environment.

Dual roles

A director sitting on two boards; a government official who is also a business owner.

Culture

"Everyone does it" norms; obedience to authority; gift-giving customs.

Unclear policies

No register of interests, no gifts policy, no guidance on what to do.

A practical resolution process (the one examiners like)

1. FactsGather relevant facts
→
2. IssuesIdentify the ethical issues and affected parties
→
3. PrinciplesWhich fundamental principles are at stake?
→
4. ProceduresFollow internal policies (register, gifts policy)
→
5. OptionsConsider alternatives and consequences
→
6. EscalateLine manager → audit committee → external

Practical resolutions for conflicts of interest

  • Declare the interest in a register of interests; update it regularly.
  • Recuse: step out of the decision and leave the room for that agenda item.
  • Independent decision by others (e.g. NEDs, procurement panel).
  • Divest the interest (sell the shares) if it is ongoing.
  • Policies: gifts and hospitality limits, procurement rules, related-party approval.

Ethical decision tools

Tucker's five questions: is the decision…

Profitable?

Legal?

Fair?

Right?

…and Sustainable / environmentally sound?

A decision should pass all five. Profitable but unfair or unsustainable = reject or modify.

American Accounting Association (AAA) seven-step model

  1. What are the facts of the case?
  2. What are the ethical issues?
  3. What are the norms, principles and values related to the case?
  4. What are the alternative courses of action?
  5. What is the best course of action consistent with the norms, principles and values?
  6. What are the consequences of each possible course of action?
  7. What is the decision?

Kohlberg's levels of moral development (to "assess" someone's behaviour)

LevelReasoningSounds like…
Pre-conventionalSelf-interest: avoid punishment, gain reward"I'll do it if I won't get caught" / "What's in it for me?"
ConventionalConform to group norms, laws and expectations"Everyone in the industry does it" / "It's legal, so it's fine"
Post-conventionalUniversal ethical principles, even beyond the law"It's legal, but it would harm patients, so we won't do it"

Whistleblowing

Reporting wrongdoing, ideally internally first (line manager → ethics officer → audit committee chair), then externally (regulator, professional body) if internal routes fail or are compromised. Good organisations provide confidential hotlines, protect whistleblowers from retaliation and investigate every report.

Step 2 · Real business exampleA hospital procurement manager's brother owns a company bidding to supply surgical gloves at the lowest price. Even if the price is best, the public could suspect favouritism. Resolution: the manager declares the relationship, leaves the evaluation panel, and an independent panel scores the bids using pre-set criteria; the decision and declaration are documented. Fair outcome, and visibly fair.

Reducing and combating fraud, bribery & corruption

A3e · Best practice to increase public confidence and trust
Step 1 · In simple wordsFraud = dishonestly deceiving someone to gain an advantage or cause a loss (e.g. fake invoices, manipulated accounts). Bribery = offering, giving, requesting or accepting something of value to improperly influence a decision. Corruption = abuse of entrusted power for private gain (wider; includes bribery, nepotism, embezzlement). All three destroy trust, so leaders must prevent, detect and respond.

The fraud triangle: why people commit fraud

Pressure / motive

Personal debt, unrealistic targets, bonus pressure, fear of job loss.

Opportunity

Weak controls, no segregation of duties, poor oversight, override by senior managers.

Rationalisation

"I'm underpaid", "I'll pay it back", "everyone does it", "it's for the good of the company".

The organisation controls mostly the opportunity, and it can influence pressure (realistic targets) and rationalisation (ethical culture).

Types of fraud to recognise

  • Misappropriation of assets: theft of cash or stock, payroll "ghost employees", fake suppliers, expense claim fraud.
  • Financial statement fraud: overstated revenue, hidden liabilities, manipulated provisions (often by senior management).
  • Procurement fraud: bid-rigging, kickbacks, inflated invoices.
  • Cyber-enabled fraud: fake supplier bank-detail changes, CEO impersonation emails.

Best practice framework

StageActions
PreventEthical tone from the top; anti-fraud and anti-bribery policy; code of conduct; segregation of duties; authorisation limits; vetting of staff and suppliers; realistic targets; training; gifts and hospitality register
DetectInternal audit; data analytics (duplicate payments, suppliers sharing bank accounts with staff); surprise checks; reconciliations; whistleblowing hotline; monitoring red flags (lifestyle beyond salary, staff never taking leave)
RespondFraud response plan; investigate promptly and independently; preserve evidence; disciplinary and legal action; recover losses; report to authorities; fix the control weakness; communicate lessons learned

Anti-bribery: the UK Bribery Act "adequate procedures" (six principles)

The UK Bribery Act 2010 is widely used as best practice and has wide reach: a company can be guilty of failing to prevent bribery by anyone acting for it (employees, agents, subsidiaries), anywhere. The only defence is having adequate procedures, guided by six principles:

1. Proportionate procedures

Controls proportionate to the bribery risks and the size and complexity of the business.

2. Top-level commitment

Board and senior management lead a culture where bribery is never acceptable.

3. Risk assessment

Regularly assess exposure: countries, sectors, transactions, business partners.

4. Due diligence

Check agents, intermediaries, joint-venture partners and suppliers.

5. Communication (incl. training)

Policies explained and training given to staff and partners.

6. Monitoring and review

Review and improve procedures over time.

Facilitation payments (small "grease" payments to speed up routine government actions, e.g. customs clearance) are illegal under the UK Act. Reasonable, proportionate hospitality is allowed, but lavish gifts intended to influence decisions are bribes.

Step 2 · Real business examples
  • Rolls-Royce paid around £671m (2017) to settle bribery investigations involving intermediaries in several countries: an example of the "agents" risk that due diligence is meant to address.
  • Public sector: a government hospital found two "suppliers" registered at the home address of a procurement clerk. Data analytics matching supplier and employee addresses and bank accounts detected it, a cheap and powerful control.

Step 4 · Model answer extract (10 marks)

  • Talent pool: a group of people being prepared for a family of roles (e.g. future ward managers), rather than one named successor per job.
  • 9-box grid: plots staff on performance (low–high) vs potential (low–high) to decide who to develop, retain, or manage out.
  • Build, buy or borrow: develop internally, recruit externally, or use contractors and partners.
  • Engagement drivers: meaningful work, good line managers, recognition, fair pay, development, flexibility, wellbeing.

Barriers and risks

  • Talent management seen as an HR project, not owned by line leaders.
  • Short-term budget cuts to training (the first thing cut, the last thing to recover).
  • Developed staff poached by competitors (so retention must go with development).
  • Favouritism in identifying "high potentials" damages trust.
  • Public sector pay scales limit ability to compete for scarce skills, so non-pay benefits matter.
Step 2 · Real business exampleHealthcare nurse shortage. Many health systems have struggled with nurse vacancies and high reliance on expensive agency staff. A talent-management response: (1) workforce plan linked to new services; (2) international recruitment with proper onboarding (attract); (3) clinical career ladders so nurses can progress without leaving bedside care (develop/retain); (4) flexible rostering apps (engage); (5) "stay interviews" asking nurses what would keep them. Result: lower agency spend and better continuity of care, which directly supports the strategy of improving quality within budget.
Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Discuss how effective talent management could support the company's new strategy". The case usually shows high staff turnover, a skills gap for a new strategy, a key person about to retire, or exit-interview comments.

Build: develop internal talent

POPIT: the four-view model for organisational improvement

H3b · People, Organisation, Processes, Information Technology · also used in your Change chapter
Step 1 · In simple wordsMost change projects fail because they fix only one thing, usually technology. POPIT says any improvement must look at four views together: the People (skills, motivation), the Organisation (structure, culture, roles), the Processes (how work flows), and the IT (systems and data). Change one without the others and the benefit is lost.

People

  • Skills and competences
  • Motivation and engagement
  • Training needs
  • Recruitment, reward
  • Attitudes to change

Organisation

  • Structure, reporting lines
  • Roles and responsibilities
  • Culture and management style
  • Communication between departments

Processes

  • How work flows end-to-end
  • Duplication, delays, bottlenecks
  • Hand-offs between teams
  • Controls

IT

  • Systems supporting processes
  • Integration and data quality
  • Automation opportunities
  • Access, usability

How to use POPIT for talent and improvement questions

ViewDiagnostic questions to ask of the case
PeopleDo staff have the skills for the new strategy? Are they motivated? Is turnover high? Do they resist change?
OrganisationDoes the structure create silos? Is anyone accountable for the whole customer journey? Does the culture support the change?
ProcessesWhere are the delays, duplications and errors? Which steps add no value for the customer?
ITAre systems integrated or do staff re-key data? Could automation, self-service or analytics help?
Step 2 · Real business exampleA hospital installs a new e-prescribing system (IT) but sees no fall in medication errors. A POPIT review finds: People: junior doctors were never trained and bypass it; Organisation: pharmacy and wards report to different directors who don't co-ordinate; Processes: paper charts are still used in parallel "just in case". Fixing the other three views is what finally delivers the IT benefit.
Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Using the POPIT model, analyse the opportunities for improving the customer complaints process" (8–12 marks). The case shows a failing process with symptoms in all four views.
Scenario for the model answerA government licensing agency takes an average of 45 days to issue business licences (target: 10). Facts: staff have not been trained since a new case-management system was introduced two years ago; three departments (intake, inspection, approval) each check the same documents; nobody owns the end-to-end licence process; applicants submit paper forms, which are scanned and re-typed into the system; the inspection team's system does not connect to the approval team's system, so files are emailed.
Step 4 · Model answer (POPIT, 8 marks)

People. Staff have had no training on the new system in two years, so they are unlikely to use its features, causing slow, manual workarounds. A training programme with "super-users" in each team would increase productivity quickly and cheaply. 2

Organisation. No one owns the end-to-end process, so each department optimises its own step and nobody is accountable for the 45-day total. The agency should appoint a licence process owner with a 10-day target and authority across all three departments. 2

Processes. The same documents are checked three times, which adds days but little value. A single, risk-based document check at intake, with inspection only where risk is high, would remove duplication. 2

IT. Paper forms are re-typed and the inspection and approval systems are not connected, so files are emailed. An online application portal feeding directly into one integrated case-management system would eliminate re-keying errors and allow applicants to track progress, improving public satisfaction. 2

Step 5 · Examiner expectationsUse all four views, each with case facts and a recommendation. Show that they are interrelated: IT alone won't fix it.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Putting structure issues under "People" (structure is Organisation).
  • Turning every answer into "buy a new system".
Step 7 · Memory tipPOPIT: "Pop the balloon": if one of the four sides is weak, the whole improvement bursts. People = who, Organisation = structure, Processes = how, IT = with what.

The Baldrige model for performance excellence

H4a · Apply the Baldrige model for world-class organisations
Step 1 · In simple wordsThe Baldrige Excellence Framework (from the US Malcolm Baldrige National Quality Award) is a "health check" for the whole organisation. It asks: is our leadership driving the right strategy, focused on customers, using data, with an engaged workforce and efficient operations, and are we getting results? It has versions for business, education and healthcare, and is used by organisations of every size.

Structure of the framework

Organisational profile (the starting point: environment, relationships, strategic situation) → six process categories → results, all linked by a systems view.

#CategoryKey questionsHospital example
1LeadershipHow do senior leaders set vision and values, communicate, and govern the organisation ethically and in line with societal responsibilities?CEO and board publish a patient-safety vision; board reviews safety data monthly
2StrategyHow do you develop strategic objectives and action plans, deploy them and measure progress?3-year plan to cut waiting times; each department has action plans
3Customers (patients in healthcare)How do you listen to customers, build relationships and use their voice to improve?Patient feedback kiosks; patient advisory panel on service redesign
4Measurement, analysis & knowledge managementHow do you select, gather, analyse and use data and knowledge to improve performance?Real-time dashboard of bed occupancy, infection rates, readmissions
5WorkforceHow do you build an effective, supportive environment, and engage and develop staff?Staff engagement survey, wellbeing programme, career ladders
6OperationsHow do you design, manage and improve key work processes and ensure operational effectiveness?Lean redesign of the discharge process; supply chain resilience
7ResultsWhat are your results for products/services, customers, workforce, leadership & governance, and finance & market?Mortality, patient satisfaction, staff turnover, compliance, budget results
How the categories linkCategories 1–3 are the leadership triad (leadership, strategy, customers). Categories 5–7 are the results triad (workforce, operations, results). Category 4 (measurement, analysis, knowledge) is the foundation under all of them. Mentioning this linkage shows a systems perspective.

Core values and concepts (underpin the framework)

Systems perspective

Manage the organisation as a whole, with linked parts.

Visionary leadership

Leaders set direction and model values.

Customer-focused excellence

Customers judge performance and quality.

Valuing people

Engage, develop and respect the workforce and partners.

Agility, resilience & organisational learning

Respond quickly to change; continuously learn and improve.

Focus on success & innovation

Short- and long-term success; take intelligent risks.

Management by fact

Decisions based on data and analysis, not opinion.

Societal contributions

Public health, environment, community well-being.

Ethics, transparency & delivering value

Ethical behaviour; balanced value for all stakeholders; results.

How Baldrige helps an organisation (benefits for exam answers)

  • Holistic self-assessment: identifies strengths and gaps across the whole organisation, not just finance.
  • Alignment: links leadership, strategy, people and processes to results.
  • Benchmarking: compares the organisation with world-class performers.
  • Focus on results balanced across stakeholders (patients, staff, community, finances).
  • Continuous improvement culture through regular reassessment.

Limitations: time- and resource-intensive; risk of becoming a paperwork exercise; needs genuine leadership commitment; benefits take years to show.

Step 2 · Real business exampleMany US hospitals and health systems have won the Baldrige Award (healthcare category). A typical winner's story: the CEO made the framework the organisation's management system; every department tracked a small set of linked measures; staff engagement and patient-safety results improved together. The lesson for SBL: excellence comes from linking leadership, people, processes and data, not from one initiative.
Step 3 · How it appears in the exam"Advise the board how the Baldrige model could be used to improve the performance of the hospital" or "Using the Baldrige categories, evaluate the organisation's current approach" (10–15 marks). Pick the categories with the strongest case evidence.
Scenario for the model answer

Riverside Health is a government-funded hospital with poor results. Facts:

  • The board meets quarterly and discusses only the financial report.
  • The hospital has a 5-year strategy document, but department heads say they have never seen it.
  • Patient complaints have doubled in two years; there is no process to analyse them.
  • Data on infections, waiting times and readmissions are held in separate spreadsheets and take weeks to compile.
  • Nurse turnover is 22%; the staff survey shows only 31% feel "listened to".
  • Theatre (operating room) utilisation is 58% because of late starts and cancellations.
Step 4 · Model answer (Baldrige, 12 marks; extract)

Leadership

The board discusses only finance, so it has no oversight of clinical quality or patient safety, the hospital's core purpose. Baldrige would require leaders to set a clear vision and review balanced performance. The board should add a quality and safety report to every meeting and meet monthly. 2

Strategy

A strategy that department heads have never seen cannot be deployed. Each department should translate the 5-year strategy into annual action plans with measurable objectives, reviewed quarterly. 2

Customers (patients)

Complaints have doubled with no analysis, so the hospital is ignoring its best source of improvement ideas. It should categorise complaints, identify root causes and involve a patient panel in redesigning the worst services. 2

Measurement, analysis and knowledge management

Key data take weeks to compile from separate spreadsheets, so decisions are based on out-of-date information. An integrated dashboard would support "management by fact" and underpin every other category. 2

Workforce

22% nurse turnover and only 31% feeling listened to suggests low engagement, which increases agency costs and risks to care. Regular listening forums, career development and recognition schemes are needed. 2

Operations

58% theatre utilisation is a major waste of an expensive resource and lengthens waiting lists. Analysing the causes of late starts (e.g. patient preparation, staff availability) and redesigning the theatre process could raise utilisation significantly. 1

Results

Riverside should track balanced results (clinical outcomes, patient satisfaction, staff engagement, financial) and benchmark them against top-performing hospitals, repeating the Baldrige self-assessment annually. 1 Evaluation

Step 5 · Examiner expectationsApply the categories to case facts; do not list the core values unless asked. Show links (e.g. poor data → poor leadership oversight → poor results).
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Reciting the seven categories with definitions and no application.
  • Confusing Baldrige with the balanced scorecard: Baldrige is a whole-organisation excellence framework including leadership and processes; the BSC is a performance measurement tool.

Critical success factors (CSFs), KPIs & empowering the organisation

H4b · Empower an organisation to reach its goals by focusing on its CSFs
Step 1 · In simple wordsCritical success factors are the few things an organisation must get right to succeed with its strategy. If any CSF fails, the strategy fails. KPIs are the measures that tell you whether you are getting each CSF right. Focusing everyone on a small number of CSFs empowers them: staff know what matters most and can make decisions without being told.

Threshold vs critical (useful distinction)

Threshold resources and competences are needed just to compete (a hospital must be licensed). Critical / distinctive factors make you better than rivals (a hospital known for the lowest surgical infection rate). CSFs should focus on what drives success, not just survival.

Good KPIs are SMART

Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant (to the CSF), Time-bound. Use a mix of leading indicators (predict future results, e.g. staff training hours) and lagging indicators (show past results, e.g. profit).

How CSFs empower an organisation

  • Focus: scarce resources go to the few things that matter most.
  • Clarity and alignment: every team can see how its work contributes.
  • Delegated decisions: staff can act within clear priorities without escalating.
  • Accountability: each CSF has an owner and KPIs.
  • Early warning: KPIs reveal problems before results fail.
  • Competitiveness: resources concentrate on what customers value most.
Step 2 · Real business exampleA low-cost airline. CSFs: quick aircraft turnaround, high seat occupancy, low cost per seat, on-time performance. KPIs: average turnaround time (target 25 min), load factor (target 90%+), cost per available seat-km, % flights on time. Ground staff are empowered to make decisions that protect the 25-minute turnaround, because everyone knows it is critical.

▶ Model answer, Requirement 1: Cultural web (14 marks). Click to reveal after your attempt

Briefing paper. To: AWCHS Board. From: Adviser. Subject: Culture, ethics and talent for "Care Closer to Home"

1. Cultural web analysis

Paradigm. The core assumption at AWCHS appears to be "care is delivered face-to-face, in our clinic, under doctors' control". This is the opposite of a strategy based on remote care and nurse-led programmes, so the culture is a major barrier. With only 5% remote consultations today, reaching 40% in three years is a transformational change. 2

Stories. Staff celebrate Dr Saeed "winning" against online booking in 2019. The story teaches that resisting technology works and is admired. Unless replaced, it will encourage resistance to video consultations. Change: share stories of clinicians and patients benefiting from remote care, e.g. an elderly diabetic patient avoiding travel, through internal newsletters and the CEO's visits. 2

Symbols. The CEO's only visit is for long-service awards, which symbolises loyalty and tenure rather than innovation. Learning strategy "from the newspaper" signals distance between leadership and staff. Change: the CEO should visit clinics regularly, and recognition awards should celebrate innovation and patient outcomes. 2

Power structures. Doctors hold power in each clinic and nurses cannot even order routine tests. The chronic-disease programme relies on nurse specialists working independently, so doctor control will block it. Change: give nurse specialists defined clinical authority under agreed protocols, and involve respected doctors as champions so they are not threatened. 2

Organisational structure. Each clinic runs separately with its own appointment system, so patients re-register when they move. This is incompatible with a network-wide video service. Change: create a central virtual-care hub and a network-wide chronic-disease service led by a nurse consultant. 2

Control systems. Clinic managers' bonuses depend only on budget. Investment in remote care or nurse training may look like overspending, so managers will avoid it. Change: balanced KPIs (% remote consultations, chronic-disease outcomes such as HbA1c control, patient satisfaction) plus budget. 2

Rituals and routines. Separate appointment systems and long-service ceremonies reinforce the clinic-centred, tenure-based way of working. Change: a single booking system with a "remote-first" default for routine appointments, and team huddles reviewing remote-care progress. 1

Priority. Changing control systems and nurse authority will have the fastest effect on behaviour. Stories and symbols will follow as leaders visibly role-model the change. 1 Analysis

▶ Model answer, Requirement 2: Ethical issues and Ms Haddad's actions (10 marks)

2. Ethical issues in Exhibit 3

Misstatement of results (integrity). Dating the invoice next year to hide part of a $1.4m overspend would deliberately mislead the regional health authority, AWCHS's funder, at its year-end review. This breaches integrity and professional behaviour and may breach public financial regulations. If Ms Haddad complies, she is part of the deception. 2

Conflict of interest (objectivity). The Director of Finance's nephew is TeleMedix's account manager, and TeleMedix is the "preferred" bidder. This raises doubt over whether the procurement is fair; a reasonable and informed third party would question it. The relationship should have been declared and the director should not influence the decision. 2

Hospitality as a potential bribe. Free flights and hotels to Singapore for the evaluation panel during a live tender is significant hospitality from a bidder. It could improperly influence the award, creating a self-interest threat and possible breach of anti-bribery law. Public sector staff are held to particularly high standards because they spend public money. 2

Intimidation threat to Ms Haddad. The request comes from a more senior director, so she may fear consequences if she refuses. 1

Recommended actions for Ms Haddad

  • Refuse to request a misdated invoice, and decline the Singapore hospitality on behalf of the panel. 1
  • Keep the email and a written record of her response. ½
  • Raise the issues with the CEO and the chair of the audit and risk committee, recommending an independent review of the TeleMedix procurement, the director's removal from it, and a check that other bids are evaluated fairly. 1
  • If internal escalation fails, use the whistleblowing procedure or report to the regional health authority. She can seek confidential advice from ACCA's ethics helpline. ½

▶ Model answer, Requirement 3: Talent management for the chronic-disease programme (10 marks)

3. Talent management

Scale of the capability gap. Only 3 of 210 nurses (about 1.4%) hold a diabetes specialist qualification, yet the programme is to be nurse-led across 22 clinics. Even one specialist per clinic means at least 19 more are needed, before allowing for heart disease and 25% turnover. Talent is the critical constraint on the strategy. 2 Commercial acumen

Develop internally (build). AWCHS should create a funded specialist pathway: select nurses with interest and potential, sponsor accredited diabetes and cardiac courses, and pair them with the 3 existing specialists as mentors. Growing its own specialists is likely cheaper than recruiting from a scarce market, and it builds loyalty. 2

Retain. Nurses leave for private hospitals that fund specialist training, so offering this training directly removes the main stated reason for leaving. A modest retention agreement (e.g. two years' service after a sponsored qualification) protects the investment. Cutting 25% turnover also reduces recruitment and agency costs, helping keep the budget flat. 2

Deploy and empower. Specialists will only stay if they can use their skills. AWCHS must change the rule requiring a doctor's signature for routine tests, giving specialists protocol-based authority (links to the cultural web). A clinical career ladder (nurse → specialist nurse → nurse consultant) gives progression without leaving patient care. 2

Attract and plan. A workforce plan should set annual targets for specialist numbers. AWCHS's employer brand ("we invest in your specialist career", work–life balance from remote clinics) can attract nurses from outside. 1

Measure. KPIs: number of qualified specialists, nurse turnover, internal promotions, chronic-disease patient outcomes. Review them quarterly at board level. 1

Self-test: 15 questions

Answer in your head first, then click to reveal
1. State three differences between leadership and management (Kotter).
Leaders set direction, managers plan and budget; leaders align people, managers organise and staff; leaders motivate and inspire, managers control and problem-solve. Leadership produces change; management produces order.
2. Why is a strongly transactional style weak for launching a new product range?
It rewards hitting existing targets, so staff avoid risk and don't share ideas. Innovation needs inspiration and intellectual stimulation (transformational), with transactional controls kept for cost and quality.
3. Give two practical ways to encourage intrapreneurship in a bureaucratic organisation.
Any two of: an innovation fund with fast single-sponsor approval for small pilots; pitch events or innovation time; rewards and recognition for innovation; tolerating failed pilots as learning; early risk and compliance review so pilots stay within limits.
4. A chairman sells shares just before bad news is announced. Which governance values are breached?
Integrity, probity/honesty, fairness (to other shareholders) and openness (delayed disclosure). It may also be illegal insider dealing. Reputation is damaged as a consequence.
5. What are Schein's three levels and which one really drives behaviour?
Artefacts, espoused values, basic assumptions. Basic assumptions drive behaviour. If they conflict with espoused values, staff follow the assumptions.
6. Name the six elements of the cultural web and what sits in the middle.
Stories, symbols, power structures, organisational structure, control systems, rituals and routines. The paradigm sits in the middle.
7. What is the difference between "power structures" and "organisational structure" in the cultural web?
Organisational structure is the formal chart and reporting lines. Power structures are who really has influence, e.g. senior consultants or a union who can block change regardless of the chart.
8. What is strategic drift, and give a real example.
When strategy gradually falls out of line with a changing environment because the culture keeps repeating past success. Phases: incremental, drift, flux, transformation or death. Example: Kodak and digital photography.
9. When is a directive or coercive change style appropriate, and what is the risk?
In a genuine crisis with little time, where survival is at stake and the leader has power. Risk: low commitment, resentment, loss of skilled staff, and the change may not stick.
10. State the three corners of Moore's strategic triangle.
Public value; legitimacy and support (the authorising environment); operational capacity.
11. List the five IESBA fundamental principles and the five threats.
Principles: integrity, objectivity, professional competence and due care, confidentiality, professional behaviour. Threats: self-interest, self-review, advocacy, familiarity, intimidation.
12. A CEO tells the financial controller "change the numbers or you're fired". Which threat, and what is the correct escalation?
Intimidation, plus self-interest (job security). Refuse and record; raise with the audit committee chair or board; use whistleblowing or external advice (ACCA helpline, legal) if internal routes fail; resign as a last resort.
13. What are the six principles of "adequate procedures" under the UK Bribery Act?
Proportionate procedures, top-level commitment, risk assessment, due diligence, communication (including training), monitoring and review.
14. Name the seven Baldrige categories. Which one is the foundation?
Leadership; strategy; customers; measurement, analysis and knowledge management; workforce; operations; results. Measurement, analysis and knowledge management is the foundation.
15. For a hospital whose strategy is "fastest emergency care in the region", give one CSF and a matching KPI.
CSF: rapid triage and treatment in the emergency department. KPI: % of patients seen by a clinician within 15 minutes of arrival (or % admitted or discharged within 4 hours), with a target such as 95%.
