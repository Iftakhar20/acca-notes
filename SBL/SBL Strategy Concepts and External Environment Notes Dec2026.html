1C1a · assumed knowledge

Strategy Basics — The Vocabulary You Must Know

The SBL examiner assumes you know these terms. They come up in almost every exam, so learn them properly first.

1.1 What is "strategy"?

📖 In simple words

Strategy is the long-term direction of an organisation: where it wants to go, and the big choices about how to get there.

A widely used definition (Johnson, Scholes & Whittington) describes strategy as the long-term direction of an organisation. Older editions add that it achieves advantage by configuring resources in a changing environment to meet stakeholder expectations.

Break that definition into five parts, because each one is something you can comment on in an exam:

Part of the definitionMeaningQuestion to ask in a case
Long-term directionYears ahead, not this monthWhere will this organisation be in 5 years?
AdvantageDoing better than rivals (or, in the public sector, delivering more value for money)Why would customers or citizens choose it?
ResourcesMoney, people, buildings, brands, data, know-howDoes it have what the plan needs?
Changing environmentThe outside world keeps movingWhat is changing around it?
Stakeholder expectationsOwners, staff, customers, government, communityWho must be satisfied?
🏢 Example — strategy vs everyday decisions

SunnyFoods makes breakfast cereals.

  • Strategic decision: "Over the next 5 years we will move into healthy snack bars and sell in three new countries." This is long-term, big money, affects the whole company and is hard to reverse.
  • Operational decision: "Run the packing line on Saturday to meet this week's order." This is short-term, small, routine and easy to reverse.

1.2 Characteristics of strategic decisions

A strategic decision usually has most of these features:

  1. Long-term: its effects last years.
  2. Scope: it defines what the organisation does and does not do (which products, which markets).
  3. Large resources: it commits significant money and people.
  4. Complex and uncertain: the future is unknown and there are many variables.
  5. Affects the whole organisation, and often changes many operational decisions below it.
  6. Hard to reverse: building a factory can't be undone next month.
  7. Driven by stakeholders' values and expectations.

1.3 Mission, vision, values, objectives

TermSimple meaningExample: "CareFirst Hospital Group" (fictional)
MissionWhy we exist, our core purpose today"To provide safe, affordable healthcare to every family in our region."
VisionWhat we want to become in the future"To be the region's most trusted healthcare provider by 2032."
ValuesThe principles that guide how we behavePatient safety first, compassion, integrity, teamwork
Goals / objectivesMeasurable targets that make the mission real"Reduce average emergency waiting time from 4 hours to 2 hours by 2028."
📖 SMART objectives

Good objectives are Specific, Measurable, Achievable, Relevant and Time-bound.

❌ "Improve patient satisfaction." → ✅ "Increase the patient satisfaction score from 72% to 85% by December 2028."

1.4 Three levels of strategy

LevelQuestion it answersWho decidesExample (SunnyFoods)
Corporate-levelWhich businesses and markets should we be in at all?Board / group CEOEnter snack bars; sell the frozen-food division
Business-level (competitive)How do we compete in each market?Divisional managersCereals compete on low price; snack bars compete on health and premium quality
Operational (functional)How does each department support the strategy?Functional managersMarketing launches a fitness campaign; operations sources organic oats

1.5 How strategy is formed — deliberate vs emergent

ApproachMeaningStrengthsWeaknesses
Deliberate (rational / planned)Analyse → choose → implement, using a formal plan, often for 3–5 yearsClear direction, coordination, resources allocated logicallySlow, can become outdated, ignores good ideas from below
EmergentStrategy develops over time from patterns of actions and learning, often bottom-upFlexible, responds to change, uses front-line knowledgeCan lack direction, harder to control and fund
Incremental ("logical incrementalism")Small, step-by-step adjustments, testing as you goLow risk, learns from experienceMay be too slow when big change is needed (this leads to strategic drift, section 7)
🏢 Real example — emergent strategy

Many well-known products began as side experiments rather than planned strategy. A workplace chat tool, for example, started life as an internal communication tool built by a games company. When the game failed, the tool became the business. Management recognised a pattern and backed it. In practice most organisations combine a deliberate plan with emergent learning.

1.6 Strategic decisions vs operational decisions — summary

🧠 Memory tip

Strategy = "Where are we going, and how will we win?" Mission = why, vision = where, objectives = how much and by when.

2C1a

Why Strategy Matters in Different Organisational Contexts

📖 In simple words

Every organisation needs strategy, but what it is for and who it must satisfy differs. A charity's strategy is judged differently from a listed company's. The SBL exam can be set in any type of organisation, so you must adapt.

ContextMain purpose of strategySpecial features to mention in an answer
Large listed companyCreate shareholder value and competitive advantageInvestor pressure, short-termism, reporting to markets, global competition
Small / medium enterprise (SME)Survive, grow, find a nicheOwner-manager dominates decisions, limited cash, informal planning, reliance on a few customers
MultinationalCoordinate many countries and businessesBalancing global efficiency with local adaptation, currency and political risk, complex structures
Public sector (government hospital, school, agency)Deliver public services and public value within a budgetPolitical influence, many stakeholders, value for money (the 3 E's), accountability to citizens, funding set by government
Not-for-profit / charityAchieve a social missionDonors and volunteers, mission drift risk, measuring outcomes is hard, limited funds
Professional services (law, accounting, consulting)Attract and retain talent and clientsThe people are the asset, partnership structures, reputation
Start-up / entrepreneurial firmFind a business model that works and scale itHigh uncertainty, emergent strategy, investor funding rounds
🏢 Example — same decision, different contexts

Decision: "Should we close our least-used site?"

  • Listed retailer: mainly a profit question. If the store loses money and the land can be sold, closure increases shareholder value.
  • Public hospital: even if the site is costly, closing it may leave a rural community with no nearby care. Political approval, public consultation and equity of access matter as much as cost.
  • Charity: closure may save money for the mission, but could upset donors who funded that site.
📝 How it appears in SBL

Rarely as "explain why strategy is important". More often you are expected to show you understand the context when evaluating a decision, for example by recognising that a public body must consider political approval, the 3 E's and fairness, not just profit.

✅ Examiner expectations
  • Identify the type of organisation in the pre-seen and let it shape your answer.
  • For public and not-for-profit bodies, talk about value for money, public value, mission and accountability rather than "profit maximisation".
❌ Common mistake

Writing "this will increase shareholder wealth" about a government hospital or a charity. They have no shareholders in that sense.

3C1b

The Johnson, Scholes & Whittington (JSW) Model

📖 Step 1 — In simple words

JSW is a map of the whole strategy process. It has three elements:

  1. Strategic position: Where are we now, and what is affecting us? (analysis)
  2. Strategic choices: What options do we have, and which should we choose? (choice)
  3. Strategy in action: How do we make it happen? (implementation)

Your syllabus words these as strategic analysis, strategic choice and strategic implementation. They mean the same thing.

🔍 Strategic position

  • Environment: PESTEL, Five Forces, scenarios, Diamond
  • Strategic capability: resources, competences, value chain, SWOT
  • Purpose: mission, stakeholders, governance, ethics
  • Culture: cultural web, history

🧭 Strategic choices

  • Business-level: how to compete (cost, differentiation)
  • Corporate-level: which markets and products (Ansoff, BCG)
  • International strategy
  • Innovation
  • Methods: organic growth, M&A, alliances

⚙️ Strategy in action

  • Evaluating options (suitability, acceptability, feasibility)
  • Organising: structure and systems
  • Resourcing: people, IT, finance
  • Leading change
  • Projects, performance measurement
⚠️ Important: the three elements overlap

JSW is not a strict step 1 → 2 → 3 sequence. The three elements influence each other: implementation problems may force you to rethink the choice, and a new environmental change may restart the analysis. JSW drew the model as three overlapping circles for exactly this reason.

The SAF test — evaluating strategic choices

Within JSW, options are judged with three tests. You'll use these constantly in SBL whenever you're asked to "evaluate" a strategy:

TestQuestionTypical evidence
SuitabilityDoes it fit our position? Does it use our strengths, fix weaknesses, grab opportunities and counter threats?PESTEL, SWOT, mission fit
AcceptabilityWill stakeholders accept the returns, the risk and the reactions?NPV, payback, risk level, stakeholder reactions (Mendelow)
FeasibilityCan we actually do it? Do we have the money, people, skills and technology?Cash available, skills gaps, timescales
🏢 Step 2 — Example: JSW applied to a coffee chain

BeanBox has 40 coffee shops in one country.

  • Position: Office workers are moving to hybrid work, so city-centre footfall is down 25% (environment). BeanBox has a strong loyalty app with 300,000 users (capability). Its mission is "great coffee wherever you are" (purpose).
  • Choices: (a) close city stores and open in suburbs, (b) launch home coffee subscriptions through the app, (c) merge with a rival.
  • SAF on option (b): Suitable: it uses the app strength and the "wherever you are" mission. Acceptable: modest investment and low risk. Feasible: it needs logistics capability BeanBox lacks, so it could partner with a courier.
  • Action: create a subscriptions team, set up the courier partnership, run a 6-month pilot with 10,000 app users, and track KPIs.
📝 Step 3 — How it appears in SBL
  • "Evaluate the proposed strategy…" → use suitability, acceptability, feasibility as headings.
  • "Explain how the board should approach the development of a new strategy…" → walk through position → choices → action, applied to the case.
  • Most often, JSW sits behind your thinking without being named, telling you to analyse before recommending.

Step 4 — Model answer extract

Task: "Evaluate the proposal for BeanBox to launch a home coffee subscription service. (8 marks)"

Suitability. The proposal responds directly to the 25% fall in city-centre footfall caused by hybrid working. It moves sales to where customers now are, at home. It also builds on BeanBox's main strength, its 300,000-user loyalty app, which gives an immediate customer base to market to at almost no cost. It fits the mission of "great coffee wherever you are". 2

Acceptability. Investment is modest compared with opening new stores, so financial risk is low and shareholders are likely to support it. Store managers, however, may see it as a threat to in-store sales and resist promoting it. Their bonuses should reflect total customer spend, not just store sales. 2

Feasibility. BeanBox has no delivery or warehousing experience. Building this in-house would be slow and costly, so a partnership with an established courier is the more feasible route. Packaging and roasting capacity also need confirming before launch. 2

Conclusion. The subscription service is suitable and acceptable. Feasibility depends on securing a delivery partner, so I recommend a 6-month pilot with 10,000 app users before a full launch. 2

✅ Step 5 — Examiner expectations
  • Use SAF as a structure, but fill it with case facts.
  • Don't just describe the three JSW elements. Show how they connect in this case.
  • End with a clear recommendation.
❌ Step 6 — Common mistakes
  • Drawing or describing the model in general terms ("JSW has three elements, which are…").
  • Treating it as a rigid sequence.
  • Leaving out feasibility. Students love ideas but forget to check whether the money and skills exist.
🧠 Step 7 — Memory tip

JSW = "Where are we → Where could we go → How do we get there." Evaluate options with SAF: "Should it, Agree to it, Achieve it?"

4C2 overview

The Layers of the Business Environment

📖 In simple words

The world outside an organisation can be pictured as layers, like an onion. Each layer has its own analysis tool.

Outside in: macro-environment → industry → competitors & markets → organisation. This chapter covers the outer layer; the next chapter covers the inner two.
LayerWhat it isToolCan the organisation influence it?
Macro-environmentBroad forces affecting everyone: economy, laws, technology, societyPESTEL, key drivers, scenarios, Porter's DiamondAlmost never
Industry / sectorOrganisations offering similar products or servicesPorter's Five Forces (next chapter)A little
Competitors & marketsSpecific rivals and customer groupsSegmentation, competitor analysis (next chapter)Yes, partly
📖 Two words you will use constantly
  • Opportunity: an external change the organisation could benefit from.
  • Threat: an external change that could harm it.

The same change can be an opportunity for one firm and a threat for another. An ageing population is an opportunity for care-home operators and a threat (from rising demand and costs) for a public health budget.

5C2a

PESTEL Analysis

📖 Step 1 — In simple words

PESTEL is a checklist of six types of outside force, so you don't miss anything important when scanning the world around an organisation.

P — PoliticalGovernment policy, stability, elections, trade policy, public spending, funding decisions
E — EconomicGrowth or recession, inflation, interest rates, exchange rates, unemployment, disposable income
S — SocialPopulation size and age, lifestyles, health awareness, attitudes, education, migration
T — TechnologicalNew tech, automation, AI, digital channels, data, cyber threats, R&D
E — EnvironmentalClimate change, pollution, waste, water and energy scarcity, extreme weather, "green" expectations
L — LegalLaws and regulations: employment, health & safety, data protection, competition, licensing
⚠️ Political vs Legal — a common confusion

Political = what government wants or intends (policies, priorities, stability). Legal = what the law requires now (rules you must obey).

Example: the government announces it wants to cut sugar consumption (political). A sugar tax is passed and must be paid (legal).

How to do a PESTEL properly — the 4-step method

  1. Identify the external factor from the case. "Interest rates have risen from 2% to 6%."
  2. Explain the impact on this organisation. "The company has $50m of variable-rate loans, so annual interest rises by $2m."
  3. Classify and rank: is it an opportunity or a threat, and how significant? "A major threat: it wipes out 40% of profit."
  4. Respond: what should the organisation do? "Refinance part of the debt at a fixed rate and delay non-essential capital spending."
🏢 Step 2 — Full example: PESTEL for a private hospital group

Meridian Health (fictional) runs 5 private hospitals in the country of Westland, funded mainly by insurance companies.

FactorCase factImpact on MeridianO/T
PoliticalGovernment plans to make health insurance compulsory for all private-sector workersMillions more insured patients; demand for private care risesO ⬆️
EconomicMedical-supply inflation of 9% vs general inflation of 3%Costs rise faster than insurer tariffs, so margins are squeezedT
SocialPopulation aged 60+ to double in 10 years; rising diabetesLong-term demand for chronic and elderly careO
TechnologicalTelemedicine widely accepted since the pandemicCheaper follow-up consultations; but new online-only competitorsO & T
EnvironmentalNew targets to cut hospital carbon emissions; summer heatwavesInvestment needed in energy efficiency; heat-related admissions riseT
LegalNew patient data protection law with large finesMust upgrade IT security and consent processesT

Notice that every row is about Meridian and says so what. That is what turns a list into analysis.

🛒 More examples by industry
IndustryFactorExample

📝 Step 3 — How it appears in SBL
  • "Using an appropriate model, assess the impact of the external environment on [organisation]…" → PESTEL is the natural choice.
  • "Evaluate the opportunities and threats facing…" → PESTEL (and Five Forces) give you the content.
  • Often not named. Use PESTEL silently to make sure you've spotted every external issue in the exhibits.

Step 4 — Model answer extract

Task: "Assess the key external factors that will affect Meridian Health's strategy over the next five years. (10 marks)"

Political – compulsory insurance (opportunity, high significance). The planned law making insurance compulsory for private-sector workers would greatly increase the number of insured patients. Since Meridian is funded mainly by insurers, demand could rise sharply. This is the most significant factor, because it could require new capacity. Meridian should start capacity planning now and talk to insurers about tariff agreements before the law takes effect. 2

Economic – medical-supply inflation (threat). Supply costs are rising at 9% while insurer tariffs are usually fixed annually, so each year of the gap erodes margins. Meridian should negotiate group purchasing contracts and build inflation-linked clauses into insurer agreements. 2

Social – ageing population and diabetes (opportunity). With the over-60 population set to double in a decade, demand for chronic disease and elderly care will grow steadily. Meridian could develop specialist diabetes and geriatric services, which also suit its long-term positioning. 2

Legal – data protection law (threat). Meridian holds sensitive patient data across five hospitals, and the new law carries large fines. A breach would damage both finances and reputation, which is critical in healthcare. A data-protection audit and IT security investment should be prioritised. 2

Conclusion. The political change is the biggest opportunity and the data protection law the most urgent threat. Meridian's strategy should focus on capacity growth in chronic care while securing compliance. 2

✅ Step 5 — Examiner expectations
  • Quality over quantity. Four well-developed factors beat twelve one-liners.
  • Every factor linked to the case, with its impact explained.
  • Prioritise: say which factors matter most ("assess" means judge significance).
  • Finish with what the organisation should do (commercial acumen).
  • You don't need every letter of PESTEL. Use only the factors the case supports.
❌ Step 6 — Common mistakes
  • Listing generic factors: "Economic: inflation may affect the company."
  • Including internal issues ("poor management", "old IT system"). PESTEL is external only.
  • Forcing a point under every letter even when the case has nothing for it.
  • Not saying whether each factor is an opportunity or a threat.
🧠 Step 7 — Memory tip

"Please Explain Some Technical Environmental Laws" = P-E-S-T-E-L. For each factor think "Fact → Effect → Response".

6C2c

Key Drivers of Change

📖 Step 1 — In simple words

A PESTEL often produces a long list. Key drivers of change are the few factors from that list that are likely to have the biggest effect on the structure of an industry, or on whether an organisation's strategy succeeds or fails.

Think of PESTEL as casting a wide net and key drivers as picking out the big fish.

How to identify a key driver

Ask two questions of each PESTEL factor:

  1. Impact: how much will it change the industry or our strategy? (high or low)
  2. Breadth: does it change the structure of the industry (who competes, how money is made), or only the details?

Factors with high impact that reshape the industry are the key drivers. Different industries have different key drivers.

IndustryLikely key drivers of changeHow the industry structure changes
HealthcareAgeing population; digital health and AI diagnostics; funding pressureShift from hospital to home and virtual care; new tech entrants
RetailE-commerce; consumer data; rising rentsFewer, smaller stores; online giants dominate
AutomotiveElectrification; emission laws; battery technologyNew EV entrants, need for charging networks, petrol-engine skills become obsolete
EnergyClimate policy; cheap renewablesMove from large fossil plants to distributed solar and wind
BankingFintech; open-banking regulationPayments taken over by tech firms; branches decline
📖 Globalisation drivers (Yip)

When an industry is becoming more global, JSW suggest four types of driver. These are useful when a case involves international expansion:

  • Market drivers: similar customer needs across countries, global customers, transferable marketing (e.g. smartphones).
  • Cost drivers: economies of scale, cheaper production countries, falling transport costs.
  • Government drivers: trade agreements, falling tariffs, fewer foreign-ownership restrictions.
  • Competitive drivers: rivals going global and interdependent operations across countries, which forces you to follow.
🏢 Step 2 — Example: a newspaper publisher

The publisher's PESTEL produced 14 factors, including paper prices, a minor tax change, an ageing readership and the move to free online news. The key driver is clearly the digital shift: it has destroyed the old model of earning from printed copies and print advertising, and has brought in new competitors (social media, search engines). Paper prices matter, but they don't change the industry's structure.

📝 Step 3 — How it appears in SBL

"Evaluate the key drivers of change likely to affect the [sector]…" or "Identify the most significant external changes…". The marker wants prioritisation: why these factors, and how they will reshape competition.

Step 4 — Model answer extract

Task: "Evaluate the key drivers of change in the private hospital sector in Westland. (6 marks)"

Key driver 1 – Compulsory health insurance. This will expand the insured population and therefore the size of the whole private market. It is likely to attract new entrants, including international hospital groups, and to change the sector from a few established players into a more competitive market. Existing providers must grow capacity quickly to protect their share. 2

Key driver 2 – Telemedicine. Virtual consultations remove the need for patients to attend a hospital for follow-ups. That lowers barriers to entry for online-only providers that have no buildings. The value in the sector shifts from bed capacity to digital access and convenience, so hospitals that don't build a digital offer may lose outpatient income. 2

Why these two. Medical inflation and environmental rules matter, but they affect all providers equally and don't change how the sector competes. These two drivers alter market size and the basis of competition, so they deserve priority in strategic planning. 2

✅ Step 5 — Examiner expectations
  • Pick the 2–4 most important factors and justify why they matter most.
  • Explain how they change the industry, not just the company.
❌ Step 6 — Common mistakes

Producing a full PESTEL when the question asks for key drivers. That wastes time and shows no judgement.

🧠 Step 7 — Memory tip

"PESTEL finds them, key drivers ranks them." Key driver = high impact and it changes the rules of the game.

7C2b

Strategic Drift

📖 Step 1 — In simple words

Strategic drift happens when an organisation's strategy slowly falls out of step with a changing environment. The organisation keeps doing what made it successful, making only small improvements, while the world changes faster. It doesn't notice the gap until performance collapses.

A useful image: a boat drifting slowly on a current. Each minute looks fine, but after an hour it's far from where it should be.

The four phases of strategic drift (JSW)

The gap between the blue line (the environment) and the red line (strategy) is the drift.
PhaseWhat happensWarning signs
1. Incremental changeStrategy changes in small steps that keep pace with a slowly changing environment. This is normal and healthy.Steady performance
2. Strategic driftThe environment speeds up but the organisation keeps making small, familiar changes. The gap widens, often unnoticed because results still look acceptable.Market share slipping slowly; "we've always done it this way"; competitors winning new customer groups
3. FluxPerformance falls clearly. Management disagrees about what to do, with lots of conflicting initiatives and no clear direction.Falling profits, leadership changes, confusion, internal conflict
4. Transformational change or deathEither a radical change of strategy realigns the organisation with its environment, or it fails, is taken over or closes.Restructuring, new CEO, big strategic shift, or collapse

Why does drift happen?

  • Past success breeds complacency. "Our formula works."
  • Culture and the "paradigm". Shared, taken-for-granted beliefs filter out uncomfortable information. You'll study this in the cultural web later.
  • Investment lock-in. Huge sunk costs in the old way (factories, systems, skills).
  • Change is slow to see. Gradual change doesn't trigger alarms.
  • Powerful internal groups who benefit from the status quo resist change.
  • Lagging performance measures. Profit can stay fine for a while even as the future erodes.

How to avoid or escape drift

  • Environmental scanning (PESTEL, key drivers, scenarios) done regularly, with board attention.
  • Leading indicators in KPIs: customer satisfaction, share among young customers, digital adoption. Not just profit.
  • Diverse board and NEDs who challenge assumptions.
  • Encouraging innovation and intrapreneurship, with small experiments in new models.
  • Strong leadership willing to drive transformational change before a crisis.
🏢 Step 2 — Real examples
  • Kodak: a leader in photographic film, it even developed early digital camera technology, but kept protecting its highly profitable film business. As consumers moved to digital and then smartphones, film sales collapsed, and the company filed for bankruptcy protection in 2012.
  • Nokia (mobile phones): dominated phones in the 2000s but kept improving hardware incrementally while the industry switched to app-based smartphones. Its handset business was later sold.
  • Blockbuster: refined its rental-store model while customers moved to postal rental and then streaming. It ended in closure.
  • A positive example: some companies escape drift by transforming. Netflix, for instance, moved from posting DVDs to streaming, cannibalising its own core business before rivals could.
🏥 Example — strategic drift in a public body

Eastvale Council Library Service (fictional) kept extending opening hours and buying more printed books for 15 years. Meanwhile people moved to e-books, online study and community digital hubs. Visits fell 60%, budgets were cut, and the council debated closing branches (flux). The transformational option was to reinvent libraries as digital-skills and community hubs, with e-lending, coding clubs and co-working spaces.

📝 Step 3 — How it appears in SBL
  • "Assess whether [organisation] is suffering from strategic drift…" → find the evidence in the case, identify the phase, and explain the causes.
  • "Advise the board how to avoid strategic drift…" → recommendations linked to the specific causes in the case.
  • Hidden version: a long-established, once-successful company with declining results and a board that "doesn't see the need to change". Mention drift even if it isn't named.

Step 4 — Model answer extract

Case: "ClassicCards" has printed greeting cards for 60 years. Sales have fallen 4% a year for six years. Its board, mostly long-serving family members, says "people will always want a real card". Online personalised card businesses have grown 20% a year. Task: "Assess whether ClassicCards is experiencing strategic drift. (6 marks)"

Evidence of drift. ClassicCards' strategy has not changed meaningfully, while the market has moved towards online, personalised cards growing at 20% a year. Its own sales have fallen 4% a year for six years. A steady decline alongside fast change elsewhere is the typical pattern of strategic drift. 2

Causes. The board's belief that "people will always want a real card" reflects a paradigm built on 60 years of success, which filters out evidence of changing behaviour. A board of long-serving family members is unlikely to provide fresh challenge. Investment in printing presses also encourages the company to protect the existing model. 2

Current phase. The gradual, accepted decline suggests ClassicCards is in the drift phase, moving towards flux. Without transformational change, perhaps launching its own online personalisation platform using its design archive, it risks failure or takeover. Appointing independent NEDs with digital retail experience would help challenge the current thinking. 2

✅ Step 5 — Examiner expectations
  • Use case evidence to prove drift: trends, quotes and comparisons with competitors.
  • Explain the causes (culture, success, lock-in) as well as the symptoms.
  • Recommend specific ways out.
❌ Step 6 — Common mistakes
  • Confusing drift with a sudden shock (e.g. a factory fire). Drift is gradual.
  • Describing the four phases without placing the organisation in one.
🧠 Step 7 — Memory tip

"I Drift, Flux, then Transform or Die" (I-D-F-T/D). The organisation is the frog in slowly heating water: the change is too gradual to feel until it's too late.

8C2d

Porter's Diamond — National Competitive Advantage

📖 Step 1 — In simple words

Why are certain countries world leaders in certain industries? Think of Swiss watches, Italian fashion, Japanese cars, Silicon Valley tech, or Dutch flowers. Porter said the answer lies in four features of the home country that form a "diamond". When all four are strong, firms there develop advantages that help them win globally.

Four determinants, plus two outside influences: government and chance.

The four determinants explained

DeterminantMeaningExample
1. Factor conditionsThe country's inputs. Basic factors are natural resources, climate, unskilled labour and location. Advanced factors are skilled people, research institutes, universities and digital infrastructure. Advanced factors matter more for lasting advantage because they are created and hard to copy.India's large pool of English-speaking IT graduates helped build its software services industry
2. Demand conditionsThe nature of home-market customers. Demanding, sophisticated local buyers force firms to innovate and raise quality early.Japan's demanding consumers and small homes pushed its firms to make compact, high-quality electronics
3. Related & supporting industriesStrong local suppliers and connected industries (clusters) that share knowledge, skills and innovation.Italy's leather tanneries, design schools and machinery makers support its luxury shoe and fashion brands
4. Firm strategy, structure & rivalryHow companies are managed and how hard they compete at home. Strong domestic rivalry pushes firms to improve, making them fit for global competition.Intense rivalry between several German carmakers drives engineering excellence
+ GovernmentCan strengthen or weaken all four, through education, infrastructure, regulation, tax incentives and trade policy.Tax-free zones and investment in fibre networks attract tech firms
+ ChanceUnpredictable events such as inventions, wars or pandemics that can shift advantage.A pandemic suddenly accelerated countries with strong digital health infrastructure

How organisations use Porter's Diamond

  1. Choosing where to expand or locate: which country offers the best diamond for our industry?
  2. Explaining our home advantage: what has helped us succeed, and is it transferable abroad?
  3. Assessing foreign competitors: why are rivals from country X so strong?
  4. Governments use it to decide which industries to support.
🏢 Step 2 — Example: where to locate an AI research hub

DataNova, a software company, is choosing between Country A and Country B for a new AI research centre.

DeterminantCountry ACountry B
Factor conditions3 top universities in computer science; high-speed networksLow salaries but few AI graduates
Demand conditionsBanks and hospitals are early AI adoptersCustomers price-focused, slow to adopt
Related & supportingCluster of cloud providers and AI start-upsFew tech suppliers

GovernmentR&D tax credits; fast work visasTax holidays but slow visas

Conclusion: Country B is cheaper, but Country A's diamond is far stronger for an innovation-driven activity, so DataNova should choose A. For a low-cost data-entry centre, B might be the better choice. The right answer depends on the activity.

📝 Step 3 — How it appears in SBL
  • "Using Porter's Diamond, assess whether [country] is a suitable location for…"
  • "Explain why [organisation] has been successful in its home country, and whether this advantage will transfer abroad."
  • Usually appears when a case involves international expansion or competition from foreign firms.

Step 4 — Model answer extract

Case: "SilkThread", a textile company in Country H, plans to set up a design studio in Country M. Exhibit: Country M has 4 leading fashion design colleges, a cluster of fabric suppliers and fashion houses in its capital, sophisticated fashion-conscious consumers, and many rival studios. Task: "Use Porter's Diamond to assess Country M as a location for the design studio. (8 marks)"

Factor conditions – strong. Country M's four leading fashion design colleges provide an advanced, specialised talent pool. This is exactly the creative skill a design studio relies on, and it would be hard for SilkThread to develop in Country H. 2

Demand conditions – strong. Fashion-conscious, sophisticated consumers give rapid feedback on trends. Designing for them would keep SilkThread's collections ahead and sharpen its products for global markets. 2

Related and supporting industries – strong. The cluster of fabric suppliers and fashion houses in the capital would allow fast sampling and prototyping, and gives access to industry networks and knowledge. 2

Rivalry – a challenge but beneficial. Many rival studios mean strong competition for top designers, which could push up salaries. However, that rivalry is also what keeps the local design standard high. Overall, Country M has a strong diamond for design, and I recommend locating the studio there, with a competitive pay package to attract talent. 2

✅ Step 5 — Examiner expectations
  • Apply each determinant to the country and industry in the case.
  • Distinguish basic vs advanced factors where relevant.
  • Judge whether the diamond fits the specific activity, and give an overall conclusion.
❌ Step 6 — Common mistakes
  • Confusing the Diamond with Five Forces. The Diamond is about a country's advantage; Five Forces is about an industry's profitability.
  • Analysing the company instead of the country.
🧠 Step 7 — Memory tip

"F-D-R-S" = "Factors, Demand, Related, Strategy", plus Government and Chance outside the diamond. Picture a diamond ring: four corners, and the government and luck that bought it.

Scenario Planning

📖 Step 1 — In simple words

Nobody can predict the future. Scenarios are a few different, believable stories about how the future might turn out, built around the biggest uncertainties. Instead of betting everything on one forecast, the organisation asks: "If this future happens, what should we do? And if that one happens?"

A forecast says "this will happen". A scenario says "this could happen; are we ready?"

When are scenarios useful?

  • When the future is highly uncertain (new technology, political change, climate).
  • When decisions are long-term and costly to reverse (new hospital, power plant, factory).
  • When several factors could combine in different ways.

The 5 steps of building scenarios

  1. Set the scope and time horizon. Example: "Demand for our hospital services in 2035."
  2. Identify the key drivers of change (from PESTEL).
  3. Select the 2 drivers that are both HIGH impact and HIGH uncertainty. Certain factors (e.g. an ageing population, which is almost guaranteed) go into every scenario. Uncertain ones are used to build the different scenarios.
  4. Develop 3–4 scenario stories, usually by combining the two uncertain drivers in a 2×2 grid. Give each a memorable name and describe what the world looks like.
  5. Identify the implications and early warning signals: what each scenario means for strategy, which strategies work across all scenarios ("robust" strategies), and which indicators to watch.
🏥 Step 2 — Example: scenarios for Meridian Health (2035)

Two high-impact, high-uncertainty drivers: (1) whether compulsory insurance is introduced and (2) how far patients adopt virtual care.

Low virtual-care adoptionHigh virtual-care adoption
Compulsory insurance introduced"Full Wards": a boom in patients wanting physical hospitals. Strategy: build beds fast."Digital Boom": many insured patients, mostly served online. Strategy: invest in a telehealth platform plus a few specialist centres.
No compulsory insurance"Status Quo": slow growth. Strategy: efficiency and niche specialisms."Online Challengers": limited insured demand and fierce online competition. Strategy: cut fixed costs, partner with a digital provider.

Robust strategy (works in all four): build a modest virtual-care capability now and add bed capacity in flexible phases.

Early warning signals: progress of the insurance bill in parliament; monthly share of consultations done online.

Advantages of scenariosDisadvantages / limitations
  • Challenges management assumptions and reduces the risk of strategic drift
  • Prepares contingency plans, so faster responses
  • Identifies robust strategies
  • Encourages long-term thinking and debate
  • Time-consuming and can be costly
  • Scenarios are subjective and may miss the real future
  • Managers may pick a favourite scenario and ignore the rest
  • Too many scenarios cause confusion
📝 Step 3 — How it appears in SBL
  • "Explain how scenario planning could help [organisation] plan for…"
  • "Develop scenarios for…", using drivers given in the exhibits.
  • "Evaluate the use of scenario planning by the board…"

Step 4 — Model answer extract

Task: "Explain how scenario planning could help Meridian Health's board decide whether to build a new 300-bed hospital. (6 marks)"

Dealing with uncertainty. A 300-bed hospital is a large, irreversible investment that will operate for decades, yet demand depends on two highly uncertain factors: whether compulsory insurance is introduced and how fast patients adopt virtual care. Scenario planning would let the board test the hospital decision against several plausible futures instead of relying on one optimistic forecast. 2

Finding a robust option. If the full hospital only makes sense in the "Full Wards" scenario, the board may instead choose a phased design, for example 150 beds with the ability to expand. That performs acceptably across all scenarios and limits the risk of empty capacity. 2

Early warning signals. Scenarios would identify signals to monitor, such as the insurance bill's progress and the share of consultations held online. The board could then trigger the second phase only when evidence supports it. 2

✅ Step 5 — Examiner expectations
  • Link scenarios to a specific decision in the case.
  • Use the case's uncertain factors as drivers.
  • Mention robust strategies and signposts. These show real understanding.
❌ Step 6 — Common mistakes
  • Calling a scenario a "forecast" or "prediction".
  • Building scenarios around certain factors, which don't create different futures.
  • Creating "best / worst / middle" cases of one variable. That is sensitivity analysis, not scenario planning.
🧠 Step 7 — Memory tip

"2 uncertainties → 4 boxes → 1 robust plan → watch the signals."

Full Exam-Style Mini-Case

📁 Case: GreenLeaf Beverages

Exhibit 1 (pre-seen extract): GreenLeaf has bottled fizzy soft drinks in Country R for 45 years. It holds 30% of the market. 90% of revenue comes from full-sugar drinks sold through supermarkets. The board has had the same members for 12 years.

Exhibit 2 (minutes of board meeting): CEO: "Our brand is loved. Sales dipped 3% this year, as they did last year, but that's the weather. We should keep doing what we do best." Marketing Director: "Younger customers are buying sparkling flavoured waters and energy drinks from new brands online."

Exhibit 3 (news): Country R's government will introduce a sugar tax of $0.30 per litre on drinks above 8g sugar per 100ml from next year. Plastic bottle deposit rules start in 2 years. The economy is in mild recession, with consumers trading down to supermarket own brands.

Requirement: As a consultant, prepare a report to the board which (a) assesses the key external factors affecting GreenLeaf (10 marks), and (b) evaluates whether GreenLeaf is experiencing strategic drift (6 marks). Professional skills marks are available for demonstrating commercial acumen (2 marks).

Time ≈ 35 minutes (18 marks × 1.95). Try writing your own answer first, then open the model answer.

❌ Show a weak answer (and why it fails)

(a) PESTEL: Political – government can change policies. Economic – recession affects sales. Social – people's tastes change. Technological – new technology. Environmental – climate change is important. Legal – companies must obey laws. 1 – only the recession is case-linked

(b) Strategic drift is when strategy doesn't keep up with the environment. It has 4 phases: incremental, drift, flux and transformation. GreenLeaf might be drifting. ½ – theory, no evidence

Estimated: 1½ / 16 technical, 0 / 2 professional skills. Not in report format, generic, no prioritisation, no evidence and no recommendations.

✅ Model distinction answer

REPORT
To: Board of Directors, GreenLeaf Beverages
From: Strategy Consultant
Subject: External environment and strategic position

1. Introduction
This report assesses the main external factors affecting GreenLeaf and evaluates whether its strategy is falling out of step with its market.

2. Key external factors

Legal / political – sugar tax (most significant threat). The $0.30 per litre tax from next year applies to drinks above 8g sugar per 100ml, which covers most of GreenLeaf's full-sugar range. With 90% of revenue from these drinks, GreenLeaf must either absorb the tax, cutting margins, or raise prices and risk losing volume. Either way, this is the most urgent issue. GreenLeaf should begin reformulating its leading brands below the 8g threshold now. 2

Social – changing tastes of younger consumers (threat and opportunity). Younger buyers are moving to flavoured sparkling waters and energy drinks (Exhibit 2), and buying them online from new brands. This shrinks GreenLeaf's future customer base. But the trend also opens a growing segment where GreenLeaf's bottling scale and supermarket relationships could help it compete if it launched its own low-sugar range. 2

Economic – recession and trading down (threat). Consumers switching to supermarket own brands puts pressure on GreenLeaf's prices just as the sugar tax raises costs. Price increases would be particularly risky now. Smaller pack sizes at lower price points could protect volume. 2

Environmental / legal – plastic deposit rules (threat, medium-term). The deposit scheme in two years will add cost and complexity to plastic bottles. GreenLeaf should plan for recycled or alternative packaging, which could also appeal to environmentally aware younger customers. 2

Technological – online channels (threat). New brands sell online directly to consumers, bypassing the supermarkets GreenLeaf depends on. GreenLeaf has no online channel, which weakens its access to younger buyers. 1

Overall assessment. The sugar tax and changing tastes are the key drivers. Together they challenge GreenLeaf's core product, so they need a strategic response, not minor adjustments. 1

3. Is GreenLeaf experiencing strategic drift?

Evidence. Sales have fallen 3% in each of the last two years, while competitors in newer categories are growing. GreenLeaf's product range and channels have not changed in response. This gradual decline against a fast-moving market is consistent with strategic drift. 2

Causes. The CEO attributes the fall to the weather and wants to "keep doing what we do best". That is a paradigm shaped by 45 years of success, which explains away warning signs. A board unchanged for 12 years is less likely to challenge this view, even though the Marketing Director has flagged the shift. 2

Phase and urgency. GreenLeaf appears to be in the drift phase. The sugar tax could quickly push it into flux, with sharply falling profits and internal conflict. Transformational change is needed before that happens. 2

4. Recommendations

  • Within 3 months, start reformulating the top five brands below the 8g sugar threshold, before the tax applies.
  • Launch a low-sugar sparkling flavoured water range using existing bottling capacity, sold online and in supermarkets.
  • Appoint at least two independent non-executive directors with consumer and digital experience to challenge current assumptions.
  • Set up a packaging project for the deposit scheme, aiming for 50% recycled plastic within two years.

Estimated: 16 / 16 technical (capped) + 2 / 2 commercial acumen. The commercial acumen comes from linking the tax to the 90% revenue dependence, spotting that price rises are risky in a recession, and using existing bottling capacity and supermarket relationships in the recommendations.

Self-Test Quiz

Answer each question in your head, or in writing, before opening it.

Q1. Give three characteristics of a strategic decision.

Any three of: long-term; affects the scope of the organisation; commits large resources; complex and uncertain; affects the whole organisation; hard to reverse; influenced by stakeholder expectations.

Q2. What are the three elements of the JSW model, and why are they drawn as overlapping?

Strategic position, strategic choices and strategy in action. They overlap because they influence each other and don't happen in a strict sequence. For example, implementation difficulties can send you back to rethink the choice.

Q3. Name the three SAF tests and give a question for each.

Suitability: does it fit our position and the opportunities and threats we face? Acceptability: will stakeholders accept the returns, risk and reactions? Feasibility: do we have the money, skills and resources to do it?

Q4. A hospital's IT system is 15 years old and keeps crashing. Is this a PESTEL factor?

No. It is an internal weakness, which belongs in SWOT. PESTEL covers external factors only. However, "new cloud-based hospital systems are now widely available" is a technological PESTEL factor.

Q5. Explain the difference between a political and a legal factor, with an example.

Political = government intentions and policy (e.g. the government announces it wants to cut sugar consumption). Legal = laws that must be obeyed now (e.g. a sugar tax is enacted).

Q6. What makes a PESTEL factor a "key driver of change"?

It has a high impact on the industry or the organisation's strategy, and it changes the industry's structure or basis of competition, not just minor details.

Q7. List the four phases of strategic drift.

1. Incremental change. 2. Strategic drift. 3. Flux. 4. Transformational change or death.

Q8. Give three causes of strategic drift.

Any three of: past success and complacency; a cultural paradigm that filters out bad news; investment lock-in in old assets; change being too gradual to notice; powerful groups resisting change; reliance on lagging measures like profit.

Q9. Name the four determinants of Porter's Diamond and the two additional influences.

Factor conditions; demand conditions; related and supporting industries; firm strategy, structure and rivalry. Additional influences: government and chance.

Q10. Why are "advanced" factor conditions more valuable than "basic" ones?

Advanced factors (skilled people, research institutions, digital infrastructure) are created through investment, are specialised and are hard for other countries to copy, so the advantage lasts. Basic factors (natural resources, cheap labour) are easy to find elsewhere or can run out.

Q11. Which drivers should be used to build scenarios, and why?

Those with high impact and high uncertainty. Certain factors appear in every scenario, so they don't create different futures. Low-impact factors don't matter enough.

Q12. What is a "robust" strategy in scenario planning?

A strategy that performs acceptably whichever scenario happens, for example building capacity in flexible phases rather than all at once.
