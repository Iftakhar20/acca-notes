Start Here: What This Chapter Does

In the last chapter you looked at the wide world outside a business: politics, the economy, technology and so on. This chapter moves closer. It asks two questions:

  1. Is this a good industry to be in? Who is taking the profit, and why?
  2. How does this organisation create value? Which activities earn money, and which waste it?

Our running story: Maison Varenne

Meet the company

⚠ Industry or market?

An industry is defined by what is sold. A market is defined by what customers want. This matters: if you define your rivals only by industry, you can miss substitutes. A railway that thinks it competes only with other railways may miss the threat from low-cost flights.

Five Forces: The Big Idea

Step 1 · In simple language

Imagine that an industry's profit is a cake. Five groups are trying to eat it. The more power they have, the less cake is left for the firms in the industry. Porter's Five Forces is a way of asking who is eating the cake, and why.

The golden rule

Strong forces mean an unattractive industry with low long-term profit. Weak forces mean an attractive industry with higher long-term profit.

Step 2 · A real business example
Why airlines earn thin margins while aircraft makers do better

Travellers compare fares in seconds (strong buyers). Very few firms make large aircraft, and few lease them (strong suppliers). Rival airlines have high fixed costs and an empty seat earns nothing, so they discount (strong rivalry). Trains and video calls replace some trips (substitutes). New low-cost carriers can lease aircraft instead of buying them (entrants). So the industry can carry billions of passengers and still earn thin margins. Being big does not make an industry profitable; the forces decide that.

Step 3 · How it may appear in the exam
  • Directly: "Evaluate the competitive forces affecting the company."
  • Indirectly: "Assess whether entering this market would be attractive." Five Forces is your tool, even though the task does not name it.
  • As part of a bigger task: you use the forces to support or challenge a board proposal.
  • In a scenario with a role: for example, you are a strategy adviser writing a briefing paper for the CEO.

Sections 4 to 8 cover each force. Section 9 brings them together with steps 4 to 7: a model answer, examiner expectations, common mistakes and memory tips.

Force 1: Threat of New Entrants

The question: if this industry is profitable, what stops others from joining and taking a share? The answer depends on the barriers to entry. High barriers mean a low threat.

BarrierWhat it meansExample
ScaleBig firms have a cost advantage that a small newcomer cannot matchA small car maker cannot match a giant's cost per car
CapitalA large investment is needed before the first saleA chip factory costs billions
RegulationLicences, safety rules or government limitsBanks, airlines and hospitals need approval to operate
Access to channelsNewcomers cannot reach customersSupermarket shelf space is already taken
Brand and reputationCustomers trust established namesLuxury watches, or a trusted bank
Switching costsCustomers find it painful to changeMoving to a different hospital records system

Add one more check: expected retaliation. If existing firms are likely to cut prices or block a newcomer, entry looks less attractive even when barriers are low.

🧠 Memory tip

"SCRABS" are the crabs guarding the beach from newcomers: Scale, Capital, Regulation, Access, Brand, Switching costs.

Varenne

Building a Swiss luxury brand takes generations, so the threat of a new luxury watchmaker is low. But Sara spots something Henri has missed: a technology giant that already has a brand, distribution and huge capital does not face the same barriers. A barrier stops some entrants, not all of them.

⚠ Technology can knock barriers down

Cloud computing removes the need to own servers. Online sales remove the need for shops. Contract manufacturers let a brand exist without a factory. Always ask if technology has lowered a barrier that looks strong.

How a firm can raise barriers

  • Build brand loyalty and long customer contracts.
  • Increase scale to lower unit costs.
  • Secure key channels, patents or scarce inputs.
  • Raise switching costs through integrated services or loyalty schemes.
5

Force 2: Threat of Substitutes

The question: can customers meet the same need in a different way? A rival sells the same kind of thing. A substitute meets the same need differently.

TypeMeaningExample
Product for productOne product directly replaces anotherPlant milk for dairy milk; email for post
Need for needA new item removes the need for the old oneA smartphone camera replaces a compact camera
Competition for moneyVery different items compete for the same spendingA holiday competes with a new sofa
Doing withoutThe customer does it themselves or does nothingCooking at home rather than ordering food

The threat is strongest when…

  • The substitute gives better value (price compared with performance).
  • Switching is cheap and easy.
  • Customers are willing to try it, for example because of changing tastes.

Substitutes also put a ceiling on prices. If a firm charges too much, customers leave for the alternative.

Varenne

Few people need a mechanical watch to tell the time. A phone does that free. Smartwatches also meet health and notification needs. Varenne's customers, however, buy craft, status and heritage. Sara concludes that the threat is high for gift buyers and younger professionals but low for serious collectors. That split is better than one rating for the whole market.

Other sectors
  • Healthcare: telemedicine replaces some clinic visits, and a day-case procedure replaces an overnight stay.
  • Public sector: an online government portal replaces counter visits.
  • Technology: an AI assistant replaces some software tools.

How a firm can respond

  • Differentiate on the benefits that the substitute cannot match.
  • Improve value for money.
  • Offer the substitute yourself, before someone else does.

Force 3: Power of Buyers

The question: can customers force prices down or demand more for the same price? "Buyers" can be end consumers or businesses such as retailers and insurers.

FactorBuyers are powerful when…Example
ConcentrationA few buyers account for a large share of salesThree supermarkets control most grocery sales
Switching costsIt is cheap and easy to change sellerChanging brand of bottled water
ImportanceThe purchase is a big cost to them, so they push hard on priceAn airline negotiating fuel prices
Integration threatThey could make the product themselvesSupermarkets launching own-label goods
DifferentiationThe product looks the same from every sellerStandard paper or basic cement
🧠 Memory tip

"Can Suppliers Influence Industry Deals?" Use the same five letters for buyers and suppliers: Concentration, Switching costs, Importance, Integration, Differentiation.

Varenne

Individual collectors have little power, because Varenne's watches are scarce and unique. But about half of Varenne's sales go through a few large luxury retail groups. Those groups take a 40% retailer margin. So Sara rates buyer power as low for end consumers but high for retail groups. Henri had been thinking only about the people who wear the watches.

✅ Look for the hidden buyer

In many cases the person who uses the product does not choose or pay for it. A patient may use a hospital, but an insurer may pay for it. A child eats the cereal, but the parent buys it and a retailer decides whether to stock it. Always ask who really has the power.

How a firm can reduce buyer power

  • Differentiate the product and build the brand.
  • Widen the customer base so no buyer dominates.
  • Sell directly to end customers (forward integration).
  • Raise switching costs through contracts and integrated services.
7

Force 4: Power of Suppliers

The question: can those who supply inputs raise prices, reduce quality or refuse to supply? Inputs include materials, components, energy, software and skilled labour.

FactorSuppliers are powerful when…Example
ConcentrationThere are few suppliers and many buyersVery few firms make advanced chips
Switching costsIt is costly to change to another supplierReplacing a hospital's core patient records system
ImportanceThe input is critical, and the customer matters little to the supplierA small firm buying from a global software vendor
Integration threatThe supplier could move into your businessA manufacturer starts selling directly to consumers
DifferentiationThe input is unique, or no substitute existsA patented drug
⚠ Skilled people are suppliers too

If specialist nurses, software engineers or master watchmakers are scarce, they can demand high pay or leave. This is supplier power in the labour market, and it is often overlooked.

Varenne

Varenne buys a key part of its movement from one Swiss specialist, and only about 200 master watchmakers in the region have the skills it needs. Supplier power is high. If that part supplier stops selling to outsiders, production stops. This is a business risk as well as a cost issue, and it would link directly to your risk answer in the exam.

How a firm can reduce supplier power

  • Use more than one supplier, or redesign the product so that inputs are standard.
  • Sign long-term contracts or build partnerships.
  • Make the input yourself (backward integration).
  • For skilled labour, train your own people and improve retention.
8

Force 5: Competitive Rivalry

The question: how hard are existing firms fighting? Intense rivalry means price wars, heavy marketing and constant promotions, all of which reduce profit.

SignWhy it increases rivalryExample
Same productWith little difference, price is the main weaponPetrol stations
Fixed costs highFirms discount to fill capacityAirlines, hotels, private hospitals
Exit barriers highFirms stay and fight, even at a loss, because leaving is costlySpecialised factories that cannot be resold
Slow growthA firm can grow only by taking a rival's customersMature markets such as mobile phone plans
Many equal rivalsNo firm can lead, so all compete hardBusy high-street restaurants
🧠 Memory tip

"Some Firms Exit Slow Markets" gives you Same product, Fixed costs, Exit barriers, Slow growth, Many equal rivals.

Varenne

The luxury watch market is mature, and a few large groups own most of the big brands. Those groups compete on heritage and marketing more than price, so rivalry is moderate. Price wars would hurt everyone's brand. However, rivals are spending heavily on advertising, and Varenne cannot match that budget.

How a firm can reduce rivalry

  • Differentiate, so that price is not the main basis of competition.
  • Focus on a niche that large rivals ignore.
  • Acquire or merge with a competitor, if competition law allows it.
  • Avoid price wars. They rarely create lasting advantage.
Strategic groups: rivals are not all equal

A strategic group is a set of firms in an industry with similar strategies. Rolex-type luxury brands and cheap fashion watches are in one industry, but they do not really compete. Your true rivals are in your own strategic group. Moving to another group is possible but difficult, because each group has its own barriers.

Writing a Five Forces Answer

You now know the forces. This section shows how to turn them into marks.

✅ The four-part formula for each force
  1. Rating: is the force high, medium or low?
  2. Evidence: which fact or number in the case proves it?
  3. So what: what does it do to profit or strategy?
  4. Action: what should the company do? (Add this where the task asks for advice.)

End with an overall conclusion: is this industry attractive, and which force matters most?

Evaluating is more than listing

An evaluation requires a judgement. Compare these two sentences:

❌ Listing

"Buyer power is high because there are few buyers."

✅ Evaluating

"Buyer power is the strongest force: two travel platforms deliver 60% of bookings and charge 15% commission. On a $100 room, that is $15 lost before any operating costs. Reducing this dependence should be the first priority."

Scoring the forces

In your planning, a simple table helps you see the full picture. Here is Sara's result for Varenne:

ForceRatingMain reasonSo what?
New entrantsLow to mediumHeritage is hard to copy, but tech giants can enterBrand must stay strong
SubstitutesHigh (entry-level models)Smartwatches and phonesEntry-level sales are at risk
BuyersHigh (retail groups)Few groups, 40% marginConsider own boutiques
SuppliersHighOne key part supplier; scarce skillsSecure supply and train staff
RivalryMediumMature market; competition on brandNiche focus, not a marketing war

Sara's conclusion: the top of the market is still attractive. The entry level is not. The most urgent issue is supplier dependence, because it could stop production.

Step 4 · A model exam answer
Task (10 marks + professional skills)

SkyHop, a fictional regional airline, is considering a new route between two major cities. Two low-cost carriers already fly it and fill about 75% of their seats. SkyHop's break-even load factor is 80%. A new high-speed rail link opens in 2 years and will reduce rail travel time to under 3 hours. SkyHop leases all its aircraft from one lessor. Airport slots are limited. The CEO says, "It's a busy route, so it must be profitable." Evaluate the competitive forces on this route and advise the CEO.

✅ Model answer

Briefing note to the CEO: attractiveness of the proposed route

Overall conclusion: the route is busy, but it is not attractive at present. Passenger numbers do not show profitability, and I recommend that we do not launch until the points below are resolved.

Rivalry (high). Two low-cost carriers already fill only 75% of seats, against our 80% break-even load factor. ✓ This suggests there is excess capacity. Aircraft costs are largely fixed, so adding seats is likely to start a fare war. ✓ If we matched their load factor, we would fly at a loss on an average trip. ✓

Substitutes (high and rising). The rail link is the most serious long-term threat. ✓ A journey under 3 hours, with city-centre stations, could compete strongly on total travel time. It could reduce demand just as our route matures, so the payback period would be short. ✓

Buyers (high). Passengers can compare fares online with almost no switching costs. ✓ This limits our ability to pass on any cost increases.

Suppliers (medium to high). We depend on one lessor, which limits our negotiating power, and any extra aircraft could raise our exposure. ✓ We should seek quotes from other lessors. ✓

New entrants (low). Limited airport slots and safety regulation protect the route once we are on it, but they are also a barrier to us. ✓ Slots may be valuable assets, so we should consider whether they could be better used elsewhere.

Recommendation. Rivalry today and rail competition tomorrow are the deciding forces. Before committing, the team should model route profit at load factors from 70% to 85%, and with a fall in demand after the rail link opens. ✓ Unless the route breaks even at 75%, we should use our capacity on less contested routes.

Technical marks awarded: about 10 of 10 (one mark per well-applied point, up to the maximum). The answer would also score well for Commercial Acumen, Scepticism and Evaluation.

Step 5 · What the examiner wants
  • Focus on what matters. Spend most of your words on the forces that drive the decision.
  • Use case evidence. Every point should use a fact or a number from the exhibits.
  • Reach a verdict. Say which force matters most and whether the industry is attractive.
  • Challenge weak claims. "It's a busy route" is an opinion from the CEO. Test it, politely. That is Scepticism.
  • Think about the future. Forces change; the rail link does not exist yet.
  • Write in role. Advise the CEO directly. Do not write an essay about Porter.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Explaining what Five Forces is. The marker knows.
  • Treating all five forces equally, even when the case supports only two or three.
  • Writing "buyer power is high" with no evidence.
  • Confusing a rival with a substitute.
  • Analysing the firm's weaknesses. Five Forces is about the industry.
  • Stopping at analysis with no "so what" or conclusion.
Step 7 · Limits and memory tips
⚠ Limits of Five Forces (good for Evaluation marks)
  • It is a snapshot. Fast-moving industries change quickly, so repeat it with scenarios in mind.
  • It treats everyone as an enemy. It can underplay partnerships. Some firms help each other: game consoles need game makers, and these are called complementors.
  • It looks at the average firm. A strong firm can do well in a poor industry.
  • Industry boundaries blur. Who is in the "payments industry" today: banks, phone makers or retailers?
Using Five Forces in the public sector

A public hospital may not earn a profit, but the thinking still works. Rivalry becomes competition for funding, staff and patients. Buyers become the government funder or commissioning body. Suppliers include drug firms and specialist staff. Substitutes include home care and telemedicine. Replace "profit" with "value for money and quality of service".

🧠 Memory tip

R-E-S-A: Rating, Evidence, So what, Action. Write this for each force that matters, then finish with a verdict.

Customers and Market Segmentation

Step 1 · In simple language

Not all customers want the same thing. Segmentation means dividing a market into groups of customers with similar needs. You then choose which groups to serve and how. It is like a restaurant deciding whether to serve quick lunches for office workers or long dinners for families. Trying to please everyone usually pleases no one.

The process has three steps, called STP:

  1. Segment: split the market into groups.
  2. Target: choose the groups worth serving.
  3. Position: decide how you want those customers to see you.

Customer, consumer and strategic customer

TermWho it isExample: baby food
ConsumerUses the productThe baby
CustomerBuys the productThe parent
Strategic customerThe person or group whose decision matters most to the strategyPossibly the supermarket that decides what goes on the shelf, as well as the parent
✅ Why this scores marks

Identifying the true strategic customer often changes the recommendation. A drug firm's strategic customer may be the prescribing doctor or the insurer, not the patient.

Ways to segment a market

BasisMeaningExample
GeographicWhere customers areCity, region, country
DemographicWho they areAge, income, family size, occupation
PsychographicHow they think and liveLifestyle, values, personality
BehaviouralHow they buy and useFrequency, loyalty, benefits sought

For business customers, segment by company size, industry, location, buying process or order size. For public services, segment users by need: for example, low-risk patients and complex patients need very different care.

Is a segment worth serving?

TestQuestion
MeasurableCan we estimate its size and spending?
AccessibleCan we reach these customers?
DifferentDo they respond differently from other groups?
ActionableDo we have the ability to serve them?
SubstantialIs it large and profitable enough?

Worked example: where does the money really come from?

Marc asks Sara to split Varenne's sales by segment.

SegmentUnitsAvg priceContribution %Contribution% of units% of contribution
Collectors500$40,00045%$9.0m4.8%35.4%
Affluent professionals4,000$8,00040%$12.8m38.1%50.4%
Gift buyers6,000$3,00020%$3.6m57.1%14.2%

How to read it: a collector watch contributes $40,000 × 45% = $18,000. Multiply by 500 units to get $9.0m.

The insight: gift buyers are 57% of the watches but only 14% of the contribution, and they are the group most exposed to smartwatches. Collectors are under 5% of the watches but over 35% of the contribution. Henri believed gift buyers "were the business". The data shows otherwise.

⚠ Stay sceptical

Before dropping gift buyers, Sara asks: do they cover shared fixed costs? Do they later become collectors? Does a cheaper model weaken the brand? Contribution alone does not answer these questions, and saying so earns Scepticism marks.

Step 2 · A real business example
Airlines and hotels

An airline sells the same flight to business travellers (who value flexibility and pay more) and leisure travellers (who value a low fare and book early). A hotel group runs luxury, business and budget brands, each aimed at a different customer group. Same industry; different segments, and very different prices.

Step 3 · How it may appear in the exam

You may be given a table of customer groups and asked which to prioritise, or a proposal to enter a new market. You could also be asked which customer data would support pricing or marketing decisions, which links to data analytics in Section E of the syllabus.

Step 4 · A model exam answer
Task (6 marks + professional skills)

Harbour Hospitals, a fictional private group, has three patient groups. Insured patients are 70% of patients and 55% of contribution, and most are covered by two insurers that are demanding a 10% fee cut. Self-paying local patients are 25% of patients and 30% of contribution. Medical tourists are 5% of patients and 15% of contribution. The Finance Director wants to "focus on the insured segment because it's the biggest". Advise the board on which segment should be prioritised.

✅ Model answer

Recommendation: we should protect the insured segment but make growth in the other two segments our priority, because size alone does not make a segment the best one to grow. ✓

Insured patients. This is the largest group, but it earns less than its share: 70% of patients bring 55% of contribution. ✓ With two insurers controlling most of this revenue, they, not patients, are our strategic customers. ✓ A 10% fee cut would reduce contribution further, so relying on them more would increase our exposure.

Self-pay patients. Each 1% of patients brings 1.2% of contribution, against 0.8% for insured patients. ✓ They are local and easy to reach, so this is a realistic growth area.

Medical tourists. They are the most profitable group: 5% of patients bring 15% of contribution, or 3% per 1% of patients. ✓ However, demand may be volatile, as it depends on travel, reputation and other countries' health policies. We should grow this cautiously.

Before deciding, we should confirm whether the figures include all costs, such as translation and overseas marketing, and whether capacity is available. ✓

Marks: about 6 of 6. Notice how each sentence uses the case figures and reaches a judgement.

Step 5 · What the examiner wants
  • Use the numbers to compare segments, and calculate something simple.
  • Link each segment to profit, risk and capability.
  • Recommend clearly, then state what extra data you need.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Choosing the largest segment without looking at profit.
  • Listing segmentation bases with no link to the case.
  • Forgetting that the buyer and the user may be different people.
  • Making a decision on revenue alone, without thinking about costs or capacity.
Step 7 · Memory tips
🧠 Memory tips
  • STP: Segment, Target, Position. Think of it as "cut the cake, pick a slice, decorate it".
  • "MADAS" checks if a segment is worth it: Measurable, Accessible, Different, Actionable, Substantial.
  • Bases: "Good Data Pays Back": Geographic, Demographic, Psychographic, Behavioural.

Porter's Value Chain

Step 1 · In simple language

A customer does not pay for your effort. They pay for what they receive. The value chain breaks an organisation into its activities, so that you can ask of each one: does this make the customer pay more, or does it reduce our cost? If neither, why do we do it?

A coffee bean is worth very little. Roasted, ground, brewed and served in a nice café, it is worth much more. Each step adds value.

Primary activities: making and selling

ActivityMeaningWatchmakerHospital
Inbound logisticsReceiving and storing inputsReceiving and storing partsReceiving drugs and supplies
OperationsTurning inputs into the product or serviceAssembling each watchSurgery, diagnosis, care
Outbound logisticsGetting it to the customerInsured delivery to shopsDischarge, medicines, reports
Marketing and salesPersuading people to buyBrand events, retail displaysReferrals, insurer contracts
ServiceKeeping value after the saleLifetime repairsFollow-up clinics

Support activities: helping the primary activities

ActivityMeaningExample
ProcurementHow inputs are bought (the process, not the goods)Negotiating with part suppliers
Human resourcesRecruiting, training, rewarding peopleA 5-year apprenticeship
Technology developmentResearch, design, IT and know-howDesigning a new movement
Firm infrastructureFinance, legal, planning, general management, qualityBudgeting and governance

How to use it: three questions

  1. Where does value come from? Which activities make customers willing to pay more?
  2. Where is cost too high? Compare with benchmarks, and find waste.
  3. How well are activities linked? Good links between activities can be hard for rivals to copy.
Linkages: the secret strength

Value often comes from how activities connect. Better purchasing (procurement) can reduce defects (operations), which reduces repairs (service). A rival may copy one activity, but it is much harder to copy the whole connected system. IKEA is a useful example: flat-pack design, large stores and customer self-assembly all work together to keep prices low.

Worked example: should Varenne open its own boutiques?

Sara looks at the outbound and sales stages for one $10,000 watch.

Per watchVia a retailerOwn boutique
Customer pays$10,000$10,000
Retailer margin (40%)($4,000)–
Boutique fixed costs ($1.8m ÷ 1,000 watches)–($1,800)
Varenne keeps$6,000$8,200

That looks like a gain of $2,200 per watch. But the $1.8m boutique cost is fixed. If a boutique sells only 600 watches, the cost per watch rises to $3,000.

✅ Find the break-even

The boutique saves $4,000 per watch in retailer margin, so it breaks even at $1,800,000 ÷ $4,000 = 450 watches a year. Below that, the retailer route is better.

Sara also warns that existing retailers may retaliate, and this could put Varenne's existing sales through them at risk. The numbers start the discussion, but they do not end it.

Step 2 · A real business example
Zara

Many fashion brands manufacture far away in order to cut cost. Zara has been known for keeping part of its production close to its home market. This can cost more per item, but it allows new styles to reach shops fast and reduces unsold stock. Zara's advantage comes from linking design, operations and logistics, rather than from having the lowest cost in one activity.

Step 3 · How it may appear in the exam
  • "Use the value chain to assess how the company creates value."
  • Recommending how new technology could improve specific activities.
  • Deciding which activities to keep and which to outsource.
  • Reviewing a process or a business in the public sector.
Step 4 · A model exam answer
Task (8 marks + professional skills)

The Metro Licensing Agency, a fictional government body, issues business licences. Applications arrive by paper and through a basic portal, and 30% are incomplete. The average time to issue is 34 days, against a 15-day target. Each case passes through 4 separate teams. Staff re-key data into 2 systems. Public complaints are up 40%, and the helpline costs 12% of the budget. The government has frozen the budget. Using the value chain, assess where the agency is failing to create public value, and recommend improvements.

✅ Model answer

Summary: the agency's problem is mainly weak links between activities, not a lack of staff. Since the budget is frozen, it should redesign how work flows rather than ask for more resources. As a public body, its "margin" is public value: fast and reliable service at a fair cost.

Inbound (receiving applications). With 30% incomplete, work is rejected or chased later. ✓ Replacing paper with a mandatory-field online form would stop most errors at the start. ✓

Operations (assessment). At 34 days, the agency takes over twice its 15-day target. ✓ Four separate teams and two systems create waiting time and re-keying, which add cost but no value to the applicant. ✓ A single system and one case owner would reduce handovers. ✓

Service (helpline). At 12% of the budget, the helpline is largely a cost of poor operations, as many calls are likely to be "where is my licence?" ✓ This should be confirmed by analysing call data. An online tracker could then release funds. ✓

Support (technology and HR). Investment in technology is the key enabler, but staff will need training and reassurance about changes to their roles. ✓

Priority. Start with the online form: it is fairly low cost, and it fixes the 30% error rate that creates work downstream. Savings on the helpline can help fund the wider system.

Marks: about 8 of 8. The public sector version keeps the model, but replaces profit with public value and the 3 E's.

Step 5 · What the examiner wants
  • Analyse activities that matter. You do not need all nine.
  • Explain value: say how each point affects cost, customer value or both.
  • Use linkages. Showing how one activity affects another is strong analysis.
  • Be practical. Give specific improvements that fit the budget and the culture.
  • Use the numbers. Translate gaps into money where you can.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Sorting case facts into headings without saying why they matter.
  • Thinking that procurement means the goods bought, rather than the buying process. Delivery and storage of the goods belong to inbound logistics.
  • Treating support activities as unimportant. They can be a major source of advantage.
  • Forgetting service businesses. "Operations" can be a consultation or a claim, not a factory.
  • Ignoring the rest of the industry. Value also depends on suppliers and partners (Section 12).
Step 7 · Memory tips
🧠 Memory tips
  • Primary: "I Often Order Mint Sweets": Inbound, Operations, Outbound, Marketing, Service. Think of the journey from delivery door to happy customer.
  • Support: "Please Hire Talented Individuals": Procurement, HR, Technology, Infrastructure.
  • Three questions: Value? Cost? Links?

Value Networks

Step 1 · In simple language

No organisation does everything itself. A value network is the whole set of organisations that work together to deliver a product or service to the customer. The value chain is one firm's activities. The value network connects your chain with those of your suppliers, partners, distributors and customers.

Think of a wedding. The couple sees one lovely day, but behind it are the hotel, florist, caterer, photographer and band. If the caterer fails, the whole day suffers. Everyone in the network shares the outcome.

The main questions to ask

  1. Where is value captured? Which player keeps most of the profit?
  2. What should we do ourselves? Keep core activities in-house and consider partners for the rest.
  3. Who has the power in the network? Is any partner impossible to replace?
  4. How do we manage the relationships? Think contracts, data sharing, trust and performance measures.

Keep, buy or partner?

OptionWhen it makes senseMain risk
Keep in-houseThe activity is a source of competitive advantageHigher cost; less flexibility
OutsourceIt is not core, and specialists can do it better or cheaperLoss of control and skills; data risk
Partner or allyBoth sides gain, and each brings something the other lacksConflict; sharing of knowledge
Acquire (integrate)Supply is critical and at riskCost; a loss of focus
Varenne

Sara maps the network: part supplier → Varenne → retail groups → customers, with insurers, authorised repairers and online resale sites alongside. She finds that a growing resale market earns money from Varenne's watches, while Varenne earns nothing from it. Her idea is a certified pre-owned service. It would protect the brand, keep customers connected to Varenne and capture value that is now lost to others.

Step 2 · A real business example
Smartphones and platforms

Apple designs its phones, while specialist partners manufacture and assemble them, and independent developers create apps for its store. Apple focuses on design, software and brand, and captures a large share of the profit. Similarly, Uber links drivers and riders without owning the cars. Many modern businesses do not own much of the network, but they control the customer relationship.

Step 3 · How it may appear in the exam
  • Advising on outsourcing or partnering. This links to syllabus section H1.
  • Assessing the risk of relying on one partner, such as a cloud provider.
  • Using technology to connect suppliers and customers (Section E).
  • Evaluating removal of a middleman, or a move to direct sales.
Step 4 · A model exam answer
Task (6 marks + professional skills)

Lumio, a fictional software company, sells scheduling software to mid-sized firms. It hosts everything on one cloud provider and sells 45% of licences via local IT resellers, which also install the software and train users. Resellers keep 30% of licence revenue. The CEO wants to cancel all resellers and "sell direct to keep the 30%". Advise the CEO on the proposal, considering Lumio's value network.

✅ Model answer

Advice: keeping the 30% looks attractive, but I recommend against removing all the resellers at once. A phased approach would be safer.

Resellers do more than sell. They perform the installation and training that customers need, and they own the local relationship for 45% of licences. ✓ Cancelling them would leave a gap in the value network. ✓ The 30% is therefore a payment for services, not pure profit. Lumio would need to build its own sales, implementation and support teams, and we need to know if this would cost less than 30%. ✓

There is a further risk. Resellers could move customers to a competitor's product, putting a significant share of revenue at risk. ✓ Direct sales would, however, give Lumio better customer data and more control of the customer relationship.

I recommend that Lumio tests direct sales in one region or for new customers, while keeping resellers where relationships are strong. ✓ The board should also review its dependence on one cloud provider, which is a separate weak point in the network. ✓

Marks: about 6 of 6. It challenges the CEO's view politely and shows that the network carries value that the CEO is ignoring.

Step 5 · What the examiner wants
  • Look beyond the company's boundaries.
  • Identify who holds the power and who captures the value.
  • Balance control against cost and flexibility.
  • Link to risk, contracts, data security and governance of partners.
  • Since this outcome is Level 3, end with clear advice.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Treating "value chain" and "value network" as the same thing.
  • Assuming that outsourcing always saves money. Include management costs and loss of skills.
  • Forgetting that partners can also become competitors.
  • Ignoring any effect on service quality and on staff.
Step 7 · Memory tips
🧠 Memory tips
  • Chain = one firm. Network = the whole wedding.
  • Who, What, Where, How: Who has power? What do we keep? Where is value captured? How do we manage relationships?

Opportunities and Threats

Step 1 · In simple language

This is where you bring your external analysis together. An opportunity is an outside development that the organisation could benefit from. A threat is an outside development that could harm it. They feed directly into SWOT, which you will study in the next chapter.

⚠ The key rule

Opportunities and threats exist outside the organisation. Strengths and weaknesses exist inside. "Our IT is old" is a weakness. "A rival has launched a better app" is a threat.

The same event can be both. A new AI tool is an opportunity if you adopt it early, and a threat if a competitor does.

How to judge them

A long list has little value. Prioritise using three questions:

  1. How big is the impact? Estimate it in money where possible.
  2. How likely is it?
  3. How soon will it happen?

Step 2 · A real business example
Netflix and Blockbuster

Faster home broadband was a threat to video rental shops, but it was an opportunity for a firm willing to deliver films online. Both firms saw the same outside change. What differed was how they responded. The lesson is that a threat is not fixed. It can be turned into an opportunity if the organisation has the capability and will to act.

Step 3 · How it may appear in the exam

You may be asked to "assess the opportunities and threats" from a market change, or to draw on them in a SWOT or a recommendation. Exhibits may include news articles, market reports or competitor information.

Step 4 · A model exam answer
Task (6 marks + professional skills)

BrightPath, a fictional private tutoring firm, earns 80% of revenue from in-person maths and science lessons. Low-cost AI tutoring apps are growing quickly, with one rival charging 10% of BrightPath's hourly rate. Parents' surveys show 72% still prefer a human tutor for exam preparation, but only 40% of those under 14. A new government grant will fund digital learning programmes for schools. Assess the main opportunities and threats facing BrightPath.

✅ Model answer

Overall: AI is the critical issue. It is the largest threat, and it could be BrightPath's largest opportunity if the firm moves early.

Threat: AI substitutes (high impact, high likelihood, now). At 10% of our hourly rate, these apps offer a strong value-for-money alternative. ✓ With 80% of revenue from in-person lessons, we are heavily exposed. ✓ The risk is highest for under-14s, where only 40% prefer a human tutor. As they get older, preference may weaken in the exam market too. ✓

Opportunity: premium human tutoring (medium impact, short term). The 72% preference suggests that exam preparation is a defensible segment. ✓ But survey preference does not prove willingness to pay, so we should test this with pricing data.

Opportunity: a blended model and school funding (high impact, medium term). A blend of AI practice with human coaching could reduce our cost per student. ✓ The government grant creates a new customer group, namely schools, and we should explore this while the funds are available. ✓

Priority: protect exam tutoring, and pilot a blended service in the next 6–12 months.

Marks: about 6 of 6. It ranks the items and uses the case numbers. It also questions the survey.

Step 5 · What the examiner wants
  • Prioritise. Do not write a shopping list.
  • Explain the size, likelihood and timing of each item.
  • Link each item to this organisation's position.
  • Treat external information with care. Ask who produced it, and how reliable it is.
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Listing internal weaknesses as threats.
  • Calling something an opportunity without checking that the firm can use it.
  • Using vague phrases such as "the economy may change".
  • Failing to rank the items.
Step 7 · Memory tips
🧠 Memory tips
  • "O and T are Outside the Tent; S and W are Sleeping Within."
  • I-L-T: Impact, Likelihood, Timing. Rank every item with these three.

Allow 40 minutes with no notes. Write your answer in a blank document before you open the model answer. This case is written in the style of the exam, but it is not an ACCA question.

Your task

Prepare a briefing paper for the board that:
  1. Evaluates the competitive forces affecting Meridian Dairies. (10 marks)
  2. Uses the value chain to recommend the improvements Meridian should make. (6 marks)

Professional skills (4 marks): Scepticism (2 marks) for challenging the CEO's views; Evaluation (2 marks) for prioritised, balanced conclusions.

Open only after writing your answer: what a weak answer looks like
❌ Weak answer (about 6 of 20)

Porter's Five Forces is a model that analyses an industry. It has five forces. Buyers are powerful when there are few of them. Meridian's buyers are retailers, so buyer power is high. Suppliers are farmers. Substitutes are things like oat milk, and these are growing. Rivalry is high as there are three companies. New entrants are low because a factory is expensive. The company should use the value chain to cut costs in all areas. It should improve delivery and reduce waste and spend more on research. The CEO has a good point about the strong brand.

Why it scores poorly: it explains the model, uses few numbers, misses OatGo's way around the barriers, gives no ranking, makes no calculation and accepts the CEO's view without testing it.

Open only after writing your answer: distinction-level answer
✅ Distinction answer (about 18 of 20)

To: Board of Directors, Meridian Dairies
Subject: Competitive position and value chain priorities

Executive summary. Meridian's fall in profit looks structural, not temporary. Although revenue has grown by 8%, operating profit has fallen by 41%, from $44m to $26m, and the operating margin has dropped from 11.0% to 6.0%. Growth is being bought at the expense of margin. Retailer power is the main cause, and it should be the board's first priority.

(a) Competitive forces

Buyer power (very high, and the most serious force). The top three retailers now account for 68% of sales, up from 52%. ✓ Own-label products have risen from 18% to 31% of the market, so retailers are also competitors for shelf space. ✓ This is a form of backward integration, and it gives them a credible alternative to our brand in price negotiations. ✓ The fall in gross margin from 34% to 29% cost about $22m at 2025 revenue (5% × $432m). Retailer pressure is likely to be a large part of this, although feed-cost pressure may also contribute. ✓

Rivalry (high). Three firms running at 78% capacity, with high fixed plant costs, have a strong reason to discount in order to fill their plants. ✓ Dairy is also a mature, hard-to-differentiate product, and own label makes this worse.

Substitutes (medium, rising). Plant-based products are 9% of the category. If growth of 12% a year continues in a flat market, this would reach about 16% in five years (9% × 1.125 = 15.9%). ✓ That is a significant trend, not a fad, though the growth rate should be checked as it may not continue.

Supplier power (medium to high). Four cooperatives supply 85% of raw milk, and milk has no substitute in our core products. ✓ Meridian is therefore squeezed from both sides: higher farmer prices cannot easily be passed on to powerful retailers. ✓

New entrants (medium for niche, low for core). Plants and chilled distribution protect the core market. However, OatGo shows that these barriers can be avoided by using contract manufacturers and selling online. ✓ Entrants are also able to avoid retailers altogether, so they are a threat at the fastest-growing edge of the market.

Conclusion. The industry is becoming less attractive. Buyers are the most important force; substitutes are the force most likely to grow.

A challenge to the CEO's view. The strength of the brand is real, but the data do not support the view that retailers need us more. Their share of our sales and their own-label share are both rising, while our margin is falling. We should test the brand's strength by measuring what happens to sales when promotions stop. Similarly, the claim that plant-based drinks are a fad is not supported by the 12% growth rate. Past success can create blind spots, and this is a warning sign of strategic drift.

(b) Value chain recommendations

1Outbound logistics3.0% of $432m revenue≈ $13.0m
2Operations (waste)2.0% of $306.7m cost of sales≈ $6.1m

Outbound logistics. Closing the gap to benchmark could add about $13.0m, half of today's operating profit. ✓ Meridian should compare keeping the fleet with outsourcing it to a chilled logistics specialist. Any contract must protect the cold chain, because a food-safety failure would damage the brand. ✓

Operations. Bringing waste to benchmark could save about $6.1m. ✓ This is largely within management's control and has lower execution risk, so it should begin now.

Technology development. Spending is one-third of the benchmark. ✓ Moving to 1.5% of revenue would cost about $4.3m a year, and it could be funded from the savings. It would support higher-margin products, including plant-based lines, which reduce dependence on commodity milk. ✓

Marketing and sales. A pilot of direct household subscriptions would test a route around retailers, but it should be small to avoid conflict with them. ✓

Recommendation. Start with waste reduction and a fleet review, and use up to $19.1m of potential savings (before any costs of change) to fund innovation. These benchmarks need confirming, as other firms may not be directly comparable. Cost savings alone will not solve the problem of retailer power, so new products are essential.

Quick Quiz

Say your answer out loud or write it down before opening each one.

1. Strong forces make an industry more or less attractive?

Less attractive. Strong forces take away the industry's potential profit.

2. A train company faces cheap flights on a route. Rival or substitute?

A substitute: a different way of meeting the same need, which is travel between two cities.

3. Give six barriers to entry.

Scale, capital, regulation, access to channels, brand and switching costs (SCRABS).

4. Three supermarkets buy 70% of a food maker's output and sell own-label products. Which force is this, and is it strong?

Buyer power, and it is very strong. Buyers are concentrated, and they also compete through own label, which works like backward integration.

5. Why do high fixed costs increase rivalry?

Firms need to cover those costs, so they cut prices to fill capacity. Any extra sale above variable cost helps.

6. Why might skilled nurses be an example of supplier power?

They supply a critical input (labour). When they are scarce, they can demand better pay or move to another employer.

7. Who is the strategic customer?

The person or group whose decision matters most to the strategy. They might not be the end user: for example, a retailer or an insurer.

8. Name the five primary and four support activities.

Primary: inbound logistics, operations, outbound logistics, marketing and sales, service. Support: procurement, HR, technology development, firm infrastructure.

9. What is the difference between a value chain and a value network?

A value chain covers one organisation's activities. A value network covers all the linked organisations that together deliver value to the customer.

10. "Our customer service staff are poorly trained." Is this a threat?

No. It is an internal weakness. Threats come from outside the organisation.

11. A company pays a 25% commission to a sales agent. The CEO says that selling directly saves 25%. What would a sceptical adviser ask?

What does the agent do for that money? What would it cost to do this ourselves? Would we lose customers or local relationships? Would the agent move to a rival?

12. Give two limits of Five Forces.

Any two of these: it is a snapshot in time, it underplays collaboration and complementors, it looks at the average firm, and industry boundaries can be blurred.
