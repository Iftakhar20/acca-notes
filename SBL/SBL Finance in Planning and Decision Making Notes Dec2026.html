HOW FINANCE IS TESTED IN SBL

Start here: finance in SBL is not FM

The big idea

In SBL you are a senior adviser, not a calculator. The numbers are usually simple (a few ratios, a payback, an NPV the exhibit nearly finishes for you). The marks come from what the numbers mean for strategy: should the board go ahead, how should it be funded, what could go wrong, and what should it do next.

What you will be asked to do

  • Evaluate an investment or project (NPV, payback, plus non-financial factors).
  • Advise on how to finance a strategy (debt vs equity vs leasing vs grants).
  • Analyse performance from extracts (ratios, KPIs, trends) and explain causes.
  • Assess the finance function: outsourcing, shared services, business partnering, automation.
  • Comment on budgets, forecasts and variance reports and whether they help strategy.
  • Spot reporting and tax effects of a decision (leases, goodwill, provisions, tax relief).

How marks are earned

  • 1 mark per well-applied point: a fact from the case + why it matters + the effect.
  • Calculations earn a few marks; interpretation earns most of them.
  • Professional skills (20 of the 100 marks across the exam): commercial acumen, analysis, scepticism, evaluation, communication.
  • SBL gives no formula sheet; discount factors are given in the exhibit when you need them.
  • Always end with a clear recommendation and its justification.
The 4-step finance answer routine (use it every time)
  1. Calculate only what the requirement needs. Show a small, labelled table.
  2. Interpret: what does each number say? Compare to target, prior year, competitor or cost of capital.
  3. Challenge (scepticism): which assumptions are weak? Who prepared the numbers and why?
  4. Recommend: a decision plus conditions (e.g. “proceed, but only if…”).
G1

Finance transformation

01
G1(a) · Discuss [2]

How technology is transforming finance and the finance function

1 · In simple words

For decades, finance teams spent most of their time processing: keying invoices, reconciling accounts, building spreadsheets, closing the books. Technology now does much of that work automatically. So the finance function is moving from a “scorekeeper” (recording what happened) to a “business partner” (helping managers decide what to do next).

Think of the finance function as having four jobs:

RoleWhat it meansEffect of technology
Transaction processingPayables, receivables, payroll, billingHeavily automated (RPA, e-invoicing, OCR). Fewer people needed.
Stewardship & controlProtecting assets, compliance, statutory reportsContinuous controls monitoring, audit trails, faster close.
Reporting & analysisManagement accounts, KPIs, dashboardsSelf-service dashboards, real-time data, data analytics.
Business partnering & value creationAdvising on strategy, pricing, investmentGrows in importance; uses predictive analytics and scenario models.

The key technologies (know one example of each)

  • Cloud ERP: one system for all entities, accessible anywhere, always updated, cheaper upgrades.
  • Robotic process automation (RPA): software “bots” copy rule-based human steps (e.g. matching invoices to purchase orders).
  • AI and machine learning: forecasting demand, flagging unusual payments (fraud detection), reading contracts.
  • Data analytics and visualisation: dashboards that let managers explore the numbers themselves.
  • Blockchain / distributed ledgers: shared, tamper-resistant records, e.g. for supply chain payments or inter-company settlement.
  • Continuous accounting / fast close: reconciliations done daily rather than all at month end.

What this means for the finance function

  • Structure: fewer processing staff, more analysts; transactional work moved to shared services or outsourced (see topic 02).
  • Skills: data skills, systems knowledge, communication and commercial understanding become as important as technical accounting.
  • Speed: management gets information in days, not weeks, so decisions improve.
  • Risks: cyber security, data quality (“garbage in, garbage out”), over-reliance on models, job losses and staff resistance, cost of implementation.
2 · Real business example

A large hospital group used to close its monthly accounts in 12 working days. Each hospital kept its own spreadsheets, and the group finance team spent days reconciling inter-company balances. After moving to one cloud ERP and using bots to match supplier invoices, the close fell to 5 days. Finance staff who used to key invoices were retrained as analysts and assigned to clinical departments, where they now help heads of department understand cost per patient and theatre utilisation.

A second, simpler example: a global consumer goods company uses machine-learning forecasts to predict weekly demand by product and region. Finance stopped arguing about the forecast and started discussing what to do about it (pricing, promotions, production).

3 · How it appears in the exam
  • “Discuss how advances in technology could change the role of the finance function at X.”
  • “Evaluate the CFO’s proposal to introduce robotic process automation.”
  • Often combined with section E (technology) or H (change management: staff resistance, Lewin, POPIT).
Scenario used in the model answer

Westbrook Public Health Authority runs 9 clinics. Its finance team of 48 staff spends about 70% of its time on processing (invoices, payroll queries, reconciliations). Month-end close takes 14 working days. Clinic managers say finance reports “arrive too late to act on and only show spend against budget”. The new CFO wants to introduce a cloud ERP with RPA and dashboards. Staff are worried about job losses. The authority suffered a ransomware attack on its patient scheduling system last year.

Model answer: Discuss how technology could transform Westbrook’s finance function8 marks

From processing to partnering

Westbrook’s finance staff spend around 70% of their time on processing. RPA can take over rule-based tasks such as matching invoices to orders and standard reconciliations, so a large share of that time could be released.1 That capacity should be redeployed as business partners for the 9 clinics, which directly answers managers’ complaint that reports “only show spend against budget”.1

Faster, more useful information

A single cloud ERP removes clinic-by-clinic spreadsheets and should cut the 14-day close significantly. Information that arrives within days is information managers can still act on.1 Dashboards would let clinic managers see cost per patient visit, staff overtime and supply usage themselves, rather than waiting for a monthly pack.1

Risks the CFO must manage

Cyber security: after last year’s ransomware attack, moving finance data to the cloud needs strong access controls, encryption and a tested recovery plan; the board must not assume the cloud provider handles all of this.1 People: staff fear job losses, so the change needs open communication, retraining in analysis and a clear plan for those affected, or resistance could delay the project.1 Data quality: bots and dashboards only work if master data (suppliers, cost centres) is cleaned first.1

Conclusion

Technology can turn Westbrook’s finance team from a slow reporting function into a partner that helps clinics manage cost and service. The CFO should proceed in phases: clean data and secure the platform first, automate processing second, and retrain staff as business partners alongside.1PS: evaluation

5 · What the examiner wants
  • Link each technology to a specific problem in the case (slow close → cloud ERP; processing time → RPA).
  • Show both benefits and risks (cyber, data, people, cost).
  • Explain the change in the finance role, not just the technology.
6 · Common mistakes
  • Writing a technology essay (“AI is a branch of computer science…”) with no link to the organisation.
  • Assuming technology is all benefit. Always cover implementation risk and staff impact.
  • Forgetting that the purpose is better decisions, not cheaper bookkeeping alone.
7 · Memory tip

“Bots do the doing, people do the thinking.” Then remember the risks as C-D-P-C: Cyber, Data quality, People, Cost.

02
G1(b) · Evaluate [3]

Structures for the finance function: business partnering, outsourcing, shared services, global business services

1 · In simple words

Where should finance work be done, and by whom? There are four main options. Most large organisations combine them.

StructureWhat it isBenefitsDrawbacks / risks
Business partneringFinance professionals sit with (or are assigned to) operational managers and help them make decisions.Better decisions; finance understands the business; challenge and support for managers.Partner may lose independence (“goes native”); needs strong commercial and people skills; costly senior staff.
Shared services centre (SSC)Routine finance work from many divisions is moved into one internal centre (e.g. all payables and payroll in one place).Economies of scale; standard processes; easier to automate; still under the organisation’s control.Upfront cost of set-up; redundancies; loss of local knowledge; divisions may see it as remote and slow.
Outsourcing (BPO)An external provider does the finance work under a contract and service level agreement (SLA).Lower cost (often offshore); access to expertise and technology; turns fixed cost into variable cost.Loss of control; data confidentiality; dependence on the supplier; hard to bring back in-house; hidden costs of managing the contract.
Global business services (GBS)An evolution of shared services: one integrated, global organisation delivering finance, HR, IT and procurement services across all regions, often with centres of excellence.End-to-end processes across functions; global standardisation; strong data and analytics; scale.Very complex to set up; cultural and time-zone challenges; needs strong governance; large investment.

Rule of thumb: high-volume, rule-based, low-judgement work (payables, payroll, billing) suits SSC or outsourcing. Judgement-based, strategic work (pricing, investment appraisal, strategy support) stays close to the business through business partners.

How to evaluate the choice (the criteria)

  • Cost: set-up costs vs annual savings; payback.
  • Quality and service: accuracy, speed, SLAs.
  • Control and risk: data security, regulation, dependence on supplier.
  • Strategic importance: is the activity a core competence? (Never outsource what gives you competitive advantage.)
  • People and culture: redundancies, morale, loss of knowledge, resistance.
  • Flexibility: can it scale up or down as the organisation grows?
2 · Real business example

Many multinational groups (consumer goods, pharma, banks) run finance shared services centres in lower-cost cities and moved to global business services models that also include HR and procurement. A typical pattern: country finance teams shrink to a small group of business partners and a controller, while invoice processing, payroll and reporting move to the regional centre. Public sector bodies do the same: several government departments share one payroll and payables centre.

3 · How it appears in the exam
  • “Evaluate the proposal to outsource the finance function / create a shared services centre.”
  • “Advise the CFO on how business partnering could help divisional managers.”
  • Links to H1 (organising: outsourcing, shared services) and to risk (D) and governance (B).
Scenario used in the model answer

Kestrel Foods is an FMCG company with factories in 5 countries. Each country has its own finance team (total 140 staff) using different systems. A consultancy proposes outsourcing all finance work to an offshore provider, claiming savings of 35% of the $9m annual finance staff cost. The finance director prefers creating an in-house shared services centre for transactions and appointing business partners for each factory. Kestrel recently lost market share because its pricing decisions were slow, and a supplier fraud of $0.8m went undetected for a year.

Model answer: Evaluate the outsourcing proposal against the shared services + business partnering option10 marks

Option 1: outsource all finance work

Cost: the claimed saving is about $3.2m a year (35% × $9m). This is attractive, but the figure comes from the consultancy proposing the deal, so it should be challenged. Contract management, transition costs and redundancy payments will reduce it.1PS: scepticism

Control: Kestrel has just suffered an undetected $0.8m supplier fraud. Handing payables to a third party without first fixing controls could make this worse, because Kestrel would have less visibility of payments.1

Strategy: outsourcing all finance includes pricing and decision support. Slow pricing decisions have already cost market share; an offshore provider with no knowledge of local markets is unlikely to improve this, and may make it worse.1 Outsourcing is also hard to reverse once in-house expertise has gone.1

Option 2: shared services centre plus business partners

Moving transactions for 5 countries into one in-house centre on one system creates economies of scale and standard processes, while Kestrel keeps control of data and payments.1 Standard processes make it easier to introduce segregation of duties and automated payment checks, directly addressing the fraud weakness.1

Business partners at each factory would support faster, better-informed pricing, which tackles the main strategic problem.1 Savings will be lower and slower than outsourcing, and the set-up needs investment in one ERP, so Kestrel should prepare a business case with realistic costs.1

Recommendation

Kestrel should reject full outsourcing and adopt Option 2. Once the shared services centre is stable and controls are proven, it could later outsource selected high-volume tasks (e.g. invoice scanning) under strict SLAs.1 This balances cost saving with control and puts finance where it adds most value: pricing and decisions.1PS: commercial acumen

5 · What the examiner wants
  • Evaluate means benefits and drawbacks, then a judgement.
  • Use the numbers in the case (savings claims) and question who produced them.
  • Separate transactional work from value-adding work; they need different solutions.
6 · Common mistakes
  • Treating shared services and outsourcing as the same thing. SSC is internal; outsourcing is external.
  • Listing generic pros and cons without linking to the case facts.
  • Not giving a recommendation, or recommending without conditions.
7 · Memory tip

“Routine goes to the centre, judgement stays with the business.” Evaluate using C-Q-C-S-P-F: Cost, Quality, Control, Strategic importance, People, Flexibility.

G2

Financial analysis and decision-making techniques

03
G2(a) · Determine [2]

Overall investment requirements of the organisation

1 · In simple words

Before deciding how to raise money, the board must know how much money the strategy needs and when. A strategy needs cash for more than the obvious new building.

What needs funding

  • Growth capital expenditure: new sites, machines, systems, acquisitions.
  • Maintenance (replacement) capex: keeping existing assets working.
  • Working capital: more sales usually means more inventory and receivables tied up.
  • Start-up and launch costs: recruitment, training, marketing, IT.
  • Contingency: projects overrun (see projects, H6).
  • Debt repayments and dividends due in the same period.

How to work out the funding gap

Total investment needed − internally generated funds = external finance required

Internally generated funds = operating cash flow after tax, interest and dividends (retained cash).

Also check timing: money may be needed in year 1 even if profits arrive in year 3. A cash flow forecast shows the peak funding need.

2 · Real business example

When a supermarket chain opens new stores, it needs the building and fit-out, but also stock to fill the shelves before a single sale is made. Fast-growing businesses often fail not because they are unprofitable but because growth absorbs cash faster than it is generated (overtrading). Airlines ordering new aircraft plan funding years ahead because deliveries and pre-delivery payments follow a fixed schedule.

3 · How it appears in the exam

An exhibit lists project costs, working capital estimates and forecast cash flows. You are asked to “determine the total funding required” or this is the first step before “advise on sources of finance”. Watch for items the case writer has hidden: working capital, maintenance capex, a loan falling due.

Scenario used in the model answer

Lakeside Hospital Group plans a 3-year expansion. Exhibit figures ($m): new surgical wing 40; medical equipment 15; patient-records IT upgrade 6; extra working capital (drugs inventory and insurer receivables) 3; maintenance capex on existing hospitals over the period 5. Forecast operating cash flow after tax, interest and dividends over the 3 years is 24. The CEO’s board paper says “we need to borrow $55m for the new wing and equipment”.

Model answer: Determine Lakeside’s external funding requirement5 marks

The total need is $69m, not the $55m in the CEO’s paper, because the paper ignores IT, working capital and maintenance spending.11 However, $24m can be funded internally, so the external requirement is $45m.1

The board should not rely on the $24m: it is a forecast and depends on patient volumes and insurer payments. A contingency (e.g. 10% of capital costs) and a monthly cash flow forecast would show the peak borrowing need, which may arise in year 1 before internal cash builds up.1PS: scepticism I recommend the board revises the paper to show a $45m external requirement plus contingency before choosing a source of finance.1

5 · What the examiner wants
  • A clear, labelled calculation.
  • Spotting omitted items (working capital is the classic).
  • Comments on timing and reliability of internal funds.
6 · Common mistakes
  • Adding only the capital items and forgetting working capital.
  • Treating forecast internal cash as certain.
  • Jumping straight to sources of finance before quantifying the need.
7 · Memory tip

“G-M-W-S-C”: Growth capex, Maintenance capex, Working capital, Start-up costs, Contingency. Then subtract what you generate yourself.

G2(b) · Assess and advise [3]

Sources of short-term and long-term finance

1 · In simple words

Money comes from two places: owners (equity) or lenders (debt), plus a few special sources like leases and grants. The golden rule is matching: fund long-term assets with long-term finance, and short-term needs with short-term finance.

Short-term sources (under 1 year)

SourceGood forWatch out for
Bank overdraftFluctuating day-to-day needs; flexibleRepayable on demand; variable interest
Short-term bank loanA known, temporary needFixed repayment schedule
Trade credit (paying suppliers later)Free finance if within termsLose early-payment discounts; damages supplier relationships
Factoring / invoice discountingTurning receivables into cash quicklyCostly; with factoring, customers see a third party collecting
Supply chain financeLets suppliers be paid early via a bankCan hide true level of debt

Long-term sources

SourceAdvantagesDisadvantages
Retained earningsNo issue costs; no loss of control; quickLimited amount; shareholders may want dividends instead
Rights issue (new shares to existing owners)No interest; no repayment; existing control kept if taken upExpensive (cost of equity > cost of debt); EPS dilution; shareholders may not have cash
New share issue / IPORaises large sums; brings new investorsDilutes control; high issue costs; more disclosure and scrutiny
Bank loan / bonds (debt)Cheaper than equity; interest is tax-deductible; owners keep controlMust be repaid; increases gearing and financial risk; covenants; security needed
LeasingUse the asset without large upfront cash; may include maintenanceLease is still a liability on the balance sheet (IFRS 16); total cost may be higher
Venture capital / private equityMoney plus expertise for high-growth or restructuring firmsDemands high returns, board seats, an exit plan; loss of control
Government grantsCheap or free money for public-interest projectsConditions; may need to be repaid if conditions are broken
Sale and leasebackReleases cash tied up in propertyLose ownership and future gains; long lease commitment

Islamic finance (e.g. sukuk, ijara)Access to Sharia-compliant investors; asset-backedMore complex structures; asset must be identifiable

Public sector and not-for-profit funding

Central government funding, grants, public–private partnerships (a private company builds and operates, the state pays over many years), donations, and user charges. Key issues: value for money, political approval and long-term commitments.

The criteria for choosing (use these as headings)

  • Cost: interest rate vs cost of equity; issue costs; tax relief on interest.
  • Gearing and risk: can the business afford fixed interest if profits fall? Check interest cover.
  • Control: will new shareholders or lenders gain influence?
  • Matching: does the term of finance match the life of the asset?
  • Security and covenants: what assets are pledged; what limits are imposed?
  • Availability and timing: can it be raised in time? Is the market open?
  • Effect on EPS and dividends: what do shareholders get?

Gearing = Debt ÷ (Debt + Equity)  ·  Interest cover = Operating profit ÷ Interest

2 · Real business example

Retailers and hotel groups have often used sale and leaseback of their stores and hotels to release cash. It helped in the short term, but when trading fell, the long rental commitments made them more vulnerable. This is why the exam expects you to look at the long-term risk, not just the cash raised. By contrast, many technology start-ups fund growth with venture capital because they have no profits to support interest payments.

3 · How it appears in the exam

“Assess the financing options available to fund the expansion and advise the board which to choose.” Usually 2–4 options are given in an exhibit. You may need simple calculations: interest cover, gearing, EPS.

Scenario used in the model answer

Sunleaf Beverages needs $50m to build a new bottling plant. Current figures: operating profit $30m; interest $4m; debt $60m; equity $140m; 100m shares; tax rate 20%. The plant should add $6m operating profit a year. Loan covenant: interest cover must stay at or above 4.0x. The founding family owns 55% of shares and does not want to invest more.

Option A: bank loan of $50m at 7%. Option B: rights issue at $2.50 per share (20m new shares).

Workings: A interest = 4 + (50 × 7%) = 7.5. Gearing (book values): A = 110 ÷ (110 + 140) = 44%; B = 60 ÷ (60 + 190) = 24%. PAT = (operating profit − interest) × 80%. Year 1 cover uses today’s $30m profit because the plant earns nothing while it is built.

Option A: bank loan

The loan gives the higher EPS (22.8c) because debt is cheaper than equity and interest is tax-deductible.1 The family keeps its 55% control.1 But interest cover falls to exactly the 4.0x covenant in year 1 while the plant is being built and earns nothing; any fall in current profits would breach it, giving the bank the right to demand repayment.11 Gearing rises from 30% to 44%, increasing financial risk in a market where beverage demand can fall with weather and consumer trends.1

Option B: rights issue

No new fixed charges, gearing falls and interest cover improves, giving Sunleaf headroom.1 EPS still rises slightly to 21.3c.1 However, the family does not want to invest more. If it does not take up its rights, its holding falls below 50% and it loses outright control; it could sell its rights to recover value.1 Equity is also more expensive and has issue costs.1

Advice

The loan sits exactly at the covenant limit, which is too risky. The board should either (1) use a rights issue, accepting the control issue after discussing it with the family, or (2) reduce the risk of the loan by negotiating a covenant holiday during construction or by combining a smaller loan with retained earnings or equipment leasing.11 On balance I recommend the mixed approach, if the bank agrees covenant terms, because it protects the family’s control while keeping interest cover safely above 4.0x.1PS: evaluation

Exam tip on workings

State your formula and basis (e.g. “gearing at book values”) in one line under the table. Markers give credit for any sensible, stated method, and it stops you losing marks for a different but valid approach.

5 · What the examiner wants
  • Short, relevant calculations, then judgement about risk, control and cost.
  • Reference to the stakeholders who care (family owners, bank, investors).
  • A recommendation that may be a combination of sources.
6 · Common mistakes
  • Saying “debt is cheaper so choose debt” without checking covenants and interest cover.
  • Forgetting the matching principle (financing a 20-year building with an overdraft).
  • Ignoring the owners’ wishes about control.
  • Writing a list of every possible source instead of assessing the options given.
7 · Memory tip

“Can Good Companies Make Safe, Available Earnings?” = Cost, Gearing, Control, Matching, Security/covenants, Availability, EPS.

G2(c) · Review and justify [3]

Investment appraisal: selecting or abandoning competing investments

1 · In simple words

Investment appraisal answers one question: will this project earn back more than it costs, allowing for the time value of money? A dollar today is worth more than a dollar in 5 years, because it could be invested in the meantime.

MethodHow it worksDecision ruleStrength / weakness
PaybackYears until cumulative cash inflows equal the investmentAccept if within target (e.g. 4 years)Simple, focuses on liquidity and risk / ignores cash after payback and time value
Discounted paybackPayback using discounted cash flowsAs paybackAllows for time value / still ignores later cash
ARR / ROCEAverage annual profit ÷ average (or initial) investmentAccept if above target %Uses profit managers understand / ignores time value; profit not cash
NPVPV of all inflows − PV of outflows, at the cost of capitalAccept if NPV > 0; choose highest NPVBest method: measures increase in shareholder wealth / depends on forecast and discount rate
IRRThe discount rate at which NPV = 0Accept if IRR > cost of capitalEasy to communicate as % / can mislead when ranking projects of different size

NPV = Σ (cash flow × discount factor) − initial investment  ·  IRR ≈ L + [NPVL ÷ (NPVL − NPVH)] × (H − L)

Relevant cash flows only

  • Include: future, incremental cash flows caused by the decision (extra revenue, extra costs, tax, working capital, scrap value, opportunity costs).
  • Exclude: sunk costs (already spent, e.g. a feasibility study), non-cash items (depreciation), allocated overheads that do not change, finance costs (they are in the discount rate).

Abandoning an investment

Ask: from today, is the PV of future cash flows from continuing greater than the cash released by stopping (e.g. resale value) plus any costs avoided? Money already spent is sunk and irrelevant. Managers often continue failing projects because they “have already spent so much” (escalation of commitment). The examiner loves this.

Non-financial factors (always add some)

Fit with strategy and mission, stakeholder impact, ESG and sustainability, reputation, staff and skills, risk, regulatory approval, flexibility (real options: the right to expand or abandon later).

2 · Real business example

Large infrastructure projects (rail lines, airports, hospital buildings) often see cost estimates rise sharply during construction. Governments then face a real abandonment decision: past spending is sunk, so the only relevant question is whether the remaining cost is justified by the remaining benefits. Politically, though, cancelling feels like admitting failure, which is why bias and stakeholder pressure matter as much as the NPV.

3 · How it appears in the exam
  • An exhibit with forecast cash flows and discount factors; you finish or check the NPV and payback.
  • An exhibit where the junior analyst has made errors (sunk costs included, depreciation included, wrong rate) and you must correct them.
  • “Evaluate whether the board should continue with project X” (abandonment).
Scenario used in the model answer

Westbrook Public Health Authority (see topic 01) is considering a new diagnostic imaging centre. Cost $10m today. Net cash savings and income: $3.2m a year for 5 years. Equipment resale value at end of year 5: $1m. Cost of capital 10%. The finance team has already spent $0.4m on a feasibility study and has included it in the cost. Discount factors at 10%: years 1–5: 0.909, 0.826, 0.751, 0.683, 0.621 (5-year annuity factor 3.791). At 20%: annuity factor 2.991; year 5 factor 0.402. The authority’s target payback is 4 years. A private provider has offered to run imaging under contract instead.

Model answer: Evaluate the imaging centre investment12 marks

Correcting the appraisal

The $0.4m feasibility study has already been spent; it is a sunk cost and must be excluded. The relevant investment is $10m.1

ItemCash $mFactor 10%PV $m
Investment (year 0)(10.0)1.000(10.000)
Annual inflows (years 1–5)3.23.79112.131
Resale value (year 5)1.00.6210.621
NPV2.752

NPV is +$2.75m, so the centre adds value at the authority’s 10% cost of capital.1 At 20%, NPV = (3.2 × 2.991) + (1 × 0.402) − 10 = −$0.03m, so IRR ≈ 10% + [2.752 ÷ (2.752 + 0.027)] × 10% ≈ 19.9%, well above 10%.1 Payback = 10 ÷ 3.2 = 3.1 years (about 3 years 2 months), inside the 4-year target.1

How safe is the result?

Annual inflows could fall by 22.7% (2.752 ÷ 12.131) before NPV becomes zero; the cost could rise by 27.5% (2.752 ÷ 10). This is a reasonable margin of safety.1 The result is also not sensitive to the discount rate: NPV stays positive at any rate up to about 19.9%.1 But inflows of $3.2m depend on patient volumes and staffing; radiographers are hard to recruit, and an under-staffed centre would not reach forecast throughput.1PS: scepticism

Non-financial factors

Local imaging would cut waiting times and improve diagnosis, which supports the authority’s public health mission.1 Owning the centre gives control over quality and data, compared with the private provider’s contract.1 However, the contract option avoids the $10m outlay and staffing risk, so the authority should compare its cost against the in-house NPV before deciding.1

Recommendation

The centre is financially attractive (positive NPV, IRR about 20%, payback within target) and supports service quality. I recommend proceeding, subject to (1) a recruitment plan for radiographers and (2) a costed comparison with the private provider’s offer.11PS: evaluation

5 · What the examiner wants
  • Correct treatment of relevant costs (sunk, depreciation, overheads).
  • At least two methods, then interpretation; NPV is the main decision tool.
  • Sensitivity or risk comment plus non-financial factors.
  • A clear recommendation, often with conditions.
6 · Common mistakes
  • Including sunk costs or depreciation in cash flows.
  • Spending 20 minutes on a perfect calculation and 2 minutes on interpretation (the reverse of the mark split).
  • Stating “NPV is positive, accept” with no comment on how reliable the forecasts are.
  • Defending a failing project because of money already spent.
7 · Memory tip

“Future, Incremental, Cash.” If a flow is not all three, leave it out. And “sunk is sunk”: yesterday’s spending never changes today’s decision.

G2(d) · Justify [3]

Decisions under risk and uncertainty

1 · In simple words

Risk: we can estimate the possible outcomes and their probabilities (e.g. past data says demand is high 3 years in 10). Uncertainty: we cannot sensibly put probabilities on outcomes (e.g. a brand-new technology). Every forecast in an NPV is a guess, so the board must know how wrong it could be and what happens then.

TechniqueWhat it doesBest used whenLimitation

1 · In simple words

A strategic decision does not only change cash. It changes how the results look in the published accounts and how much tax is paid. Investors, lenders (covenants) and managers (bonuses) all react to the reported numbers. You need broad awareness, not detailed accounting.

2 · Real business example

When IFRS 16 came into force in 2019, airlines and retailers with large lease portfolios saw billions of dollars of lease liabilities added to their balance sheets overnight. Nothing about their cash had changed, but reported gearing rose, which affected covenants and credit ratings. Several multinationals have also faced public criticism for routing profits through low-tax jurisdictions, even where this was legal.

3 · How it appears in the exam

Usually a few marks inside a bigger requirement: “…including the financial reporting and tax implications”. Or the CEO suggests a way to “make the results look better” and you must advise, linking to ethics and governance.

Scenario used in the model answer

Kestrel Foods (topic 02) is considering: (1) buying Crunchwell, a snack brand, for $100m; the fair value of Crunchwell’s net assets is $70m. Acquisition of Crunchwell

Financing with debt

The loan increases liabilities and gearing, so the board should model gearing after the deal against the 50% covenant.1 Interest on the loan is normally tax-deductible, reducing its after-tax cost.1

  • Broad, correct principles linked to the decision, not detailed standards.
  • Effects on key users: covenants, EPS, investors.
  • An ethical stance if the case hints at manipulation.
6 · Common mistakes
  • Writing pages of IFRS detail (this is SBL, not SBR).
  • Saying leasing keeps debt “off balance sheet” (no longer true under IFRS 16 for most leases).
  • Missing the ethics angle when the CEO wants to “manage” profits.

For any decision ask “B-P-T-E”: what happens to the Balance sheet, the Profit, the Tax bill, and is it Ethical?

G2(f) · Assess [3]

Assessing performance and position: KPIs, ratios and performance management techniques

1 · In simple words

Ratios turn raw numbers into a story: Is the business profitable? Is it using its assets well? Can it pay its bills? Is it taking on too much debt? One ratio on its own means little; you need a comparison (last year, budget, competitor, industry) and a reason from the case.

Profitability

  • Gross margin = Gross profit ÷ Revenue
  • Operating margin = Operating profit ÷ Revenue
  • ROCE = Operating profit ÷ Capital employed
  • Asset turnover = Revenue ÷ Capital employed

ROCE = margin × asset turnover. Use this to explain why ROCE changed.

Liquidity and efficiency

  • Current ratio = Current assets ÷ Current liabilities
  • Quick ratio = (Current assets − Inventory) ÷ Current liabilities
  • Receivable days = Receivables ÷ Revenue × 365
  • Inventory days = Inventory ÷ Cost of sales × 365
  • Payable days = Payables ÷ Cost of sales (or purchases) × 365

Gearing and investor

  • Gearing = Debt ÷ (Debt + Equity)
  • Interest cover = Operating profit ÷ Interest
  • EPS = Profit after tax ÷ Number of shares
  • P/E = Share price ÷ EPS
  • Dividend cover = EPS ÷ Dividend per share

Beyond ratios: frameworks

  • Balanced scorecard (Kaplan & Norton): Financial, Customer, Internal process, Learning & growth. Links KPIs to strategy.
  • Building blocks (Fitzgerald & Moon, for service businesses): dimensions (results and determinants), standards, rewards.
  • Value for money (public sector): Economy, Efficiency, Effectiveness (3Es).
  • Non-financial KPIs: customer satisfaction, quality, waiting times, staff turnover, emissions.

Good KPIs are SMART and linked to critical success factors

Critical success factor (CSF) = what the organisation must get right. KPI = how we measure it. Example: CSF “fast emergency care” → KPI “% of patients seen within 4 hours”. Watch for dysfunctional behaviour (managers hitting the target while harming the real goal) and too many KPIs.

2 · Real business example

Hospitals measured only on waiting-time targets have sometimes kept patients in ambulances outside the emergency department so the “clock” did not start: the KPI improved while care did not. This is classic dysfunctional behaviour, and it is why SBL expects a balanced set of financial and non-financial measures.

3 · How it appears in the exam

An exhibit with two years of financial data. “Assess the financial performance and position of X” or “Evaluate the KPIs currently used and recommend improvements”. Marks are mostly for explaining causes and consequences, not the arithmetic.

G3(a) · Discuss [3]

The continuing strategic need for cost management and control

1 · In simple words

Even in a digital, data-rich world, organisations still need to know what things really cost and keep costs under control. Why? Because cost decides price, profit and survival. A cost leader (Porter) cannot exist without excellent cost control. A public hospital with a fixed budget can only treat more patients if each treatment costs less.

Why cost management matters strategically

  • Competitive strategy: cost leadership needs the lowest cost; differentiation still needs costs below the premium customers pay.
  • Pricing: you cannot price profitably if you do not know the cost per product, customer or service.
  • Public sector: fixed funding and value-for-money duties (economy, efficiency, effectiveness).
  • Pressure on margins: inflation, powerful buyers (supermarkets, insurers), new low-cost entrants.
  • Sustainability: energy, waste and carbon are costs too, and increasingly regulated and taxed.
  • Control and accountability: managers need cost responsibility and feedback.

Modern cost management techniques (know what each is for)

TechniqueSimple ideaStrategic use
Target costingStart with the market price, deduct the required profit → target cost; then design the product to meet itNew products in competitive markets

Cost of qualityPrevention + Appraisal (cost of conformance) vs Internal failure + External failure (cost of non-conformance)Spending on prevention to cut expensive failures

Target cost = Target price − Required profit  ·  Cost gap = Estimated cost − Target cost

Quick example: a home health monitor will sell for $50 in a crowded market. The company needs a 30% margin, so target cost = $50 − $15 = $35. The current design costs $41: a cost gap of $6 to close through redesign, cheaper components or supplier negotiation, before launch.

2 · Real business example

Japanese car makers made target costing famous: the selling price of a new model is set from market research first, and engineers must design the car to hit the target cost. Low-cost airlines are another example: everything from seat pitch to turnaround time is managed to keep cost per seat-kilometre below competitors’.

3 · How it appears in the exam
  • “Discuss, from a strategic perspective, why X needs effective cost management.”
  • A case where the current costing system (one overhead rate) gives misleading product or service costs and leads to bad pricing decisions.
  • Public sector: budget cuts and a need to show value for money.

G3(b) · Evaluate [3]

Forecasting, budgeting, standard costing and variance analysis

1 · In simple words

A forecast is what we think will happen. A budget is the plan we commit to (and hold managers accountable for). A standard cost is what one unit should cost. A variance is the difference between what should have happened and what did. In SBL the question is always: do these tools still help strategy, or do they get in the way?

Forecasting methods

  • Time series / trend analysis: project past patterns (trend + seasonal variation).
  • Regression and the high-low method: link cost to activity. Example: 8,000 visits cost $520k; 12,000 cost $680k → variable cost = $160k ÷ 4,000 = $40 per visit; fixed = $520k − $320k = $200k. Forecast for 15,000 visits: $200k + $600k = $800k.
  • Predictive analytics / machine learning: many variables, large data sets (links to E).
  • Judgement / Delphi / scenario planning: where there is no history.

Budgeting approaches

ApproachHow it worksAdvantagesDisadvantages
IncrementalLast year + or − a percentageQuick, simple, stableBuilds in past waste; “spend it or lose it”; no link to strategy
Zero-based (ZBB)Every activity justified from zero each period; ranked by priorityRemoves waste; links spend to objectives; good for public sector and support functionsTime-consuming; needs skills; managers may resist
Rolling budgets / forecastsUpdated regularly (e.g. each quarter adds a new quarter)Always current; good in fast-changing marketsEffort; targets keep moving
Activity-based budgetingBudget by activities and cost driversBetter understanding of what causes costComplex
Flexible budgetsBudget is adjusted to actual activity level before comparisonFair comparison; better variancesNeeds fixed/variable split
Beyond budgetingReplace fixed annual budgets with relative targets, rolling forecasts and devolved decisionsAgile; reduces gamingBig cultural change; loss of traditional control

Behavioural side of budgets

Participation (bottom-up) improves motivation but invites budgetary slack (padding). Imposed (top-down) budgets are quicker but may demotivate. Targets should be challenging but achievable.

Standard costing and variances

  • Material: price variance = (standard price − actual price) × actual quantity; usage variance = (standard qty for actual output − actual qty) × standard price.
  • Labour: rate variance = (standard rate − actual rate) × actual hours; efficiency variance = (standard hours for actual output − actual hours) × standard rate.
  • Sales: price and volume variances.
  • F = favourable (better than standard), A = adverse.

Is standard costing still useful? Yes for repetitive, standardised work (manufacturing, lab tests, call centres). Less useful where products are customised, life cycles are short, or the focus is quality and continuous improvement (a fixed standard can encourage “good enough”). Variances also need investigating for causes, and variances are often linked (cheap material → more waste).

2 · Real business example

Some large organisations have moved from annual budgets to rolling quarterly forecasts because markets change faster than a 12-month plan can follow. Many governments have applied zero-based reviews to departmental spending during periods of austerity, forcing each programme to justify its existence. Fast-food chains still use standard costs heavily: each item has a standard recipe and cost, and food waste variances are tracked daily.

3 · How it appears in the exam
  • “Evaluate the current budgeting process at X and recommend improvements.”
  • An exhibit with a variance report; “assess what the report tells the board and whether the standard costing system is appropriate”.
  • Links to governance (accountability), people (motivation) and technology (forecasting with analytics).

Result. NPV is +$0.84m and payback is exactly 4 years, so on the director’s figures the centre just meets both targets.111

Margin of safety. The NPV is very small relative to the investment. Operating inflows (PV $22.977m) need to fall by only 3.7% (0.84 ÷ 22.977) for NPV to become zero.11

Utilisation assumption. The forecast assumes 95% utilisation from year 1, but new centres average 80%. If inflows fall in proportion (80 ÷ 95 = 84%), PV of operating inflows ≈ $19.35m and NPV ≈ −$2.8m. Payback would move beyond 4 years.11

Bias. The appraisal was prepared by the director who would run the centre, which creates a risk of optimism bias. It should be independently reviewed by finance.1PS: scepticism

Demand risk. No insurer has signed a contract; “interest” is not a commitment. Insurers hold strong buyer power and could negotiate prices below those assumed.1

Strategic and non-financial factors. The centre fits the CEO’s vision and could strengthen Harbourline’s reputation and referrals to other services.1 It also needs scarce specialist staff (oncologists, radiographers), which could delay opening.1

Conclusion. The financial case is marginal and rests on an optimistic assumption. The board should not approve it yet. It should require (1) a revised appraisal at realistic utilisation, (2) signed letters of intent from insurers, and (3) consider a phased opening to reduce the initial investment.1PS: evaluation

Option A keeps family control and debt is the cheapest source, with tax-deductible interest.1 Both covenants are met today (6.1x and 36.8%), but gearing headroom is only about 3 percentage points below the 40% limit.1 If the centre underperforms (as part (a) suggests is possible), Harbourline is committed to fixed repayments from a project that may not generate the cash.1

Option B releases cash from an asset that is not used for patient care, and keeps control.1 But it is not “off balance sheet”: under IFRS 16 the lease liability is recognised, so gearing is still about 35%.1 It commits Harbourline to 15 years of rent ($16.5m in total) and it loses any future gain in the building’s value.1

Option C adds no fixed payments and strengthens the balance sheet, which suits a risky project.1 But the family falls from 60% to 45% and loses outright control, which it values, and the fund gains two board seats.1 A 5-year exit target may push for short-term profit, which could conflict with patient care and long-term strategy.1

Advice. Given the weak appraisal, the board should first resolve part (a). If the centre is approved on realistic figures, I recommend option B: it keeps control, keeps both covenants with more headroom than A, and uses an asset that does not support patient care. Model answer (c): finance function proposal (13 marks)

Benefits of shared services. Bringing billing, payables and payroll for 6 hospitals into one centre on one system gives economies of scale and standard processes.1 Standard billing with automated claim checks should cut the 9% first-time rejection rate, speeding up cash from insurers. This is cash the group needs for its expansion.1 Replacing six sets of spreadsheets with one system should shorten the 11-day close.1

Benefits of business partners. Clinical directors cannot see which services make money. Business partners for each service line can produce service-line profitability (ideally using activity-based costing) and support pricing negotiations with insurers.1 This directly supports the oncology decision in part (a), where the quality of financial advice is the problem.1

Financial case. Set-up $3m and savings $2m a year give a simple payback of 1.5 years, which is attractive. But the savings estimate should be checked; redundancy and system costs are often underestimated.1PS: scepticism

Risks. Staff representatives are already concerned. Poor handling could lower morale and cause errors during transition; Harbourline should consult early, offer retraining for business-partner roles and manage the change carefully (e.g. Lewin: unfreeze, change, refreeze).11 Hospitals may lose local knowledge of their insurers, so service levels between the centre and the hospitals should be agreed.1 The migration should not happen during the oncology project’s busiest period.1

Conclusion. The proposal addresses Harbourline’s real problems (rejected claims, slow information, no service-line profitability). I recommend approval, with a phased roll-out starting with billing, where the cash benefit is greatest.1PS: evaluation

Finance cheat sheet

G1 Finance transformation

  • Scorekeeper → business partner.
  • Tech: cloud ERP, RPA, AI/ML, analytics, blockchain, fast close.
  • Risks C-D-P-C: Cyber, Data, People, Cost.
  • Structures: business partnering, SSC (internal), outsourcing (external), GBS (global, multi-function).
  • Evaluate: C-Q-C-S-P-F.

Investment needs and finance

  • Need: G-M-W-S-C minus internal funds = external need.
  • Match term of finance to life of asset.
  • Choose: Cost, Gearing, Control, Matching, Security, Availability, EPS.
  • Gearing = D ÷ (D + E). Interest cover = OP ÷ Interest.
  • Leases are on balance sheet (IFRS 16).

Investment appraisal and risk

  • NPV > 0 → accept; highest NPV wins.
  • IRR ≈ L + [NPVL ÷ (NPVL − NPVH)] × (H − L).
  • Sensitivity = NPV ÷ PV of the variable.
  • EV = Σ outcome × probability (long-run average).
  • Maximax optimist · Maximin pessimist · Minimax regret worrier.
  • Sunk is sunk. Abandon if future PV < cash from stopping.

Reporting, tax, performance

  • B-P-T-E: Balance sheet, Profit, Tax, Ethics.
  • Goodwill = price − FV net assets; annual impairment test.
  • Restructuring provision needs formal plan + announcement.
  • Interest is tax-deductible; dividends are not.
  • ROCE = margin × asset turnover.
  • What → Why → So what → Now what.
  • BSC 4 perspectives; 3Es for public sector.

Cost management

  • Target cost = price − required profit.
  • Life cycle = whole-life cost.
  • ABC = overhead by cost driver.
  • Quality: prevention + appraisal vs internal + external failure.
  • Link to strategy: cost leadership, pricing, VFM.

Budgets and variances

  • Incremental = lazy · ZBB = justify · Rolling = refresh · Beyond = free.
  • Flex first, then variances.
  • Price/rate × actual quantity; usage/efficiency × standard price.
  • Look for linked variances.
  • Watch slack and “spend it or lose it”.
