ACCA Strategic Business Leader · Syllabus area C5

Strategic Choices

You've finished analysing the business (environment, five forces, capabilities, SWOT). Now the board has to decide what to do. This chapter gives you the tools to generate options, choose between them, and defend that choice like a senior adviser.

Syllabus C5 a–f Intellectual level 2–3 Very high exam frequency ≈ 75 min read

0The big picture

Where this chapter sits, and the four questions it answers.

The Johnson, Scholes & Whittington model (syllabus C1) splits strategy into three parts. You've done the first. This chapter is the second.

Strategic positionWhere are we now? PESTEL, 5 Forces, value chain, SWOT
Strategic choicesWhat should we do? ← this chapter
Strategy in actionHow do we make it happen? Change, projects, structure
In very simple language

Imagine you own a small hospital. After your SWOT you know your strengths and the opportunities around you. Now you must decide four things:

  1. How will we win against competitors? Cheaper? Better? Specialist? → Porter's generic strategies, the strategy clock, the 7Ps, lock-in
  2. Which direction should we grow in? Same patients, new services? New cities? → Ansoff, diversification, international expansion
  3. Which of our existing services deserve money? → BCG matrix (private sector) and the public sector portfolio matrix
  4. How do we actually grow? Build it ourselves, buy someone, or partner? → Organic growth, acquisitions, alliances, joint ventures, franchising

Then one final test checks if the chosen option is any good: Suitability, Acceptability, Feasibility (SAF).

Remember the order: HOW → WHERE → WHICH → METHOD → TEST

How to compete (Porter) → Where to grow (Ansoff) → Which units to fund (BCG) → Method (organic / buy / partner) → Test it (SAF). In a real exam you rarely need all five. You pick the one tool that answers the requirement.

1Porter's generic strategies

The basic answer to "how do we beat our competitors?"

In very simple language

Michael Porter said there are only two ways to win: be the cheapest to operate or be different enough that customers pay more. You can do either across the whole market or for one small slice of it.

Lower cost
Differentiation
Competitive scope
Cost leadershipBroad market. Be the lowest-cost producer in the industry. Keep prices at industry level and earn higher margin, or cut price and win volume.
DifferentiationBroad market. Offer something customers value as unique (quality, brand, service, innovation) and charge a premium.
Cost focusNarrow segment. Lowest cost in serving one niche.
Differentiation focusNarrow segment. A specialist that serves one niche better than anyone.
Source of competitive advantage

Top row = broad target, bottom row = narrow target.

Examples you can remember

StrategyEveryday exampleHealthcare example
Cost leadershipBudget airlines (Ryanair): one aircraft type, no free food, fast turnaroundsA high-volume day-surgery centre doing cataract operations in a standardised, conveyor-like process (similar to Aravind Eye Hospitals in India)
DifferentiationApple: design, brand, ecosystem, premium pricesA premium private hospital: celebrity consultants, private suites, short waiting times, concierge service
Cost focusAldi/Lidl for price-sensitive shoppers with a limited rangeA low-cost clinic network serving only blue-collar workers covered by basic insurance
Differentiation focusRolex, FerrariA specialist IVF or cardiac centre known as the best in the region

"Stuck in the middle"

Porter warned that a firm that tries to be both cheap and special, without excelling at either, ends up with no advantage. Customers who want cheap go to the cost leader; customers who want quality go to the differentiator.

Example

A mid-sized FMCG tea brand charges 10% more than supermarket own-label tea, but its packaging and taste are no better than own-label. It isn't cheapest and it isn't special. Its market share drops every year. That is stuck in the middle.

Exam angle — be sceptical of Porter

Modern critics say a hybrid strategy can work (e.g. IKEA, Toyota, Amazon combine low cost and differentiation through scale and technology). Showing you know this limitation earns scepticism/evaluation marks.

Risks of each strategy

Cost leadership risks

  • Price wars squeeze everyone
  • A competitor finds a cheaper technology
  • Cost-cutting damages quality and reputation (very serious in healthcare: patient safety)

Differentiation risks

  • Customers stop valuing the difference
  • Competitors copy it
  • The premium becomes too high, e.g. in a recession

2Bowman's strategy clock

Porter's idea seen from the customer's point of view: price vs perceived value.

In very simple language

Porter looks at the company's costs. The strategy clock looks at what the customer sees: the price they pay and the value they think they get. There are 8 positions, like hours on a clock.

#RoutePrice / Perceived valueExampleWill it work?
1No frillsLow price / low valueBudget motels, basic generic medicinesYes, for price-sensitive segment
2Low priceLow price / standard valueSupermarket own-label goodsYes, if costs are low (else price war)
3HybridLow-ish price / high valueIKEA, UniqloYes, needs scale and efficient processes
4DifferentiationStandard–high price / high valueBranded FMCG (Nestlé), premium hospitalsYes
5Focused differentiationVery high price / very high valueLuxury brands, VIP medical suitesYes, for a niche
6Increased price / standard valueHigher price, nothing extraRaising prices without improving serviceFails unless monopoly
7Increased price / low valuePay more, get lessOnly possible for monopoliesFails
8Low value / standard priceSame price, worse productShrinkflation noticed by customersFails, loses share
Memory trick

Routes 1–5 = "survive". Routes 6–8 = "die". If a case shows a company raising prices while complaints rise and quality falls, call it out: "The company is drifting towards routes 6–8 of the strategy clock, which are failure strategies."

Healthcare example

A private hospital raises consultation fees by 15% but waiting times get longer and patient-satisfaction scores fall. Customers see a higher price for lower value: route 7. Insurers and patients will switch to competitors unless the hospital is the only provider in the area.

3Sustaining competitive advantage

Syllabus C5(c): price-based strategies, differentiation, lock-in and the 7Ps.

Getting an advantage is one thing. Keeping it is harder, because competitors copy you. The examiner wants you to explain how an advantage can be protected.

3.1 Sustaining price-based strategies

  • Genuinely lower costs: economies of scale, experience curve, efficient processes, automation/RPA, cheap supply contracts.
  • Accept lower margins: only works if you have deep pockets (e.g. cash from another business).
  • Win a price war: need the lowest cost base, otherwise you lose.
  • Focus on segments where competitors can't follow: e.g. a no-frills clinic in an area large hospitals don't serve.

3.2 Sustaining differentiation

  • Hard to imitate: unique resources, patents, culture, reputation, know-how of key staff (link to core competences from chapter C4).
  • Imperfect mobility: the resource can't easily be bought or moved, e.g. a top surgeon tied into a long contract, brand reputation built over 50 years.
  • Continuous innovation: stay one step ahead.
  • Re-invest the price premium in R&D, marketing and service.

3.3 Lock-in

In very simple language

Lock-in means the customer could leave, but switching is so costly, risky or annoying that they don't. Often the product becomes the industry standard.

ExampleWhy customers are locked in
Microsoft Office / WindowsIndustry standard; everyone shares files in that format; retraining staff is expensive
Apple ecosystemPhotos, apps, iCloud, Watch all linked; switching means losing purchases and convenience
Nespresso, printer ink cartridgesCheap machine, then only compatible capsules/cartridges
Hospital Electronic Health Record (EHR) systemsYears of patient data, staff trained, integrations with billing and labs; migrating costs millions and carries patient-safety risk
ERP systems (e.g. Oracle, SAP)Data, customisation and processes all built around one vendor
Exam angle

Lock-in has a downside: regulators may see it as anti-competitive, and customers may resent it, which damages reputation. Also, if YOUR organisation is the customer (e.g. choosing a cloud provider), lock-in is a risk to mention: "Vendor lock-in would weaken our bargaining power in future contract renewals."

The 7Ps are the practical tools used to deliver the chosen strategy. The first four are for products; the extra three were added for services (which makes them perfect for hospitals, banks, consultancies).

Exam tip

Never just list the 7Ps. In SBL each P must be applied to the case and linked to the strategy. Write: "Process: the 45-minute average wait in Exhibit 3 contradicts the premium positioning; an online check-in system would…"

4Ansoff's growth vector matrix

The direction of growth: which products, which markets.

In very simple language

To grow, you can sell more of what you have, sell new things, go to new customers, or do both new. The further you move from what you know, the higher the risk.

Existing products
New products
Markets
Market penetrationExisting markets. Grow share with current products: price promotions, loyalty, marketing. Lowest risk.
Product developmentExisting markets. New products for current customers. Uses customer knowledge; needs R&D.
Market developmentNew markets. Current products to new customers, new geographies or new segments.
DiversificationNew markets. New products for new markets. Highest risk.
Products

Top row = existing markets, bottom row = new markets.

One company, four directions

Take a fictional private hospital group, "Crescent Health", with two hospitals in one city.

Ansoff boxCrescent Health optionKey risk / requirement
Market penetrationSign more insurance companies, launch a loyalty-style family health plan, advertise maternity services in its own cityCompetitors may respond with price cuts; is the market saturated?
Product developmentAdd an oncology centre or telehealth consultations for existing patientsCapital cost, specialist staff, regulatory licences
Market developmentOpen a hospital in another emirate or country, or target medical touristsLocal regulations, culture, brand unknown in new market
DiversificationLaunch a health-insurance company or a medical equipment manufacturing unitNo experience; very different skills; highest risk

The risk ladder

Market penetration: known product, known customers
Product development: known customers, unknown product
Market development: known product, unknown customers
Diversification: everything unknown

Whether product development or market development is riskier depends on the case. Argue it from the facts.

Don't forget: "do nothing", consolidation and withdrawal

Johnson, Scholes & Whittington remind us that not every option is growth. Strong candidates also consider:

  • Consolidation / protect and build: defend current position, improve efficiency, maybe shrink to profitable core.
  • Withdrawal: sell or close a unit that destroys value, e.g. a loss-making outlet in a declining area.
  • Do nothing: almost never acceptable in a changing environment (strategic drift, chapter C2), but a useful baseline for comparison.
Exam angle

A typical SBL requirement: "Using Ansoff's matrix, evaluate the growth options proposed by the CEO." Marks come from: correctly classifying each option (1 mark each, roughly), then evaluating each one with case facts (risk, fit with competences, resources, stakeholder reaction), then a recommendation.

5Diversification in depth

The riskiest Ansoff box, so the examiner loves to test it.

5.1 Related diversification

Moving into areas connected to what you already do, so you can share skills, customers, technology or supply chain.

Backward vertical

Move up the supply chain, towards suppliers.

Hospital group opens its own pharmacy distribution company or diagnostic lab.

Forward vertical

Move down the chain, towards customers.

A tea manufacturer opens its own branded tea cafés; a drug maker opens pharmacies.

Horizontal

Move into complementary or competing activities at the same stage.

A hospital adds physiotherapy and home-care services; an FMCG firm adds coffee to tea.

✅ Benefits of vertical integration

  • Secures supplies or distribution
  • Captures the supplier's or retailer's margin
  • Better quality control
  • Barrier to entry for competitors

⚠️ Risks of vertical integration

  • "All eggs in one basket": one industry downturn hits every stage
  • Less flexibility to switch to cheaper suppliers
  • New skills needed (running a lab ≠ running a ward)
  • Internal supplier may become inefficient (no competition)

5.2 Unrelated (conglomerate) diversification

Moving into a business with no link to the current one. Example: a hospital group buying a hotel chain; Tata Group (steel, cars, tea, IT, salt).

Arguments for

  • Spreads risk across different economic cycles
  • Uses surplus cash
  • Exploits management or parent skills (e.g. turnaround expertise)
  • Can work in emerging markets where capital markets are weak

Arguments against

  • Shareholders can diversify themselves more cheaply by holding a portfolio
  • No synergy, so where does added value come from?
  • Management lacks knowledge of the new industry
  • "Conglomerate discount": markets often value the group at less than the sum of its parts

5.3 Synergy: the "2 + 2 = 5" test

Diversification only creates value if the combined business is worth more than the two separately. Sources of synergy:

  • Cost synergies: shared purchasing, one finance shared-service centre, closing duplicate sites.
  • Revenue synergies: cross-selling (hospital patients referred to the group's own physiotherapy unit).
  • Knowledge synergies: sharing expertise, systems, brand.
Scepticism alert

In SBL cases, a CEO or acquisition target often claims large synergies. The examiner expects you to question them: "The projected synergy of $8m a year is not supported by any analysis in Exhibit 4. Research shows many acquisitions fail to deliver expected synergies. The board should commission independent due diligence before relying on this figure."

6National, multinational and global perspectives

Syllabus C5(b): going international as a growth option.

6.1 Why go international?

  • Home market saturated: growth must come from elsewhere
  • Economies of scale: spread R&D and brand costs over a bigger market
  • Follow customers who are expanding abroad
  • Access cheaper resources (labour, materials) or skills
  • Spread risk across economies

6.2 Yip's globalisation drivers

Yip says an industry "goes global" when four types of driver push it there. Use this to judge whether an industry suits a global strategy.

DriverMeaningExample
MarketCustomer needs are similar across countries; global customers; global brandsSmartphones, soft drinks
CostBig scale economies; cheaper locations availableGeneric drug manufacturing in India
GovernmentLow trade barriers, free-trade zones, common standardsFree zones; EU single market
CompetitiveRivals are global, so you must be tooBig pharma, consulting firms

Healthcare delivery is a weak global industry: regulation is national, licences are local and patients want care close to home. Pharmaceuticals and medical devices are strongly global.

Healthcare example

Crescent Health wants to enter a neighbouring country where foreign ownership of hospitals is limited to 49%. A joint venture with a local healthcare group is the realistic route: it meets the law, brings licences and relationships with the Ministry of Health, and shares the capital cost. The risks are cultural clashes between partners and disputes over clinical standards, so the JV agreement must cover governance, quality protocols and exit terms.

Link back

Porter's Diamond (chapter C2) explains why a country breeds competitive firms. Use it alongside this section to assess an overseas market: factor conditions, demand conditions, related and supporting industries, and firm strategy, structure and rivalry.

7The BCG matrix

Which business units or products deserve investment, and which should be milked or sold?

In very simple language

A company with many products is like a farmer with different animals. Some give milk every day (cash cows), some are young and promising (stars), some are uncertain (question marks), and some just eat food (dogs). The BCG matrix tells the farmer where to spend money.

High relative market share
Low relative market share
Market growth
⭐ StarHigh growth. Market leader in a fast-growing market. Earns cash but needs heavy investment to keep up with growth. Strategy: BUILD / HOLD.
❓ Question markHigh growth. Also called the problem child. Small share of a growing market; uses lots of cash. Strategy: BUILD selectively or DIVEST.
🐄 Cash cowLow growth. Leader in a mature market. Generates surplus cash with little investment. Strategy: HOLD / HARVEST.
🐕 DogLow growth. Small share of a mature or declining market. Strategy: DIVEST / HARVEST, unless it supports other products.
Relative market share

Top row = high market growth, bottom row = low market growth.

How to calculate the axes

Relative market share = Our market share ÷ Market share of the largest competitor

  • Above 1.0 means we are the market leader, so high share
  • Below 1.0 means someone is bigger, so low share

Market growth = annual growth of the whole market. Traditionally above roughly 10% counts as high, but judge it against the industry and economy in the case.

Worked example: an FMCG beverages company

ProductOur shareLargest rivalRelative shareMarket growthCategory
Loose black tea40%20%40/20 = 2.02%🐄 Cash cow
Ready-to-drink iced tea30%15%2.018%⭐ Star
Energy drinks5%35%0.1415%❓ Question mark
Instant coffee8%45%0.181%🐕 Dog
How to write it up

"Loose tea is a cash cow (relative share 2.0, growth 2%). It should be managed for cash, with minimal investment, and that cash used to fund iced tea, a star in an 18% growth market, to protect its leadership. Energy drinks are a question mark: with only 0.14 relative share against a dominant rival, the board must decide whether it can realistically gain share. If not, it should exit. Instant coffee is a dog but should not be dropped automatically. It may be needed to give supermarkets a full hot-beverage range, so the board should check whether its removal would hurt tea sales."

BCG in healthcare

Hospital service lines
  • Star: Cardiology, which is the regional leader in a fast-growing market (ageing population, rising diabetes)
  • Cash cow: Maternity, a market leader in a stable market with predictable income
  • Question mark: Telehealth, with a small share in a booming market
  • Dog: Cosmetic dermatology, with a small share in a crowded, slow market

Careful: a hospital may have to keep a "dog" like the Emergency Department because it is a licensing requirement and feeds patients into profitable departments.

Limitations (= scepticism marks)

  • Only two factors. Ignores profitability, competitive strength, synergies and strategic importance.
  • Defining "the market" changes everything: is it the global tea market or the UAE premium-tea market?
  • Assumes high market share = high profit (experience curve), which isn't always true.
  • Labelling a unit a "dog" can demotivate staff and become self-fulfilling.
  • Not designed for public sector or not-for-profit organisations. Use the public sector portfolio matrix instead.

8The public sector portfolio matrix

Montanari & Bracker's version of the BCG for government and not-for-profit bodies.

In very simple language

A government health authority doesn't care about market share or profit. It cares about two questions: Does the public need and support this service? and Are we actually good at delivering it?

Exam angle — link to public sector governance (B6)

Always add the political and value-for-money angle: decisions must meet the 3Es (economy, efficiency, effectiveness), and closing a service has political consequences even if the matrix says "back drawer". A strong answer says: "Although the matrix suggests closure, the authority should consult stakeholders (Mendelow: the public and elected officials have high interest), and phase the change with a communication plan."

9Methods of growth

Syllabus C5(f): build it, buy it, or partner for it.

In very simple language

You've decided where to grow (Ansoff). Now: do you build it yourself (organic), buy a company that already does it (acquisition/merger), or team up with someone (alliance, joint venture, franchise, licence)?

9.1 Organic (internal) development

✅ Advantages

  • Full control over quality and culture
  • Learning: builds the organisation's own competences
  • Cost spread over time; can stop if it fails
  • No integration problems; no acquisition premium

⚠️ Disadvantages

  • Slow: competitors may get there first
  • The organisation carries all the risk
  • May lack skills or licences for new markets
  • Adds capacity to the market, which may trigger a competitive reaction

Best when: the move is close to existing competences, speed is not critical, culture and quality control matter (e.g. clinical standards), or there are no suitable targets.

9.2 Mergers and acquisitions

Acquisition: one company buys control of another. Merger: two companies combine on roughly equal terms.

✅ Advantages

  • Speed: instant market presence, customers, staff, licences
  • Buys capabilities you lack (technology, brand, know-how)
  • Removes a competitor; increases market power
  • Economies of scale and synergies

⚠️ Disadvantages

  • Expensive: an acquisition premium (often 20–40% above market value)
  • Cultural clash: the most common reason for failure
  • Hidden problems: liabilities, poor systems, lawsuits
  • Key staff may leave
  • Competition authority approval may be needed
Why acquisitions fail: write these in exams
  • Overpaying ("winner's curse" in a bidding war; CEO hubris and empire-building, an agency problem, chapter B1)
  • Over-optimistic synergy estimates
  • Poor integration planning, especially IT systems and culture
  • Inadequate due diligence: financial, legal, operational, IT, HR, and in healthcare clinical/regulatory

9.3 Strategic alliances and partnering

TypeWhat it isExample
Joint ventureA separate company jointly owned by two or more parents, sharing investment, risk and profitA hospital group and a tech firm create a jointly owned telehealth company
Non-equity allianceContractual co-operation, no shared companyAirline alliances (Star Alliance); hospital and university research partnership
LicensingRight to use IP for a feeA pharma company licenses a drug formula to a local manufacturer
FranchisingRight to operate a whole business formatMcDonald's, Starbucks; clinic chains such as dental franchises
Outsourcing / partneringA long-term contract for a serviceA hospital outsources laundry, catering or IT to specialists
Public–private partnership (PPP)Government partners with private firms to build or run servicesA private company builds and runs a hospital; the government pays an annual fee

✅ Why alliances

  • Faster than organic and cheaper than buying
  • Share cost and risk
  • Access partner's skills, technology, licences, local knowledge
  • Easier exit than an acquisition
  • May be the only legal option abroad

⚠️ Risks

  • Conflicting objectives between partners
  • Loss of control over quality and brand
  • Leakage of know-how; partner becomes a competitor
  • Profit must be shared
  • Who leads? Governance disputes

9.4 Quick decision guide

If the case says…Lean towards…
"We must move quickly before competitors"Acquisition or alliance
"We lack the technology / licence / local knowledge"Acquisition, JV, or alliance
"Foreign ownership is restricted"Joint venture
"Cash is limited; high debt"Organic, licensing, franchising (others' capital)
"Quality and culture are critical to our brand"Organic (or careful acquisition with strong integration)
"The market is uncertain; we want to test it"Alliance or licensing (easy exit)
Link to other chapters

Growth method decisions pull in finance (G2: sources of finance, NPV, payback), risk (D: ERM, TARA), change management (H5: integrating cultures with Lewin, Balogun & Hope Hailey) and governance (B: board approval, directors' motives). SBL rewards these links. That's what "integrated case study" means.

10Evaluating options: Suitability, Acceptability, Feasibility

The most useful exam framework in this chapter. Learn it perfectly.

In very simple language

Before saying "yes" to any strategy, ask three questions:

  • Suitability: Does it make sense? Does it fit our situation?
  • Acceptability: Will the people who matter be happy with the outcome (returns, risk, reactions)?
  • Feasibility: Can we actually do it? Do we have the money, people, skills and time?
TestKey questions to askTools / evidence
Suitability
"Does it fit?"
  • Does it exploit our strengths and opportunities?
  • Does it fix weaknesses or counter threats?
  • Does it fit our mission, objectives and culture?
  • Does it use our core competences?
  • Is it consistent with our generic strategy?
SWOT, PESTEL, 5 Forces, value chain, mission statement
Acceptability
"Will stakeholders accept it?"
  • Return: NPV, ROI, payback, shareholder value, public value
  • Risk: within risk appetite? Gearing? Sensitivity?
  • Stakeholder reactions: shareholders, regulators, staff, unions, government, community
NPV, payback, ratios, Mendelow, risk appetite, ethics
Feasibility
"Can we do it?"
  • Finance: can we raise the funds?
  • People: enough skilled staff?
  • Technology, systems, licences, supplies
  • Time: can it be done in time?
  • Can we cope with competitor reaction?
Cash-flow forecasts, funding sources, resource audit
Memory trick: "Fit, Folk, Funds"

Suitability = Fit · Acceptability = Folk (stakeholders) · Feasibility = Funds & resources.
Acceptability has its own 3 Rs: Return, Risk, Reactions.

How to use SAF in the exam
  1. Use the three headings for each option (or compare options under each heading).
  2. Every point must use a fact or number from the exhibits.
  3. Be balanced: show the good and the bad.
  4. Finish with a clear recommendation and justification. "It depends" earns nothing.
  5. Don't forget a next step (e.g. due diligence, pilot, further data), which shows commercial acumen.

11Worked case study with model answer

SBL style. Read the exhibit, plan, then compare with the model answer.

Meridian Health Group (fictional)

Background. Meridian is a privately owned group running three general hospitals in one Gulf city. Revenue last year was $320m with an operating margin of 11% (industry average 14%). Its strengths are a strong brand for maternity and cardiology and good relationships with the major insurers. Its weaknesses are high fixed costs and an outdated patient app with a 2.1-star rating. The population is growing at 4% a year, government is pushing digital health, and three new competitor hospitals open within two years.

Exhibit: Email from the CEO to the board

"We have two exciting options. Option A: acquire QuickCare, a chain of 12 outpatient clinics, for $90 million. QuickCare has revenue of $40m, but its profit has fallen for two years. I am confident we will achieve synergies of $8m per year from referrals and shared purchasing. Option B: a 50:50 joint venture with TechMed, a telehealth software company, to launch a virtual-consultation app. Our share of the investment is $15m. TechMed forecasts 200,000 users in year one. Our current gearing is 55% and the bank has said it will not lend above 60%. The family shareholders want stable dividends. Both options are brilliant, and frankly I'd like us to do both."

Requirement (20 marks, including 4 professional skills marks)
As a strategic adviser to the board, write a briefing paper which evaluates Options A and B using the suitability, acceptability and feasibility criteria, and recommends which option the board should pursue. Evaluation Scepticism Communication

Plan (spend ~8–10 minutes)
  • Format: briefing paper → To / From / Date / Subject, headings, short paragraphs.
  • Classify: A = acquisition, market penetration/development (outpatients, near Ansoff box 1); B = JV, product development.
  • Numbers to use: $90m price; $40m revenue (2.25× revenue); $8m synergy (11.25-year simple payback on price); gearing 55% vs 60% cap; $15m JV; 200,000 users.
  • Scepticism targets: CEO "confident" synergies; TechMed's user forecast (it's the partner selling the deal); "do both" is unaffordable.
  • Recommendation: B now, A only after due diligence and if financed with equity, or reject.
BRIEFING PAPER
To: Board of Directors, Meridian Health Group
From: Strategic Adviser
Date: [Exam date]
Subject: Evaluation of growth Options A (QuickCare acquisition) and B (TechMed telehealth JV)

1. Purpose

This paper evaluates the two growth options proposed by the CEO against the criteria of suitability, acceptability and feasibility, and recommends which the board should pursue. Communication

2. Option A: Acquisition of QuickCare

Suitability. Meridian faces three new competitor hospitals within two years. Owning 12 outpatient clinics would give it a "feeder" network that refers patients into its hospitals, strengthening its position in maternity and cardiology, where its brand is already strong. This is largely market penetration, which is low-risk because Meridian understands the local market. ✓ However, it does nothing to fix Meridian's main weakness, its poor digital offering, at a time when the government is promoting digital health. QuickCare's falling profits also suggest it may be losing patients to more convenient models, possibly digital ones. Commercial acumen

Acceptability. The price of $90m is 2.25 times QuickCare's revenue for a business with declining profits. If the $8m synergies are achieved, the simple payback on the price alone is over 11 years, which is unlikely to satisfy family shareholders seeking stable dividends. ✓ The synergy figure is the CEO's own estimate and no supporting analysis has been provided. Acquisition synergies are frequently overstated, and the CEO may have a personal interest in growing the group. The board should not rely on this figure without independent verification. Scepticism Insurers may welcome the wider network, but QuickCare staff may resist integration into a more hospital-centric culture.

Feasibility. This is the decisive problem. Meridian's gearing is already 55% and the bank's limit is 60%. Borrowing $90m would almost certainly breach that limit. The acquisition could only be financed through new equity (diluting the family) or by cutting dividends, both of which conflict with shareholder objectives. Meridian also lacks experience integrating an acquired business. ✓

3. Option B: Telehealth joint venture with TechMed

Suitability. This is product development: a new service for Meridian's existing patients and insurers. It directly addresses Meridian's weakest area (its 2.1-star app) and the government's digital health opportunity, and the JV gives access to TechMed's technology skills that Meridian lacks. Virtual consultations can also lower cost per patient, helping close the gap between Meridian's 11% margin and the 14% industry average. Analysis

Acceptability. The investment of $15m is one-sixth of Option A, so the downside risk is far smaller, which suits shareholders who want stable dividends. However, the 200,000-user forecast comes from TechMed, which benefits from Meridian signing the deal. It is unverified, and it is ambitious for a city where the population (4% growth) and insured base are not given. Scepticism Doctors may resist remote consultations because of clinical-liability concerns, and the regulator will need assurance on data privacy.

Feasibility. $15m could be funded with a small increase in borrowing within the 60% limit, or from operating cash flow. TechMed supplies the technology; Meridian supplies doctors and patients. The main feasibility risks are JV governance (50:50 ownership can lead to deadlock) and the integration of patient data with Meridian's systems.

4. Should Meridian do both?

No. The combined cost of $105m cannot be financed within the bank's gearing limit without new equity, and management attention would be split during a period of strong new competition. Evaluation

5. Recommendation

The board should pursue Option B, the telehealth joint venture. It is more suitable because it tackles Meridian's main weakness and fits the government's direction. It is more acceptable because the downside risk is lower, protecting dividends. And unlike Option A, it is feasible within current borrowing limits.

Before signing, the board should:

  • commission an independent market study to test the 200,000-user forecast;
  • agree a JV contract covering deadlock resolution, data ownership, clinical governance and an exit clause;
  • run a 6-month pilot with maternity and cardiology follow-up appointments, where Meridian's brand is strongest.

Option A should not proceed at this time. If the board wishes to keep it under review, independent due diligence should verify the synergy estimate and QuickCare's reasons for declining profits, and the deal should be revisited only if equity financing acceptable to the family shareholders is available. Commercial acumen

Why this answer scores well
  • Uses every number in the exhibit (gearing, price, revenue, synergies, users, margin).
  • Questions who produced each forecast and why they might be biased: that's scepticism.
  • Deals with the CEO's "do both" remark: a classic examiner trap.
  • Gives a clear, justified recommendation plus practical next steps.
  • Format fits the requested document (briefing paper) and the audience (board).

Estimated mark: 14–15 technical out of 16, and 3.5–4 professional skills out of 4.

12How this chapter is examined

What the examiner actually wants to see.

Typical requirement wording

  • "Evaluate the proposed strategic options using the suitability, acceptability and feasibility framework."
  • "Advise the board on the most appropriate method of entering Country X."
  • "Using the BCG matrix, assess the company's portfolio and recommend actions for each business unit."
  • "Assess the public sector portfolio of the health authority's services."
  • "Discuss how the company can sustain its competitive advantage."
  • "Prepare presentation slides with notes evaluating the acquisition proposal."

Examiner expectations

Application over theory

Theory alone earns very little. One mark typically needs: a point + case evidence + consequence for the organisation.

Use the numbers

If the exhibit has figures (market share, growth, gearing), calculate something with them: relative share, payback, % growth, revenue multiples.

Balanced, then decisive

Show both sides, then commit to a recommendation. The examiner has repeatedly said that weak candidates fail to conclude.

Right role and format

If you are "the CFO writing to the board", write like one: report/memo headings, professional tone, no "I think".

Where the professional skills marks come from

Time management

The exam is 3 hours 15 minutes for 100 marks, so roughly 1.9 minutes per mark including reading and planning. A 20-mark task gives about 38 minutes: about 8–10 to read and plan, 28–30 to write.

13Common mistakes students make

Avoid these
  1. Drawing or describing the model instead of using it. Explaining what a "cash cow" is gets almost nothing. Saying which unit is a cash cow and what to do with it earns the marks.
  2. Using BCG for a public sector body. Use the public sector portfolio matrix.
  3. Mixing up Ansoff (direction) with growth method (how). "Acquisition" is not an Ansoff box. An acquisition can be used for market development.
  4. No recommendation, or "both options are good". Always choose, and say why.
  5. Accepting management's numbers at face value. Synergy figures, forecasts and "I am confident" statements are scepticism invitations.
  6. Ignoring finance constraints. Gearing limits, cash and covenants often make an attractive option unfeasible.
  7. Forgetting stakeholders in acceptability: staff, unions, regulators, government, family owners.
  8. Calculating relative market share wrongly. Divide by the largest competitor, not by the total market. And if you are the leader, divide by the second-largest firm.
  9. Auto-killing dogs. Check whether the product supports others or is strategically or legally required.
  10. Generic lists of advantages of joint ventures copied from a textbook, with no case link.

15Practice questions

Try each one first, then click to reveal the answer.

Q1. A tea company that sells loose tea to supermarkets launches bottled iced tea through the same supermarkets. Which Ansoff box?

Product development: new product, existing market (same supermarket channel and customers). The company uses its existing customer relationships and brand, but needs new production skills (bottling, cold-chain), so the risk is moderate.

Q2. Our share of the dialysis-services market is 25%. The largest competitor has 50%. Market growth is 12%. Classify and recommend.

Relative market share = 25 ÷ 50 = 0.5 (low). Growth 12% (high). This is a question mark. Rising diabetes rates mean the market is growing, so the board must decide: invest heavily to build share (e.g. acquire smaller providers or open satellite units) or exit. Recommendation depends on available cash and whether dialysis supports other services (e.g. nephrology referrals).

Q3. A government health department runs a mobile breast-screening unit. Public demand and political support are very high, but the unit is old, often broken, and waiting lists are 9 months. Classify using the public sector portfolio matrix and advise.

Political hot box: high public need and support, low ability to serve effectively. This is highly visible and politically risky. Advise urgent investment in new equipment, a temporary contract with private screening providers to clear the backlog, and clear public communication of the improvement plan. Measure progress with waiting-time KPIs to demonstrate value for money (3Es).

Q4. A private clinic raised its prices by 20% last year. Patient complaints about waiting times rose by 35%. Which strategy-clock route is it moving towards, and what is the consequence?

Route 7: increased price, low value. It is a failure strategy. Unless the clinic has a monopoly, patients and insurers will switch to competitors offering better value. It should either restore service value (reduce waits, improve process) to justify the premium (route 4), or reverse the price increase.

Q5. A hospital group wants to enter a country where foreign companies can own at most 49% of a healthcare business, and where it has no local contacts. Which entry method should it use, and what are the key risks?

A joint venture with a local partner. It meets the ownership law and brings licences, relationships with regulators and local market knowledge. Key risks: conflicting objectives, control over clinical quality and the brand, profit sharing, cultural clashes and deadlock. Mitigate with a detailed JV agreement (governance, quality standards, dispute resolution, exit clause) and thorough due diligence on the partner.

Q6. Give an example of lock-in in a B2B setting and explain one risk it creates for the customer.

A hospital's Electronic Health Record (EHR) or an organisation's ERP system. Migrating data, retraining staff and rebuilding integrations would be very costly and risky, so the customer stays. Risk for the customer: weak bargaining power at contract renewal, so the vendor can raise licence fees or slow innovation. The customer should negotiate long-term price caps and data-portability clauses.

Q7. The target company's CEO tells your board the acquisition will "easily deliver $12m of synergies". Write two sentences demonstrating scepticism.

"The $12m synergy estimate comes from the target's own CEO, who has a clear interest in maximising the sale price, and no supporting analysis has been provided. The board should commission independent due diligence to verify the achievable cost and revenue synergies, and the integration costs needed to deliver them, before agreeing any price."

Q8. Mini essay (10 marks): An FMCG company with high gearing wants to expand into three new Asian countries quickly. Advise on suitable methods of growth.

Key points to include:

  • Organic: full control but too slow for "quickly" and capital-intensive. Not feasible with high gearing.
  • Acquisition: fast and gives brands and distribution, but expensive. Debt financing is unlikely with high gearing. Equity dilution and integration risk across three different cultures at once.
  • Joint ventures: share capital and risk; local partners give distribution and regulatory knowledge. Lower capital need fits the gearing problem. Risk of conflict and know-how leakage.
  • Licensing / franchising / distributors: very low capital, fast, easy exit; lower control and margin. Good for testing demand.
  • Recommendation: a phased approach. Start with distributors or licensing (or a JV where local law or distribution needs it), test demand, then convert successful markets to JVs or acquisitions once gearing has fallen. Use Yip's drivers and Porter's Diamond to prioritise the three countries.

Professional skills: tie every point to gearing and speed, which are the two constraints in the question.
