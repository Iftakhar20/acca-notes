Syllabus E1(a)

1. Technology as a strategic issue

Step 1 – In simple words

Technology is not just "an IT department matter". It is a board-level strategy question, for three reasons:

  1. FIT: technology must support the strategy we have already chosen (for example, a cost-leadership strategy needs systems that cut cost).
  2. OPTION: technology can open completely new strategies (new products, new channels, new markets).
  3. DRIFT: if competitors adopt technology and we don't, we slowly fall behind. This is strategic drift (you met it in Chapter C).

Technology can be a competitive necessity (everyone has it, and you lose customers without it, like online banking) or a source of competitive advantage (you do it first or better than others).

Step 2 – Real business example

Kodak: a Kodak engineer built the first digital camera in 1975, but the company kept protecting its profitable film business. Competitors moved to digital and Kodak filed for bankruptcy in 2012. This is classic strategic drift: the technology existed inside the company, but leadership didn't treat it as a strategy question.

Blockbuster vs Netflix: Netflix used the internet to change how films were rented (first by post, then by streaming). Blockbuster stayed with physical stores and went bankrupt in 2010.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

Typical task: "Discuss why the board should consider adopting new technology", often because a CEO or director in the case is sceptical ("we are a hospital, not a tech company"). You may be asked to write a briefing note or a set of slides with notes.

📄 Scenario – MedCare Clinics

  • MedCare runs 12 private outpatient clinics. Appointments are booked by phone and written in paper diaries at each clinic.
  • A competitor launched a mobile app with online booking 18 months ago. It says average waiting time to book fell from 9 minutes to under 1 minute.
  • In MedCare's latest patient survey, 38% of patients complained about long phone waits.
  • MedCare's IT spending is 0.8% of revenue. The sector average is 3%.
  • The CEO said at the last board meeting: "We are a healthcare business, not a technology company. Our doctors are our advantage."

Task: Discuss why the board should treat technology as a strategic issue. (8 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Briefing note to the Board of MedCare Clinics

Risk of strategic drift. The CEO is right that doctors are MedCare's core competence, but patients now judge the whole experience, including how easy it is to book. The competitor has already cut booking time from 9 minutes to under 1 minute. If MedCare does nothing, it risks gradual loss of patients to a competitor that is easier to deal with. This is strategic drift: the environment is changing while MedCare is standing still.

Customer dissatisfaction is already visible. 38% of patients complained about phone waits. Online booking is quickly becoming a threshold requirement in private healthcare: it won't win patients on its own, but not having it will lose them.

Under-investment compared with the sector. MedCare spends 0.8% of revenue on IT against a sector average of 3%. This suggests the gap with competitors is structural and will widen unless the board makes a deliberate decision.

Technology can support the existing strategy. Digital booking frees reception staff, reduces no-shows through automatic reminders and gives data on demand by clinic and specialty, which supports better doctor rostering. So technology strengthens the doctor-led advantage rather than replacing it.

New strategic options. An app could later support teleconsultations, online prescriptions and follow-ups, which could increase the number of patients each doctor sees.

Caution. Technology should follow strategy. The board should approve a technology plan linked to clear objectives (for example, booking time, patient satisfaction, no-show rate), not buy systems for their own sake. Clinic staff will also need training and support, because moving from paper diaries is a significant cultural change.

Recommendation: the board should put a digital patient-access strategy on the next board agenda, set measurable targets, and commission a costed proposal for online booking as the first step.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Use the facts: the 38%, the 0.8% vs 3%, the competitor's results.
  • Respond respectfully to the CEO's view. Accept what is right in it, then challenge it with evidence (this is scepticism and commercial acumen).
  • Link technology to the strategy and to strategic drift. Don't describe the technology's features.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Listing generic benefits ("technology improves efficiency") with no link to the case.
  • Saying "adopt all new technology", which ignores cost, culture and whether it fits the strategy.
  • Dismissing the CEO rudely. Professional skills marks reward a courteous challenge.

Step 7 – Memory tip

"Tech is a strategy question, not an IT question." Ask three things: FIT (does it support our strategy?), OPTION (what new strategies does it open?), DRIFT (what happens if we don't?).

2. Cloud computing

Step 1 – In simple words

Cloud computing means renting computing power, storage and software over the internet instead of owning your own servers. Think of electricity: you don't build a power station, you plug into the grid and pay for what you use.

Three service models (what you rent)

IaaS – Infrastructure as a ServiceYou rent servers and storage. You still install and manage your own software. Example: renting virtual servers to run your own hospital system.
PaaS – Platform as a ServiceYou rent a ready platform to build and run your own apps. The provider manages servers and operating systems. Example: developers building a patient app on a cloud platform.
SaaS – Software as a ServiceYou simply use the finished software in a browser and pay a subscription. Example: cloud email, cloud payroll, cloud ERP.

Pizza way to remember it: IaaS = you get the oven and cook yourself · PaaS = pizza delivered, you set the table · SaaS = you eat at the restaurant.

Four deployment models (who shares it)

Public cloudPrivate cloudHybrid cloudCommunity cloud
Shared infrastructure owned by a provider and used by many customers. Cheapest and most scalable.Used only by one organisation. More control and security, but more expensive.A mix: sensitive data in private, the rest in public.Shared by organisations with similar needs (for example, several government bodies).

Benefits vs risks

BenefitsRisks
  • Cost: capital spending becomes operating spending, and you pay for what you use
  • Scalability: capacity can grow or shrink quickly (for example, at busy seasons)
  • Access anywhere: supports remote and mobile working and multiple sites
  • Latest software: the provider applies updates and security patches
  • Expert resilience: professional data centres, backups and disaster recovery
  • Focus internal staff on core business, not on running servers
  • Lock-in: hard and costly to switch provider
  • Outages: depends on the internet connection and the provider's uptime
  • Compliance and data location: laws may require data to stay in the country
  • Keeping costs under control: subscriptions rise over time and usage can creep up
  • Security and privacy: data is held by a third party
  • Loss of in-house skills and control; migration risk

Step 2 – Real business example

Netflix suffered a major database corruption in its own data centre in 2008, which disrupted DVD shipments. It decided to move to Amazon Web Services and completed the move in 2016. This let it scale globally without building data centres in every country. It also shows the trade-off: Netflix now depends heavily on one provider.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

Very common: "Evaluate the proposal to move the organisation's systems to the cloud" or "Advise whether the cloud is a suitable alternative to replacing our own servers." There are usually numbers to compare, and someone in the case who is over-enthusiastic or over-cautious.

📄 Scenario – Gulf Specialist Hospitals (GSH)

  • GSH owns 2 private hospitals. Its server room is 9 years old. Replacing it would cost AED 4.2m now, plus running costs of AED 0.6m per year.
  • A cloud provider offers SaaS hospital systems for AED 1.1m per year on a 5-year minimum contract, with an exit fee if GSH leaves early.
  • Health regulations require patient data to be stored inside the country. The provider's standard data centre is abroad. An in-country data centre is available for a 15% premium.
  • Last year the server room had 2 outages totalling 14 hours. Two of the 6 IT staff resigned recently and have not been replaced.
  • The IT manager is worried that "we will lose control of our own systems".

Task: Evaluate whether GSH should move to the cloud rather than replace its server room. (12 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Financial comparison (5 years)

Replace server room: 4.2m + (0.6m × 5) = AED 7.2m Cloud, standard: 1.1m × 5 = AED 5.5m (not allowed – data abroad) Cloud, in-country: 1.1m × 1.15 × 5 = AED 6.325m Saving with compliant cloud: AED 0.875m over 5 years (≈12%)

Even with the 15% premium, the compliant cloud option is about AED 0.9m cheaper and avoids a AED 4.2m up-front outlay, which protects GSH's cash.

Benefits for GSH

Reliability. 14 hours of outage in a hospital can affect patient safety (records, lab results, pharmacy). A professional provider with a guaranteed uptime should reduce this.

Skills gap. GSH has lost 2 of its 6 IT staff. Running an ageing server room with a shrinking team is a serious operational risk, and cloud transfers much of that work to a specialist.

Up-to-date systems and security. The provider patches and updates software continuously, which a stretched team may struggle to do.

Risks for GSH

Regulatory compliance. The standard offer is not an option because patient data must stay in the country. Only the in-country option is acceptable, and GSH must get this in writing in the contract.

Lock-in. A 5-year minimum with an exit fee makes it hard to leave if service is poor. GSH needs strong service level agreements (uptime, response times, penalties) and a clear exit and data-return clause.

Dependence on the internet. If the hospital's connection fails, systems become unavailable. GSH would need a second, independent internet line.

Loss of control. The IT manager's concern is valid. However, control can be kept through the contract, regular service reviews and the right to audit the provider. Remaining IT staff can move into managing the provider and supporting users.

Price over time. The comparison assumes the fee stays at AED 1.1m. GSH should negotiate a price cap for renewal.

Recommendation

GSH should move to the in-country cloud option, because it is cheaper, more reliable and reduces dependence on a shrinking IT team. This should be conditional on: written data-location guarantees, an SLA with penalties, a backup internet line, an exit clause that returns all data, and a phased migration starting with non-clinical systems.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • A balanced evaluation (benefits AND risks), then a clear recommendation with conditions. This is where the Evaluation professional skills marks come from.
  • Use the numbers, even a simple calculation. Spot that the non-compliant option is not a real option.
  • Treat people's concerns (the IT manager) seriously and answer them.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Writing only benefits ("cloud is cheaper and flexible") with no risks.
  • Forgetting data protection and data location rules, which are huge in healthcare and government.
  • Explaining IaaS/PaaS/SaaS at length when the task is to evaluate. Definitions earn very few marks.
  • No final recommendation, or "it depends" with no decision.

Step 7 – Memory tip

Benefits = SCALE: Scalability, Cost (capex → opex), Access anywhere, Latest software, Expert resilience.
Risks = LOCKS: Lock-in, Outages, Compliance & data location, Keeping costs under control, Security & privacy.

3. Mobile and smart technology (Internet of Things)

Step 1 – In simple words

Mobile technology: staff and customers use phones and tablets to reach systems from anywhere. Examples: sales reps taking orders on tablets, patients booking through an app, managers approving invoices on their phone.

Smart technology / Internet of Things (IoT): ordinary physical objects fitted with sensors and connected to the internet, so they send data automatically. Examples: smart electricity meters, fitness watches, hospital infusion pumps, shop fridges that report temperature and stock.

1. SenseA sensor measures something (temperature, location, stock)
→
2. SendThe data travels over the internet
→
3. Make senseSoftware analyses it and spots problems
→
4. ActSomeone (or a system) takes action
BenefitsRisks
  • Real-time information for decisions
  • Remote monitoring and predictive maintenance (fix before it breaks)
  • Customer convenience (apps, self-service)
  • New business models, such as selling a service instead of a product
  • Productivity of mobile staff
  • Security: many devices mean many entry points, and cheap devices are often poorly protected
  • Lost or stolen devices
  • Privacy and consent over the data collected
  • Connectivity gaps in remote areas
  • Data overload with no one acting on it
  • Staff resistance; cost of devices and platforms

Step 2 – Real business example

Rolls-Royce aircraft engines contain sensors that send performance data while the plane is flying. Under its "Power by the Hour" / TotalCare approach, airlines pay for each hour the engine flies, and Rolls-Royce uses the data to plan maintenance before faults happen. IoT turned a product sale into a long-term service revenue stream.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Discuss the benefits and risks of the proposed smart technology" or "Advise how mobile technology could improve the sales team's performance."

📄 Scenario – ColdChain Beverages

  • ColdChain supplies drinks and owns 2,400 branded fridges placed in small grocery shops.
  • Sales reps visit each shop every 2 weeks. On visits, 9% of fridges are found broken or switched off.
  • Management estimates empty or broken fridges cost AED 3.5m of lost sales per year.
  • Proposal: fit IoT sensors at AED 180 per fridge plus a platform fee of AED 60,000 per year. Sensors report temperature, door openings and stock level. Reps get alerts on their tablets.
  • Some shops are in rural areas with weak mobile signal. Several senior reps say they "know their shops" and don't need alerts.

Task: Discuss the benefits and risks of the IoT proposal. (10 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Sensor cost: 2,400 × AED 180 = AED 432,000 (one-off) Platform: AED 60,000 per year If sensors recover only 20% of the AED 3.5m lost sales = AED 700,000 a year of sales

Benefits

Recovering lost sales. Today a broken fridge can go unnoticed for up to 2 weeks. Real-time temperature and power alerts let ColdChain fix faults within a day. Even recovering a small share of the AED 3.5m of lost sales would pay back the AED 432,000 quickly (sales, not profit, so contribution margin should be checked).

Smarter rep routes. Stock-level data lets reps visit shops that actually need restocking instead of following a fixed 2-week cycle. This saves time and fuel.

Customer insight. Door-opening data shows footfall and buying patterns by shop and time, which can guide promotions and where to place fridges.

Better relationships with shopkeepers, who lose fewer sales from broken fridges.

Risks

Connectivity. Rural shops with weak signal may not send data, so the system will have blind spots exactly where visits are hardest.

Staff resistance. Senior reps may ignore alerts. Without changing how reps work and how they are measured, the data will not lead to action.

Security and privacy. Poorly secured devices could be hacked, and shopkeepers should be told what data is collected from fridges in their shops.

Uncertain benefits. The AED 3.5m figure is a management estimate and should be tested before it is relied on.

Recommendation

Run a pilot of about 200 fridges across urban and rural shops for 3 months. Measure recovered sales and connectivity, involve senior reps in designing the alerts, then decide on full rollout.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Link the technology to business results (lost sales, efficiency), not technical features.
  • Scepticism about estimates such as "AED 3.5m".
  • Remember the human side: technology fails if people don't use it.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Describing how sensors work technically.
  • Forgetting that data is useless unless someone acts on it.
  • Ignoring a cheap way to reduce risk, such as a pilot.

Step 7 – Memory tip

Sense → Send → Make sense → Act. Marks come from the last two steps: what decisions improve, and who acts.

4. Big data

Step 1 – In simple words

Big data is data that is too large, too fast and too varied to handle with normal tools like spreadsheets. It is described with the 5 Vs:

VolumeHuge amounts of data (millions of records)
VelocityArrives fast, often in real time (card payments, sensor readings)
VarietyMany forms: numbers, text, images, video, social media posts
VeracityHow accurate and trustworthy it is
ValueOnly worth collecting if it leads to better decisions

Sources: sales transactions, loyalty cards, website clicks, social media, GPS, IoT sensors, wearables, call-centre recordings, patient records.

OpportunitiesThreats
  • Understand customers better and personalise offers
  • Better forecasting of demand
  • Spot fraud and risk early
  • Improve efficiency (for example, staffing to match demand)
  • Find ideas for new products
  • Competitive advantage if competitors are slower
  • Cost of systems and scarce analyst skills
  • Data protection laws, with heavy fines for misuse
  • Poor-quality data leads to wrong decisions
  • Larger target for hackers
  • Information overload
  • Ethical misuse damages trust
  • Competitors who use data better

Step 2 – Real business example

Tesco Clubcard (launched 1995): Tesco used loyalty-card data on what each customer bought to send personalised coupons and decide product ranges for each store. It helped Tesco grow into the UK's largest supermarket. It also shows the threat side: customers must trust how their data is used.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Discuss the opportunities and threats that big data presents to the organisation." Use the 5 Vs as a structure only if they help; marks come from applying them to the case.

📄 Scenario – Riverside Health

  • Riverside runs 3 private hospitals with 1.2 million patient visits per year and 8 years of electronic patient records.
  • Its wellness app has 40,000 users who share step counts and heart-rate data from wearables.
  • The appointment no-show rate is 18%, which leaves doctors idle.
  • The board wants to use data to predict no-shows and plan staffing.
  • Data sits in 5 separate systems with different patient ID formats. Riverside employs 2 data analysts.
  • The health regulator has recently fined two other hospitals for misusing patient data.

Task: Discuss the opportunities and threats big data presents to Riverside Health. (10 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Opportunities

Reducing no-shows. With 1.2m visits a year, even a small cut in the 18% no-show rate frees thousands of appointments. Using 8 years of history (day, time, specialty, distance, past no-shows), Riverside could predict who is likely to miss an appointment, send targeted reminders, or carefully overbook high-risk slots.

Staffing to match demand. The same data can forecast busy periods by hospital and specialty, so rosters match patient demand. This reduces overtime and idle time.

Preventive health services. Wearable data from 40,000 app users could support new services, such as early alerts or wellness programmes. These can build loyalty and new revenue.

Threats

Data quality (veracity). 5 systems with different patient IDs mean the same patient may appear several times. Predictions built on duplicated or mismatched records will be wrong. Data must be cleaned and linked first.

Regulation and trust. Health data is highly sensitive, and the regulator is actively fining hospitals. Using wearable data for purposes patients didn't agree to could breach data protection rules and damage reputation. Riverside needs clear consent and a data protection review before any project.

Skills and cost. 2 analysts cannot build and run predictive models across 3 hospitals. Riverside will need to recruit, train or use a specialist partner, which adds cost.

Security. Putting data together in one place makes it a bigger target for hackers, so security must increase at the same time.

Conclusion

The opportunity is real, but Riverside should first fix data quality and governance, then start with one focused project (no-show prediction), measure the results and expand.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Specific opportunities tied to the case's problems (here, the 18% no-shows).
  • Threats that come from the facts (5 systems, 2 analysts, regulator fines).
  • A practical next step.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Defining all 5 Vs with no application. This earns about 1 mark at most.
  • Assuming more data automatically means better decisions.
  • Forgetting consent and data protection.

Step 7 – Memory tip

5 Vs: "Very Very Varied, Verify Value" → Volume, Velocity, Variety, Veracity, Value. Veracity (data quality) is the V the examiner most likes you to question.

5. Data analytics for strategic decisions (new products, marketing and pricing)

Step 1 – In simple words

Data analytics means turning data into decisions. There are four levels, each more advanced:

DescriptiveWhat happened?
"Sales fell 5% in Q3."
→
DiagnosticWhy did it happen?
"A competitor cut prices."
→
PredictiveWhat will happen?
"Sales will fall another 8%."
→
PrescriptiveWhat should we do?
"Cut price 3% in these 10 stores."
Decision areaHow data helps
New product developmentSpot unmet needs from search trends, reviews, social media and complaints. Test ideas with small trials before full launch.
MarketingSegment customers, target the most valuable groups, personalise offers, measure which channels actually bring sales, calculate customer lifetime value.
PricingMeasure how sales react to price changes (price sensitivity), test prices in some stores, monitor competitor prices, and use dynamic pricing (prices change with demand).

Step 2 – Real business example

Airlines and ride-hailing apps such as Uber use dynamic pricing: prices rise automatically when demand is high (surge pricing) and fall when it is low. Amazon uses purchase and browsing history to recommend products, a large driver of extra sales. Both show analytics moving from "describe" to "prescribe".

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

You are often given a small table of data and asked to "advise how data analytics could support decisions on pricing, marketing and new products". There are marks for actually using the numbers and for questioning them (scepticism).

📄 Scenario – FreshBake

  • FreshBake sells packaged bread through supermarkets. Its main loaf sells for AED 8, with a variable cost of AED 5, and sells 10,000 units per week.
  • A price test charged AED 9 in 4 stores for 3 weeks. Scaled to all stores, sales would be 9,400 units per week. The test took place during a public holiday period.
  • Social media monitoring found 2,300 posts in 6 months asking for a sugar-free loaf. Online searches for "sugar-free bread" in the region rose 60% year on year.
  • FreshBake's app users are 22% of customers but generate 41% of revenue.

Task: Using the data provided, advise how data analytics can support FreshBake's pricing, new product and marketing decisions. (10 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Pricing

At AED 8: (8 − 5) × 10,000 = AED 30,000 contribution per week At AED 9: (9 − 5) × 9,400 = AED 37,600 contribution per week Increase: AED 7,600 per week (+25%), about AED 395,000 per year

The test suggests demand is not very price-sensitive: a 12.5% price rise lost only 6% of volume. However, the test covered just 4 stores for 3 weeks during a holiday, when buying patterns are unusual. FreshBake should repeat the test in more stores during a normal period, and watch competitor reactions, before changing the price everywhere.

New product development

2,300 customer requests and a 60% rise in searches point to real demand for a sugar-free loaf. Analytics can size the opportunity (search volumes by area), and a small test launch in selected stores can confirm actual purchases before full investment. Social media posts can be biased towards a vocal minority, so actual sales in the test are the real proof.

Marketing

App users are 22% of customers but 41% of revenue, so each app user is worth roughly 2.5 times an average non-app customer (41/22 ÷ 59/78). Marketing should focus on moving more customers onto the app, using personalised offers based on purchase history. It should also measure customer lifetime value to decide how much is worth spending to win each customer.

Conclusion

Analytics can move FreshBake from guessing to evidence-based decisions. It should run a larger price test, a controlled launch of the sugar-free loaf, and an app-focused marketing campaign, each with clear measures of success.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Analysis: calculate something simple and meaningful from the data (contribution, percentage change, value per customer).
  • Scepticism: question the sample size, period and source of data.
  • Clear advice for each decision area.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Ignoring the numbers given.
  • Looking at revenue instead of contribution or profit.
  • Treating a small test as proof.

Step 7 – Memory tip

"What? Why? What next? What now?" = Descriptive, Diagnostic, Predictive, Prescriptive. For any data table: calculate → question → advise.

Syllabus E3(a)

6. AI, machine learning, robotics and RPA – the benefits

Step 1 – In simple words

Artificial intelligence (AI)Computers doing tasks that normally need human intelligence: understanding language, recognising images, making decisions.
Machine learning (ML)A type of AI that learns patterns from data instead of following rules written by people. For example, it learns which transactions look like fraud.
Robotic process automation (RPA)Software "bots" that copy what a clerk does on a computer: open emails, copy data, match invoices. They follow fixed rules and don't "think".
RoboticsPhysical machines doing physical work: warehouse robots, surgical robots, factory arms.

Generative AI (for example, chatbots that write text) is a newer form of AI that creates content.

Benefits: speed · accuracy and consistency · 24/7 operation · lower cost · better predictions and decisions · staff freed from boring work for higher-value tasks · easy to scale up.

Step 2 – Real business example

Amazon bought the warehouse robot maker Kiva Systems in 2012. Robots now bring shelves to workers instead of workers walking the aisles, which speeds up order picking. In finance departments, RPA is widely used for bank reconciliations and invoice matching, the kind of high-volume, rule-based work it suits best.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Explain the potential benefits of using AI/RPA to support the organisation's objectives." In public sector cases, link the benefits to the 3 Es (economy, efficiency, effectiveness) and public value.

📄 Scenario – Municipal Services Authority (MSA)

  • MSA is a government body. Its finance shared service processes 220,000 supplier invoices per year using 38 accounts payable clerks.
  • Processing cost is AED 32 per invoice, and 2.5% of invoices contain errors (duplicate or wrong payments).
  • 70% of invoices arrive in a standard electronic format.
  • A vendor proposes RPA with machine learning for invoice matching: AED 1.6m to implement plus AED 300,000 per year. The vendor estimates automated invoices would cost AED 6 each.
  • The vendor also proposes a chatbot for supplier queries. The team currently handles 14,000 supplier calls per month, mostly "when will I be paid?".
  • Government policy says no civil servant will be made redundant because of automation.

Task: Explain the potential benefits of the proposal to MSA. (10 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Current cost: 220,000 × AED 32 = AED 7.04m per year Automated invoices: 70% × 220,000 = 154,000 Saving: 154,000 × (32 − 6) = AED 4.0m per year Less running cost AED 0.3m → net saving ≈ AED 3.7m per year Payback on AED 1.6m ≈ 5–6 months (if the vendor's estimate is right)

Economy and efficiency. The proposal could save about AED 3.7m a year, a very fast payback. This is public money, so the saving supports the economy and efficiency duties of a public body.

Accuracy. A 2.5% error rate on 220,000 invoices means about 5,500 wrong invoices a year, with risk of duplicate payments and fraud. Bots apply the same matching rules every time, and ML can flag unusual invoices for review.

Better service to suppliers. Faster processing means faster payment, which matters to small local suppliers. The chatbot could answer most of the 14,000 monthly "when will I be paid?" calls instantly, 24/7.

Better use of staff. Because no staff will be made redundant, the 38 clerks can move to higher-value work: handling exceptions, supplier relationships, analysing spending and spotting savings. This improves effectiveness and staff development.

Scalability. If invoice volumes grow, bots can handle the extra work without more hiring.

Note of caution. The AED 6 cost per invoice is the vendor's own estimate, so MSA should check it with references from other users or a pilot. Also, the saving will only become a cash saving if staff are genuinely redeployed to useful work.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Benefits quantified where the data allows.
  • Public sector context: 3 Es, public value, staff policy.
  • Recognise who provided the figures (a vendor has a reason to be optimistic).

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Confusing RPA (follows rules) with ML (learns).
  • Assuming all invoices can be automated. Only the 70% in standard format can.
  • Promising headcount savings when the case says redundancy is not allowed.

Step 7 – Memory tip

RPA = hands (follows rules) · ML = brain that learns · Robotics = body. RPA suits work that is high-volume, rule-based, repetitive and uses digital inputs.

7. AI – risks, controls and ethics

Step 1 – In simple words

RiskWhat it means
BiasAI learns from past data. If past decisions were unfair, the AI repeats the unfairness, at scale.
Black boxSome models cannot explain why they made a decision, a problem when people or regulators need reasons.
Errors at scaleOne wrong rule can affect thousands of decisions before anyone notices.

Ethics: link to the ACCA fundamental principles. Objectivity (bias), professional competence (understand the tool before relying on it), confidentiality (data), integrity and professional behaviour (honesty with customers and regulators).

Step 2 – Real business example

Amazon built an AI tool to screen job applications. It was trained on 10 years of past CVs, mostly from men, and learned to downgrade CVs that included the word "women's" (as in "women's chess club"). Amazon stopped using it (reported in 2018). This is the classic example of AI copying historic bias.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Assess the risks and ethical implications of the proposed use of AI and recommend controls." There are often ethical marks for spotting unfair treatment of a group.

📄 Scenario – CareFirst Insurance

  • CareFirst, a health insurer, wants a machine learning model to automatically approve or reject medical claims.
  • The model was trained on 5 years of decisions made by human claims assessors.
  • In testing, the model rejected 11% of claims from one region, against a 4% average elsewhere. The region has a large low-income population.
  • The model cannot show the reasons for individual decisions. The insurance regulator requires written reasons for every rejected claim.
  • The claims team has 120 staff. The COO plans to reduce this to 40 within a year.

Task: Assess the risks and ethical issues of the proposal and recommend controls. (12 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Risks and ethical issues

Possible bias. A rejection rate of 11% in one region against 4% elsewhere is almost three times higher. The model learned from human decisions, so it may have copied past unfairness, and the region's low-income profile raises a serious fairness concern. Automating this would apply any discrimination to every claim, quickly and invisibly. This conflicts with the principle of objectivity and could seriously damage CareFirst's reputation.

Regulatory breach. The regulator requires written reasons for every rejection, but the model cannot give reasons. Using it for rejections would breach regulation and could lead to fines or loss of licence.

Accountability. If a wrong rejection harms a patient (for example, treatment is delayed), it must be clear who is responsible. Right now there is no named owner.

People risk. Cutting from 120 to 40 staff in one year removes the experienced assessors needed to check the model and handle complex claims. Morale among remaining staff may fall, and knowledge will be lost.

Recommended controls

  • Automate approvals only. Let the model auto-approve straightforward claims, but send every proposed rejection to a human assessor, who writes the reasons. This meets the regulation and keeps a human in the loop.
  • Investigate the regional difference before launch. Check whether it reflects genuine claim differences or bias, and retrain the model if needed.
  • Explainability. Ask the vendor for a model, or an add-on tool, that shows the main factors behind each decision.
  • Governance. Appoint a senior owner, add AI risk to the risk register, and report bias test results to the risk committee every quarter.
  • Phased staff reduction. Reduce headcount gradually, based on proven accuracy, and retrain some assessors as model reviewers.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Spot the ethical issue from the data (11% vs 4%) and say why it matters.
  • Link to regulation and reputation.
  • Practical controls that still let the business get benefits. Don't just say "don't use AI".

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Generic "AI is risky" answers.
  • Recommending a total ban. The examiner prefers balanced, controlled use.
  • Missing the people and skills risk.

Step 7 – Memory tip

Ethics = FAT-P: Fairness, Accountability, Transparency, Privacy. Controls = HUMAN.

"Assess the organisation's current approach to e-business" and "Advise how information technology could support the value chain." Using value chain headings gives a clear structure.

📄 Scenario – Al Waha Pharmacies

  • Al Waha has 45 pharmacies. Its website only shows store locations and opening hours.
  • A new online competitor delivers medicines within 2 hours of ordering.
  • 30% of Al Waha's customers collect repeat prescriptions every month.
  • The central warehouse uses paper picking lists. Expired medicines written off cost AED 2.1m per year.
  • Staff rosters for all 45 stores are kept on separate spreadsheets.

Task: Assess Al Waha's current approach to e-business and advise how technology could support its value chain. (12 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Current approach

Al Waha is at the brochureware stage: its website gives information but no way to order, track or interact. Inside the business, key processes (warehouse picking, rostering) are manual. Meanwhile a competitor with 2-hour delivery is attacking the most valuable customers. Al Waha is behind its market and at risk of strategic drift.

How technology could help across the value chain

Marketing & sales: online and app ordering with prescription upload, particularly for the 30% of customers on repeat prescriptions. Automatic reminders ("your refill is due") and a choice of collection or delivery would protect this loyal, regular revenue from the competitor.

Outbound logistics: click-and-collect from any of the 45 stores, plus delivery from the nearest store. The store network is a strength the online competitor lacks, and it allows fast delivery without a new warehouse.

Inbound logistics & operations: a warehouse management system with barcode scanning and "first-expiry-first-out" picking would reduce the AED 2.1m of expired stock. Linking store sales data to automatic reordering would keep the right stock in each store.

Service: online chat with a pharmacist and digital medication reminders would improve care and build loyalty.

HR: a central rostering system would match pharmacist hours to busy periods across all stores and ensure a qualified pharmacist is always present.

Recommendation

Priority 1 is a repeat-prescription app with collection and delivery, because it protects the most valuable customers. Priority 2 is the warehouse system, whose savings on expired stock can help fund the change. Integration between the app, store stock and the warehouse is essential, so customers aren't promised items that are out of stock.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • An assessment (where they are now, and how that compares with competitors), not just ideas.
  • Ideas mapped to real problems in the case.
  • Prioritisation, which shows commercial acumen.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Forgetting the support activities.
  • No prioritisation.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Explore how the 6 I's could help the organisation develop its e-marketing." Use each I as a heading and apply it.

📄 Scenario – Desert Spring Water

  • Desert Spring sells bottled water. Its AED 6m marketing budget goes mostly on billboards and TV adverts. It holds almost no data on end customers.
  • It sells through distributors who take an 18% margin.
  • It is launching a home-delivery subscription app aimed at families across the whole country, not just its home city.
  • Customer complaints currently arrive by letter or phone and take about 10 days to answer.

Task: Explore how the 6 I's could help Desert Spring's e-marketing. (12 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Intelligence: for the first time Desert Spring will know who its customers are, what they order, how often, and where. This data shows which areas and family sizes are most profitable, so the AED 6m budget can be moved towards channels that actually bring subscribers.

Individualisation: the app can suggest the right delivery size for each household, send reminders before a family usually runs out, and make offers based on order history (for example, a discount for adding sparkling water). This raises order value and retention.

Integration: the app must link to stock, delivery routes and billing, so customers see accurate delivery times. Billboards and TV should carry the app download link, so traditional and digital marketing support each other.

Industry structure: selling direct removes the distributor's 18% margin on those sales (disintermediation). Distributors may react badly, for example by giving competitors better shelf space, so Desert Spring must manage the relationship carefully. One option is to use distributors for delivery.

Independence of location: the app can reach families in every part of the country without new sales offices. The real limit is delivery logistics, so Desert Spring should expand area by area as delivery capacity allows.

Recommendation: move part of the AED 6m from billboards and TV to app promotion, and track the cost to acquire each subscriber against their lifetime value.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • All 6 I's applied, each with a benefit to the business.
  • Notice the conflict with distributors, which shows commercial acumen.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

Step 2 – Real business example

United Airlines (2009): a musician whose guitar was broken by baggage handlers posted a song called "United Breaks Guitars" on YouTube after the airline refused compensation. Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Assess the importance of online branding to the organisation and compare it with its traditional approach."

📄 Scenario – Lotus Women & Children Hospital

  • Lotus has built a strong reputation over 20 years, mainly through word of mouth from doctors and families.
  • Its online review rating is 3.6 out of 5. Its two main competitors are rated 4.5 and 4.4.
  • 62% of new patients now say they found Lotus by searching online.
  • A video complaining about a 4-hour wait in the emergency department had 1.1m views. Lotus did not respond for 6 days.
  • The hospital website does not work well on mobile phones.

Task: Assess the importance of online branding to Lotus compared with its traditional approach. (10 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

The way patients choose has changed. Lotus's brand was built over 20 years through word of mouth, which is slow but trusted. Now 62% of new patients find Lotus online, so for most new patients the online brand is the first, and possibly the only, impression.

Lotus is losing the online comparison. A 3.6 rating next to competitors' 4.5 and 4.4 means a family comparing hospitals on their phone is likely to choose a competitor, however good Lotus's doctors are. Online, the brand is what customers say, not what the hospital says.

Speed of damage. The waiting-time video reached 1.1m views. Traditional reputation problems spread slowly; online, they spread in hours. Six days without a reply makes Lotus look as if it doesn't care, which is especially damaging for a hospital where trust is everything.

Basic weaknesses. A website that doesn't work on phones loses people at the moment they are searching, often parents in a hurry.

Recommendations:

  • Set up a social media and reviews team with a target to respond to complaints within 24 hours, with clinical leaders involved in serious cases.
  • Fix the cause, not just the message. Review emergency department waiting times; good branding cannot hide poor service for long.
  • Redesign the website for mobile, with online booking and clear waiting-time information.
  • Invite satisfied families to leave reviews, to bring the rating closer to the real quality of care.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • A real comparison (traditional vs online) applied to the case.
  • Commercial acumen: online branding problems often come from service problems, so fix the root cause.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Recommending more advertising when the issue is reviews and service.
  • Ignoring the time-critical crisis (the unanswered video).

📄 Scenario – Northern Region Health Authority (NRHA)

Step 1 – In simple words

IT controls make sure systems and data are:

C – ConfidentialOnly the right people can see the data
I – IntegrityData is complete, accurate and not tampered with
A – AvailableSystems work when needed

Why this is a strategic issue: data is a key asset; decisions and financial reports depend on it; regulations demand it; and a failure can stop the business and destroy reputation.

General controls – the whole IT environment
("locks on the building")		Application controls – inside one system
("checks at each counter")

  • Access controls: unique user IDs, strong passwords, multi-factor authentication (MFA), least privilege, removing leavers quickly, segregation of duties

Step 2 – Real business example

Equifax (2017): hackers exploited a known software weakness for which a fix (patch) had been available for months but hadn't been applied. Personal data of about 147 million people was stolen. "Assess the adequacy of the organisation's IT controls and recommend improvements." The best format is a table: Weakness → Implication → Recommendation.

📄 Scenario – Horizon Medical Labs

Overall, Horizon's controls are inadequate for an organisation handling sensitive patient results that doctors rely on for treatment. The weaknesses threaten all three aims of confidentiality, integrity and availability.

Step 2 – Real business example

WannaCry (2017): ransomware spread worldwide through computers that hadn't been updated. Parts of the UK's NHS were affected, and thousands of appointments and operations were cancelled. Maersk (2017): the shipping company was hit by the NotPetya malware and had to rebuild thousands of computers. It estimated the cost at USD 250–300m.

Step 3 – How it appears in the exam

"Evaluate and recommend ways in which the organisation could promote cyber security." Often after an incident described in an exhibit. Mark-scoring answers cover people, process and technology, and give the board a role.

📄 Scenario – Prime Payroll Services

  • Prime runs outsourced payroll for 300 client companies, holding employees' salary and bank details.
  • Last month a finance clerk paid AED 780,000 to a fake supplier after an email that appeared to come from the CEO asked for an "urgent, confidential payment". The money was not recovered.
  • Email accounts have no multi-factor authentication.
  • Staff last received cyber security training 3 years ago.
  • Board minutes show the board considers cyber security to be "an IT matter". There is no incident response plan.

Task: Evaluate Prime's approach to cyber security and recommend how it could be improved. (12 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Evaluation

Prime's approach is weak, and the AED 780,000 loss shows the risk has already materialised. For a business whose whole service depends on clients trusting it with salary and bank data, a major breach could lose clients, bring regulatory penalties and threaten the business itself. Cyber risk is therefore a strategic risk, not an IT matter.

Recommendations

Governance (board level): the board should take ownership: put cyber risk on the risk register, set a risk appetite, appoint a director responsible, and receive a cyber report every quarter. This sets the tone from the top.

People: the fraud succeeded because a clerk trusted an email. Annual training for all staff, plus regular phishing simulation tests, would teach staff to spot fake requests. Staff should feel safe to question even the CEO about unusual payment requests.

Process: require every new supplier and every change of bank details to be verified by phoning a known number (not one in the email), plus two-person approval for payments above a set limit. This control alone would probably have stopped the AED 780,000 fraud.

Technology: turn on MFA for all email accounts, which makes stolen passwords far less useful. Add email filtering that flags external emails pretending to be internal, encrypt client data, and commission an annual penetration test.

Respond and recover: write and rehearse an incident response plan covering who decides, how clients and regulators are informed, and how systems are restored from offline backups. Consider cyber insurance to transfer part of the financial risk.

Priority: payment verification and MFA can be done within weeks at low cost and should come first.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • An evaluation first (how weak, and why it matters for this business).
  • Recommendations that fix the actual causes in the case (no MFA, no training, no verification, board indifference).
  • A board-level role: SBL is a leadership exam.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Only technology answers ("install a firewall"). Most incidents start with people.
  • Not mentioning the board.
  • No incident response or recovery element.

Step 7 – Memory tip

People · Process · Technology, wrapped in Govern → Identify → Protect → Detect → Respond → Recover. "Humans are the weakest link, and the strongest defence."

Task: Evaluate City Dental's controls over its IT assets and recommend improvements. (10 marks)

Step 4 – Model answer

Overall: controls are poor and directly threaten the objective of being the "most trusted" provider. Patients trust a dentist with sensitive health data, and City Dental has already lost data twice.

Data stored on local PCs. Keeping records on 18 separate PCs means each clinic is a single point of failure, as the flood showed when 2 years of records were lost. Recommendation: move records to a central, secure system (for example, a compliant cloud service) with automatic backups following the 3-2-1 rule, so no clinic holds the only copy.

Unencrypted laptops. 3 stolen laptops means patient data may now be in criminal hands. This is likely a reportable data protection breach that could bring fines and patient claims. Recommendation: encrypt all laptops and phones immediately, add remote-wipe software, and review whether patient data needs to be on laptops at all.

No asset register. Without one, City Dental doesn't know what devices it owns, where they are, or what data they hold, so it can't even confirm what was lost. Recommendation: create a register with tags, assigned users and a check every 6 months.

No business continuity plan. A 5-day closure means lost income, cancelled treatments and patients moving to competitors. Recommendation: write a BCP setting out how patients are moved to nearby clinics, how records are reached from any site, and targets for how quickly services and data must be restored. Test it each year.

Priority: encryption and the central backup first, because they address the biggest risks to patients and reputation.

Step 5 – What the examiner wants

  • Link controls to the organisation's objectives (the syllabus says "to ensure the organisation meets its business objectives").
  • Spot the legal or data protection consequences.
  • Practical, prioritised recommendations.

Step 6 – Common mistakes

  • Forgetting that data, not hardware, is the most valuable asset.
  • Recommending backups without saying where they are kept or that they must be tested.

Step 7 – Memory tip

"Lock it, Log it, Back it up, Plan for it, Train for it." Backups: 3-2-1.

Mini case study – Oasisfield Foods (40 marks, about 78 minutes)

Practise this under exam conditions. Read the exhibits, plan, write your answers, then open the model answers. You are an external consultant advising the Board of Oasisfield Foods.

Exhibit 1 – Company background and email from the Chief Financial Officer

Oasisfield Foods is a dairy and juice manufacturer in the Gulf region with revenue of AED 1.4bn, 2,100 employees and 3 factories.

From: CFO · To: Board · Subject: ERP replacement – decision needed this month

Our on-premise ERP system is 11 years old, and vendor support ends in December 2027. We have two options:

  • Option A – Upgrade on-premise: AED 9.5m for hardware and licences now, plus AED 2.0m per year running costs (IT staff, power, maintenance).
  • Option B – Cloud ERP (SaaS): AED 3.0m migration cost now, plus AED 3.2m per year subscription. 5-year contract. Exit fee of AED 2m if we leave in the first 3 years.

Under Option B we would need 6 IT staff instead of our current 14. The cloud provider's data centre is in Ireland; it says a Gulf data centre is "expected to open next year". Factory 3, in a rural area, had 11 internet outages last year.

Cloud is obviously cheaper and safer. I propose we sign this month.

Exhibit 2 – Memo from the Marketing Director: "Oasisfield Direct" app

Our 6-month trial of a home-delivery subscription app in Dubai has been a success: 85,000 downloads, 21,000 active subscribers, average order AED 64 per week.

The app collects purchase history, location, delivery times, family size and the ages of children (parents enter these to receive "kids' nutrition tips").

Order trends during the trial: oat milk orders +140%, lactose-free milk +70%, full-fat milk −12%.

My plans:

  1. Sell anonymised customer data to a baby-food company for AED 1.5m per year.
  2. Introduce automatic dynamic pricing that adds 15% to prices for customers in high-income postcodes.

Exhibit 3 – Internal audit memo: ransomware incident, August 2026

  • Ransomware entered through a phishing email opened by a production planner at Factory 2. It encrypted the production planning server.
  • Factory 2 stopped production for 3 days. Estimated lost sales: AED 4.8m. No ransom was paid.
  • Backups were stored on a network drive connected to the same server and were also encrypted. Data was restored from a 3-week-old tape.
  • Last phishing training: 2023, head office staff only. Factory staff have never been trained.
  • Multi-factor authentication is used only by directors.
  • 31% of factory PCs run an operating system no longer supported by its vendor.
  • There is no incident response plan. The IT manager learned of the attack from factory staff 9 hours after it began.
  • Board minutes for the last 2 years contain no discussion of cyber security.

Tasks

Task 1 (14 marks, about 27 minutes). Evaluate the CFO's proposal to adopt the cloud ERP (Option B) rather than upgrade on-premise (Option A), and recommend a course of action. (12 technical marks + 2 professional skills marks for evaluation)
Task 2 (12 marks, about 23 minutes). Advise how data from the Oasisfield Direct app can support pricing and new product decisions, and assess the ethical issues raised by the Marketing Director's plans. (10 technical marks + 2 professional skills marks for commercial acumen)
Task 3 (14 marks, about 28 minutes). Prepare a briefing note for the Board that assesses the adequacy of Oasisfield's cyber security controls, as revealed by the incident, and recommends improvements. (12 technical marks + 2 professional skills marks for communication)
▶ Model answer – Task 1 (open after you have tried it)
5-year cost: Option A: 9.5m + (2.0m × 5) = AED 19.5m Option B: 3.0m + (3.2m × 5) = AED 19.0m Difference: AED 0.5m in favour of B (≈2.6%)

Cost. The CFO says cloud is "obviously cheaper", but over 5 years it is only AED 0.5m cheaper, about 2.6%. That difference could disappear if subscription fees rise, or if extra costs arise such as a second internet line for Factory 3. Cost alone does not decide this; the decision should rest on non-financial factors. Option B does, however, reduce the up-front cash outlay from AED 9.5m to AED 3.0m.

Benefits of Option B. Oasisfield would always be on the latest version with vendor support, avoiding a repeat of today's end-of-support problem. The provider brings professional security and resilience, which matters given the recent ransomware attack. Capacity can grow if the business expands across the region.

Data location risk. The data centre is in Ireland, and the Gulf centre is only "expected". Oasisfield must check whether local laws allow its customer and employee data (including the app data) to be stored abroad. Signing before this is confirmed could create a compliance breach.

Connectivity risk. Factory 3 had 11 internet outages last year. With cloud ERP, every outage would stop production planning, stock and despatch at that factory. A backup connection (a second provider or satellite) is essential.

Lock-in. A 5-year contract with an AED 2m exit fee makes it costly to leave if service is poor. Service level agreements with penalties and a data-return clause are needed.

People. Reducing IT staff from 14 to 6 brings savings but also loss of knowledge and possible resistance during migration, exactly when that knowledge is most needed. Some staff should be retrained to manage the provider and support users.

Timing. Support ends December 2027, so there is time to negotiate properly. "Sign this month" is unnecessary pressure and increases the risk of a poor contract.

Recommendation. Proceed with Option B in principle, but do not sign this month. Over the next 2–3 months: (1) obtain legal advice on data location and negotiate a contractual date for Gulf hosting, (2) negotiate SLAs, a price cap and exit terms, (3) install a backup internet link at Factory 3, and (4) plan a phased migration with a retained IT team.

Marker's comments: the calculation showing the costs are almost equal is the key insight, and it challenges the CFO's "obviously cheaper" with evidence (scepticism and evaluation). Balanced points plus a conditional recommendation earn the professional skills marks.

▶ Model answer – Task 2

Using the data for decisions

Annual revenue from app subscribers: 21,000 × AED 64 × 52 ≈ AED 69.9m (if the trial pattern holds) Conversion: 21,000 / 85,000 ≈ 25% of downloads became active subscribers

New products. Oat milk +140% and lactose-free +70%, with full-fat −12%, show a clear shift towards plant-based and specialist products. This supports developing a wider plant-based range (for example, oat-based yoghurt and juice blends). The data comes from Dubai app users only, who may be younger and wealthier than the average customer, so supermarket sales data should confirm the trend before major factory investment.

Pricing. Purchase data shows how often customers order and how they respond to promotions, so Oasisfield can test prices and bundles (for example, a discounted family subscription) and measure the effect on order value and on customers cancelling. Customer lifetime value can guide how much to spend winning each subscriber.

Ethical issues

Selling customer data. Customers gave their data, including children's ages, to receive nutrition tips, not to be sold to a baby-food company. Selling it would break the purpose they agreed to, may breach data protection law, and "anonymised" data with location and children's ages can often be traced back to families. The AED 1.5m is small compared with the AED 70m subscription revenue that depends on customer trust. This plan should be rejected.

Postcode pricing. Automatically charging 15% more by postcode, without telling customers, lacks transparency and could be seen as unfair. If discovered, it could cause serious reputational damage on social media. It also conflicts with integrity and fairness. A more acceptable approach is open pricing, with premium options that customers can choose.

Recommendation. Use the data internally for product development and transparent pricing tests, update the privacy notice, and set up a data ethics review for any future use of customer data.

Marker's comments: commercial acumen marks come from comparing the AED 1.5m with the much larger revenue that depends on trust, and from questioning whether the Dubai trial represents all customers.

Exam technique for technology tasks

Roles and formats you may be given

IT or digital consultant, adviser to the board, head of internal audit, CFO's assistant. Formats: briefing note, report, slides with notes, email. Use the format asked for, because the communication marks depend on it.

A 5-step plan for any technology task

1. VerbEvaluate, assess, advise or recommend? Evaluate and assess need a judgement.
→
2. FactsHighlight numbers, people's opinions and constraints (laws, staff, connectivity).
→
3. CalculateDo one or two quick sums (5-year cost, saving, payback).
→
4. BalanceBenefits AND risks, each applied to the case.
→
5. RecommendA clear decision, with conditions and priorities.
