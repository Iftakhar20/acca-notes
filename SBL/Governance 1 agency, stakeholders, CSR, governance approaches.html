1. The big picture: why governance exists

Corporate governance is the system by which organisations are directed and controlled. It answers three simple questions:

  1. Who is in charge? (the board, management)
  2. For whose benefit? (shareholders and other stakeholders)
  3. How do we check they are doing the job properly? (accountability, transparency, controls, reporting)

Think of it like a building. Leadership and ethics (Section A) are the foundation. Governance is the structure on top. Strategy, risk and control (C, D, F) are the rooms inside. If the structure is weak, the whole building is at risk, however good the strategy looks.

Core principles of good governance (useful in any answer)

PrincipleWhat it means in plain words
AccountabilityDirectors must answer to the owners and stakeholders for their decisions
TransparencyOpen disclosure; no hiding bad news
IntegrityHonesty and straightforward dealing
IndependenceFreedom from conflicts so judgement is objective (e.g. NEDs, auditors)
Probity / honestyNot misleading people, especially with financial information
ResponsibilityAccepting consequences, including for mistakes
FairnessTreating all shareholders and stakeholders equitably (e.g. minorities)
ReputationBehaving so that trust in the organisation is protected
JudgementBalancing competing interests sensibly

Memory tip: "A TIP I R F R J" → Accountability, Transparency, Integrity, Probity, Independence, Responsibility, Fairness, Reputation, Judgement. Use them as the ruler against which you measure the case.

2. Agency theory (B1)

Step 1 · Simple explanation

An agency relationship exists when one party (the principal) hires another (the agent) to act on its behalf. In a company, shareholders (principals) appoint directors (agents) to run the business.

The problem: the agent may act in their own interest rather than the principal's. This is the agency problem. It happens because of separation of ownership and control: the owners do not manage, and the managers do not own.

Why agents misbehave

  • Different goals: directors want salary, bonus, status, job security; shareholders want long-term value
  • Information asymmetry: directors know far more than shareholders
  • Different risk appetite: directors may be too cautious (protect job) or too risky (chase bonus)
  • Short-termism: bonuses based on this year's profit

Agency costs

  • Monitoring costs: audits, NEDs, board committees, reports
  • Bonding costs: incentives paid to align the agent (share options, LTIPs)
  • Residual loss: value still lost despite monitoring and bonding

Fiduciary duty: directors must act in good faith in the best interests of the company. This is the legal backbone of the agent's obligation.

Other agency chains: shareholders → board → CEO → managers; pension savers → fund managers → company; citizens → government → hospital board. SBL loves the public sector chain: the public are the ultimate principals, and the agent's duty is to deliver public value.

Step 2 · Real business example

A listed private hospital group pays its CEO a large bonus based on annual EBITDA. The CEO delays replacing old MRI machines and freezes nurse recruitment. EBITDA rises this year and the bonus is paid. Next year, machine breakdowns cancel appointments, overtime costs rise and patient satisfaction falls. Shareholders have paid a bonus for a result that destroyed long-term value. That is the agency problem in action.

Solution in practice: link part of the bonus to multi-year targets (3-year EPS growth, patient outcome scores, staff turnover), pay part in shares held for several years, and include malus and clawback.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam
  • "Discuss the agency issues between the shareholders and the board of X" (often 8–12 marks)
  • A family member or founder dominates the board → agency problem between majority and minority shareholders
  • A public body whose management is pursuing its own agenda instead of the ministry's policy
  • Usually combined with remuneration (Governance 2) or ethics (Section A)
Step 4 · Model exam answer
Scenario (all facts you need): MedLine plc is a listed group of 12 private clinics. The founder, Dr Rana, is CEO and holds 8% of the shares; 70% is held by institutional investors. Last year the board approved a CEO bonus of 150% of salary based only on annual operating profit. Operating profit rose 18%, but capital expenditure fell by 40% and two clinics failed their regulatory inspection due to poorly maintained equipment. The share price fell 22% after the inspection results were published. Shareholders have requested a meeting with the chair.

Requirement: Discuss the agency problem at MedLine plc and recommend how it could be reduced. (10 marks)

Briefing note to: Chair, MedLine plc  |  Subject: Agency issues raised by shareholders

The agency relationship. MedLine's institutional investors own 70% of the shares but do not manage the clinics. They rely on the board, and especially Dr Rana as CEO, to act in their long-term interests. Because Dr Rana owns only 8%, most of the value lost from poor decisions falls on other shareholders, which weakens his personal incentive to protect long-term value.

Misaligned incentive. The bonus is 150% of salary and based only on annual operating profit. This rewards short-term profit, and the 40% fall in capital expenditure suggests profit was increased by cutting investment rather than by improving performance. Shareholders have effectively paid a bonus for an 18% profit rise that was followed by a 22% share price fall, so value was transferred from owners to the agent.

Information asymmetry. Shareholders only discovered the impact of under-investment when inspection results were published. This shows the board did not report the risk of declining equipment standards. The NEDs and remuneration committee should have challenged a capex cut of this size.

Wider stakeholder impact. Failing inspections puts patient safety and the group's licence to operate at risk. The agency problem therefore damages patients and regulators as well as shareholders, which increases reputational risk.

Recommendations.

  1. Redesign the bonus: no more than half based on profit, the rest on quality measures (inspection ratings, patient safety incidents, equipment uptime). Justification: rewards the outcomes shareholders and patients actually value.
  2. Introduce a long-term incentive plan with three-year performance and shares held for a further two years, plus malus and clawback. Justification: the CEO bears the long-term consequences of decisions.
  3. Strengthen monitoring: the board should approve a minimum maintenance capex budget and receive quarterly regulatory compliance reports, with the audit/risk committee reviewing them.
  4. Engage with institutional investors: the chair should meet them, explain the actions and put the revised remuneration policy to a shareholder vote. Justification: rebuilds trust and reduces the risk of a vote against the board.

Conclusion. The root cause is an incentive scheme that paid for short-term profit. Rebalancing pay towards long-term, quality-based outcomes and improving board oversight will realign Dr Rana's interests with those of shareholders and patients.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations
  • Name the principal and the agent from the case; don't define agency in the abstract
  • Use the numbers (8%, 70%, 150%, −40%) to prove the misalignment (analysis and scepticism marks)
  • Recommendations must be practical and justified (evaluation marks)
  • Write in the format asked (briefing note, report, email) with a professional tone (communication marks)
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Writing half a page on Jensen & Meckling theory with no link to the case
  • Saying "the CEO is greedy" instead of analysing why the incentive structure caused the behaviour
  • Recommending "pay less" without explaining how to align interests
  • Forgetting agency in non-company settings (hospitals, charities, government)
Step 7 · Memory tip

Agency = "GIRS" problems: different Goals, Information asymmetry, different Risk appetite, Short-termism. Solutions = "Monitor + Bond": watch them (NEDs, audit, reporting) and tie them in (long-term pay).

3. Stakeholders and the Mendelow matrix (B2a–b)

Step 1 · Simple explanation

A stakeholder is anyone who affects, or is affected by, the organisation. Stakeholders have claims: things they want from the organisation.

ClassificationMeaningHospital example
InternalInside the organisationDoctors, nurses, managers
ConnectedHave a contract with the organisationInsurers, suppliers, lenders, patients
ExternalNo contract but affected or influentialHealth regulator, government, local community, media
Direct claimCan speak for themselvesUnions, shareholders
Indirect claimCannot speak for themselves; someone must represent themFuture patients, the environment, unborn generations

Mendelow's power/interest matrix

Power = ability to influence the organisation's decisions. Interest = how much they care (how much they will act).

Low interest
High interest
Low power
A · Minimal effort
B · Keep informed
High power
C · Keep satisfied
D · Key players
  • Key players (D): involve them in decisions; strategy must be acceptable to them
  • Keep satisfied (C): dangerous because they can move to D if annoyed (e.g. a regulator that suddenly becomes interested)
  • Keep informed (B): they can lobby or join forces to gain power (e.g. a patient group joining with the media)
  • Minimal effort (A): monitor them

Critical assessment (needed for level-3 marks): the matrix is a snapshot. Positions move over time. It is subjective, it ignores whether a stakeholder's claim is ethically legitimate (a low-power group may still deserve protection), and power is hard to measure.

Resolving conflicts between stakeholders

  • Prioritise using Mendelow but test fairness and legitimacy
  • Negotiation and compromise (e.g. phase a change)
  • Communication and consultation to reduce resistance
  • Use the organisation's mission and values as the tie-breaker
  • Consider the public interest, especially in public sector bodies
Step 2 · Real business example

A government health authority plans to close a small emergency department and centralise emergency care in a larger hospital 25 km away. The ministry (key player) supports it because it saves cost and improves specialist cover. Local residents (keep informed) are highly interested but individually weak. They start a media campaign and get elected officials involved. Their power rises and they move towards the key-player quadrant. The authority then has to consult publicly and add a 24-hour urgent care clinic to win acceptance.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam
  • "Analyse the power and interest of the stakeholders affected by the proposal" (often 8–10 marks)
  • "Discuss the conflicts between stakeholder claims and advise how they could be resolved"
  • Frequently combined with strategic choices (acceptability in Johnson, Scholes and Whittington's suitability/acceptability/feasibility test) or with change management
Step 4 · Model exam answer
Scenario: CityCare is a government-owned hospital trust. It plans to outsource its hospital laundry and catering to a private contractor, saving 12% of the annual cost. 180 staff would transfer to the contractor. The workers' union represents 90% of these staff and has threatened strike action. The Department of Health has set CityCare a target to cut non-clinical costs by 10% within two years. A local patient group has complained in the press that outsourced catering in another hospital led to poor-quality meals. The hospital's medical director is concerned about infection-control standards for laundry.

Requirement: Using Mendelow's matrix, analyse the stakeholders and recommend how CityCare should manage them. (10 marks)

Department of Health – Key player (high power, high interest). It funds CityCare and set the 10% cost target. The 12% saving exceeds that target, so it is likely to support the plan. CityCare should involve the department early and show that quality safeguards are built in, because it will not want negative press either.

Union and the 180 staff – Key player (high power, high interest). Representing 90% of affected staff and threatening a strike, the union can disrupt services patients rely on. Its claim is job security and protected terms. CityCare should consult formally, negotiate guaranteed terms on transfer and agree a timetable. If a strike stops laundry, operations could be cancelled, which would wipe out much of the saving.

Medical director – Keep satisfied, could quickly become key player. Normally less interested in support services, but infection control is a patient-safety matter on which the medical director's view would carry great weight with the board and regulators. CityCare should make infection-control standards and audits mandatory contract terms and give the medical director sign-off.

Patient group – Keep informed, power rising. Individually weak, but using the media raises its power. Its concern is legitimate. CityCare should publish meal-quality standards, include patient representatives in contract monitoring and communicate proactively.

Scepticism. The 12% saving should be challenged. It may not include transition costs, contract monitoring, or the cost of service failures. The board should ask for a fully costed business case before committing.

Recommendation. Proceed only if the contract contains enforceable quality and infection-control KPIs with penalty clauses, staff terms are protected, and the business case still shows a saving of at least 10% after hidden costs. This balances the department's cost target with the legitimate claims of staff and patients.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations
  • Classify each stakeholder with evidence from the case (why high power? why high interest?)
  • State their claim (what they want) and the action required
  • Recognise movement between quadrants: this shows insight (commercial acumen)
  • Don't draw the grid and stop. The marks are for analysis and management actions
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Listing every possible stakeholder, including ones not in the case
  • Calling "customers" one group when the case shows different groups with different interests
  • Ignoring stakeholders with indirect claims (environment, future patients)
  • Treating the matrix as fact and not critiquing it when the question says "critically assess"
Step 7 · Memory tip

For every stakeholder write "P-I-C-A": Power (why), Interest (why), Claim (what they want), Action (how to manage). Four short sentences per stakeholder = full marks structure.

4. Social responsibility and sustainability (B2c)

Step 1 · Simple explanation

Corporate social responsibility (CSR) means an organisation accepts duties to society beyond simply making profit and obeying the law. Sustainability means meeting today's needs without harming the ability of future generations to meet theirs.

Carroll's four responsibilities (pyramid, bottom to top)

LevelMeaningHealthcare example
1 EconomicBe profitable / financially viableHospital stays within budget so it can keep operating
2 LegalObey the lawMeets licensing, data protection and employment law
3 EthicalDo what is right even if not legally requiredHonest disclosure of medical errors to patients
4 PhilanthropicGive back voluntarilyFree health screening camps for low-income communities

Positions on social responsibility (Gray, Owen and Adams): from narrow to wide

PositionView
Pristine capitalistOnly duty is to maximise shareholder wealth (Friedman view)
ExpedientDo CSR only where it helps profit (e.g. reputation)
Proactive / social contractarianThe organisation has a "contract" with society; it exists because society allows it
Social ecologistThe organisation must reduce its environmental and social damage
SocialistBusiness should redistribute power and wealth more equally
Radical feminist / deep ecologistCurrent economic systems are fundamentally harmful; nature has rights

Triple bottom line (Elkington): report on Profit, People, Planet.

Business case for CSR: reputation, staff attraction and retention, access to ESG-focused investors, lower regulatory risk, cost savings (energy and waste), licence to operate.

Step 2 · Real business example

Hospitals are among the largest energy users in any city and produce clinical waste. A health system that installs solar panels, switches to low-emission anaesthetic gases and reduces single-use plastics cuts both cost and carbon. In the Gulf region, healthcare providers increasingly link this to national net-zero strategies. That is CSR that supports economic, legal and ethical goals at the same time.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam
  • "Evaluate the board's view that the company's only responsibility is to its shareholders"
  • A scenario where cutting cost harms the community or the environment
  • Often linked to integrated reporting (Governance 2) and climate risk (D1c)
Step 4 · Model exam answer
Scenario: FreshFoods, an FMCG company, owns a factory in a small town where it is the largest employer. A board member, Mr Klein, argues: "Our only job is to make money for shareholders. CSR is a cost we can't afford." The CSR budget (community health programme and water recycling) costs $2m a year against profit of $60m. Last year a competitor faced a consumer boycott after it was found to be polluting a river. Two of FreshFoods' largest investors have signed up to responsible investment principles.

Requirement: Evaluate Mr Klein's view. (8 marks)

Support for Mr Klein's view. Mr Klein holds the pristine capitalist position: directors are agents of shareholders and should not spend shareholders' money on social goals. The $2m CSR spend reduces profit, and if shareholders want to support social causes they can do so themselves with their dividends.

Against his view.

  • Small cost, large risk reduction. $2m is only 3.3% of profit. The competitor's boycott shows the cost of being seen as irresponsible can far exceed this amount. Water recycling directly reduces the pollution risk that caused that boycott.
  • Investor expectations. Two of the largest investors follow responsible investment principles. Cutting CSR could lead them to sell shares or vote against directors, which contradicts Mr Klein's aim of serving shareholders.
  • Licence to operate. As the town's largest employer, FreshFoods depends on community goodwill, staff recruitment and local permits. The community health programme supports a healthy, productive workforce.
  • Ethical duty. Beyond profit, FreshFoods has an ethical responsibility not to harm the community, consistent with Carroll's ethical level.

Conclusion. Mr Klein's view is too narrow. Serving shareholders' long-term interests requires CSR, especially activity that reduces environmental risk. The board should keep the programme but measure and report its benefits (water saved, staff absence reduced) so its value to shareholders is visible.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations
  • "Evaluate" = both sides plus a clear conclusion
  • Use numbers to put cost in proportion (2/60 = 3.3%)
  • Show CSR as enlightened self-interest, not just charity
Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Writing a moral lecture with no commercial reasoning
  • Listing all of Gray, Owen and Adams' positions without applying any
  • No conclusion
Step 7 · Memory tip

Carroll = "ELEP" (Economic, Legal, Ethical, Philanthropic), from "must do" to "nice to do". CSR argument formula: cost vs risk avoided + investor view + licence to operate + ethics.

5. Rules-based vs principles-based governance (B3b)

Step 1 · Simple explanation
Rules-basedPrinciples-based
IdeaGovernance is set in law; you must complyGovernance is set in a code; you comply or explain why not
ExampleUSA – Sarbanes-Oxley Act 2002 (after Enron/WorldCom)UK Corporate Governance Code; many codes worldwide
PenaltyLegal sanctions, fines, prisonMarket discipline: investors judge the explanation
AdvantagesClear, consistent, enforceable, high investor confidenceFlexible, fits different sizes and sectors, focuses on spirit not letter, lower cost
DisadvantagesCostly ("one size fits all"), box-ticking, loopholes ("if it's not banned it's allowed")Can be ignored with weak explanations, less certainty, depends on active investors

"Comply or explain": a listed company states whether it has complied with each provision; where it hasn't, it explains why. Shareholders decide if the explanation is acceptable.

When is each appropriate? Rules suit situations with low trust, weak investor activism, or after major scandals. Principles suit mature markets with strong institutional investors and varied company types.

Step 2 · Real business example

A small newly listed technology firm may explain that it combines the chair and CEO roles for two years because the founder's expertise is critical, with a senior independent director appointed as a safeguard. Under principles-based governance this is acceptable if investors agree. Under a strict rules-based regime it may simply be prohibited.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam

"The CFO has suggested the company does not need to comply with the code as it is only voluntary. Discuss." Here you explain comply-or-explain and the consequences of a poor explanation (investor pressure, share price, reputation, listing rule obligation to disclose).

Step 4 · Model exam answer
Scenario: TechServe plc recently listed on a stock exchange that uses a principles-based governance code. The CFO tells the board: "The code is voluntary, so we can ignore the requirement to have a majority of independent NEDs. We only have 2 NEDs on a board of 9." Pension funds hold 45% of TechServe's shares.

Requirement: Explain to the board why the CFO's view is mistaken. (6 marks)

Comply or explain is not optional. Listing rules require TechServe to disclose in its annual report whether it complies with the code and to explain any departure. TechServe can deviate, but it cannot stay silent.

Explanation must convince investors. With only 2 independent NEDs out of 9 directors (22%), the board has little independent challenge. Pension funds holding 45% of shares are likely to treat a weak explanation as a governance risk. They could vote against director re-elections, engage publicly or sell shares, which would lower the share price and increase TechServe's cost of capital.

Spirit of the code. Principles-based codes rely on companies adopting best practice voluntarily. Ignoring the code signals a poor tone at the top.

Recommendation. TechServe should appoint additional independent NEDs to reach a majority over the next 12 months and, until then, explain the timetable to shareholders in the annual report.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations

Correct technical understanding (voluntary ≠ ignorable), applied to the investors in the case, with a practical recommendation.

Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Saying principles-based codes have "no consequences"
  • Describing SOX in detail; SBL needs the concept, not section numbers
Step 7 · Memory tip

Rules = "Letter of the law" (US, SOX, comply or else). Principles = "Spirit of the code" (comply or explain, investors are the police).

6. Institutional investors (B3a)

Step 1 · Simple explanation

Institutional investors are organisations that invest large pools of other people's money: pension funds, insurance companies, investment trusts, unit trusts/mutual funds, sovereign wealth funds. Together they own most shares in large listed companies, so they have real power.

Their role in governance (stewardship): monitor the board, vote at AGMs, engage with directors, and intervene when needed.

Escalating ladder of intervention

  1. Private meetings and letters with the chair / senior independent director
  2. Expressing concerns to the company's advisers
  3. Meeting other shareholders to act collectively
  4. Making a public statement
  5. Voting against resolutions / director re-election
  6. Requisitioning a general meeting to replace directors
  7. Last resort: selling the shares (exit)

Reasons to intervene: poor strategy, poor performance, excessive remuneration, weak internal control, governance non-compliance, ethical and environmental failures, failure to manage risk.

Limitations: they may be short-term focused, passive (index funds), have conflicts of interest, or simply sell rather than engage.

Step 2 · Real business example

"Say on pay" votes: institutional investors regularly vote against executive pay reports they consider excessive. Large shareholder revolts make headlines and usually force the remuneration committee to redesign the policy the following year.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam

"Advise the pension fund on the actions it could take regarding its concerns about X's board." Your answer should follow the escalation ladder and recommend the appropriate starting point, not jump to selling or replacing the board.

Step 4 · Model exam answer
Scenario: Gulf Pension Fund holds 9% of HealthBridge plc. HealthBridge's CEO has also become chair. Two independent NEDs resigned last month, saying they were "not given information on time". The share price is down 15% this year, while competitors rose 5%.

Requirement: Advise Gulf Pension Fund on how it should respond. (8 marks)

Why intervention is justified. Three warning signs exist: the CEO/chair combination concentrates power, the NED resignations suggest the board is not receiving information and cannot challenge, and the 20-percentage-point underperformance against competitors shows value is being lost. As a 9% holder, the fund has a stewardship duty to its pension members to act.

Recommended approach (escalating).

  1. Request a private meeting with the senior independent director to understand the reasons for the role combination and the resignations. This is low-cost and keeps the relationship constructive.
  2. If not satisfied, coordinate with other institutions. 9% alone is significant, but joint action carries far more weight.
  3. Vote against the CEO's re-election as chair at the AGM and make the reasons public.
  4. Requisition a general meeting only if the board refuses to separate the roles or appoint new NEDs.

Why not sell immediately? Selling a 9% stake would depress the price further and crystallise losses for pension members. Engagement offers a better chance of recovering value.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations

Justify why intervention is needed using case evidence, then recommend a proportionate sequence of actions.

Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Recommending "sell the shares" first
  • Listing the ladder without saying which step fits the case
Step 7 · Memory tip

"Talk → Team up → Tell the world → Vote → Remove → Run": from quiet conversation to exit.

7. Ownership models: insider (family) vs outsider (joint stock) (B3c)

Step 1 · Simple explanation
Insider-dominated (family / founder / state)Outsider-dominated (joint stock, dispersed shareholders)
Where commonMiddle East, Asia, much of Europe, family conglomeratesUK, USA
Who ownsA family, founder, bank or government holds majorityMany shareholders, mostly institutions
Agency problemSmall between owners and managers (they are the same people), but large between majority and minority shareholdersLarge between shareholders and managers
AdvantagesLong-term view, quick decisions, strong values, low agency costEasier to raise capital, professional management, more transparency, market discipline
DisadvantagesFamily disputes, succession problems, minority abuse, nepotism, less transparency, harder to raise external capitalShort-termism, high agency costs, hostile takeover pressure
Step 2 · Real business example

A family-owned regional trading and healthcare group with the second generation running it. The founder's sons are all directors, but two disagree on expansion. The group wants an IPO to fund new hospitals. To attract outside investors, it must appoint independent NEDs, form audit and nomination committees, and document related-party transactions. This is a classic move from an insider to an outsider model.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam

Family company preparing to list or raise equity; governance weaknesses due to family control; succession planning after a founder retires.

Step 4 · Model exam answer
Scenario: Al Noor Group is owned 100% by the Haddad family. Five of the six directors are family members; the sixth is a family friend. The group buys all its medical supplies from a company owned by the chair's brother, without tender. The board plans to sell 30% of the shares to outside investors through a listing next year.

Requirement: Discuss the governance issues Al Noor must address before listing. (8 marks)

Lack of independence. Five family directors and a family friend means there is no independent challenge. Outside investors buying 30% would be minority shareholders with no voice. Al Noor should appoint independent NEDs, ideally at least half the board excluding the chair, and a senior independent director.

Related-party transactions. Buying all supplies from the chair's brother without tender creates a conflict of interest and risks overpaying. After listing, this could transfer value from minority shareholders to the family. Al Noor should introduce competitive tendering and require independent approval and disclosure of related-party transactions.

Board committees. No audit, remuneration or nomination committees exist. These must be set up with independent NEDs to provide assurance over reporting, pay and appointments.

Transparency and reporting. Listed companies must publish audited reports and a governance statement under comply-or-explain. The family must accept greater disclosure.

Succession and family governance. A family charter separating family matters from business decisions would reduce the risk of family disputes affecting the company.

Conclusion. Investors will pay a lower price for shares (a governance discount) unless these changes are made before listing, so reform is in the family's own financial interest.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations

Identify the majority vs minority agency problem, use specific facts (5 of 6 directors, supplier owned by the brother), and give practical fixes.

Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Saying family firms have "no agency problem"
  • Being culturally dismissive of family business; acknowledge its strengths
Step 7 · Memory tip

Insider problem = majority vs minority. Outsider problem = shareholders vs managers.

8. ICGN Global Governance Principles (B3d)

Step 1 · Simple explanation

The International Corporate Governance Network (ICGN) is a global investor-led organisation. Its Global Governance Principles set out what investors expect from company boards anywhere in the world. They are useful as a checklist for evaluating a board in any SBL case.

#PrincipleIn plain words
1Board role and responsibilitiesThe board promotes long-term value, oversees strategy, and is accountable to shareholders and relevant stakeholders
2Leadership and independenceSeparate chair and CEO; independent chair or lead independent director; enough independent directors
3Composition and appointmentRight mix of skills, experience and diversity; formal appointment and regular evaluation
4Corporate cultureThe board sets and monitors values, ethics and behaviour
5RemunerationPay aligned with long-term performance and transparent
6Risk oversightThe board oversees risk, including sustainability and climate risks
7Corporate reportingAccurate, timely, understandable financial and non-financial (sustainability) reporting
8Internal and external auditIndependent, effective audit with an audit committee overseeing it
9Shareholder rightsEqual treatment; protect minority shareholders; approve major decisions
10Shareholder meetingsMeetings are accessible; voting is fair and results are disclosed
Step 2 · Real business example

Investors use principles like these when they vote. For instance, if a company proposes a dual-class share structure giving founders super-voting rights, investors cite principle 9 (equal shareholder rights) to oppose it.

Step 3 · How it appears in the SBL exam

"Using the ICGN principles, assess the corporate governance of X." Pick the 4–5 principles most relevant to the case evidence. Do not go through all 10 mechanically.

Step 4 · Model exam answer (extract)
Scenario: PrimeLabs, a listed diagnostics company, has a combined chair/CEO. The board has not discussed climate risk although its main laboratory is in a flood zone. The annual report contains no sustainability information. Minority shareholders were not allowed to vote on a large acquisition from a company owned by the CEO.

Leadership and independence (P2). The combined chair/CEO role means nobody leads the board independently of management. PrimeLabs should separate the roles or at least appoint a lead independent director.

Risk oversight (P6). Failure to discuss flood risk at the main laboratory is a serious gap. A flood could stop testing entirely. The board should add climate risk to the risk register and test business continuity plans.

Corporate reporting (P7). No sustainability disclosure means investors cannot assess environmental exposure. PrimeLabs should report climate-related risks.

Shareholder rights (P9). Denying minorities a vote on a related-party acquisition from the CEO's company risks value transfer. Such transactions should require approval by independent shareholders.

Step 5 · Examiner expectations

Apply selectively; each point = principle + case evidence + consequence + fix.

Step 6 · Common mistakes
  • Reciting all 10 principles from memory with no application
Step 7 · Memory tip

Group the 10 into "Board (1–4) · Pay & Risk (5–6) · Report & Audit (7–8) · Shareholders (9–10)".

9. Integrated case practice: hospital group

Case: VitaHealth Group

VitaHealth is a private hospital group with 6 hospitals in the Gulf region. It was founded 20 years ago by Mr Saeed, who is chair and CEO and owns 55% of the shares. The remaining 45% was sold to two institutional investors three years ago: a sovereign wealth fund (25%) and an international private equity fund (20%).

The board has 7 members: Mr Saeed, his two sons (operations director and finance director), two long-serving managers and two NEDs. One NED is Mr Saeed's former university classmate; the other was appointed by the sovereign wealth fund.

Recent events:

  • VitaHealth plans to open a 7th hospital funded by $80m of new debt. The private equity fund opposes this and prefers dividends.
  • Nurses at two hospitals have complained about staff-to-patient ratios. Nurse turnover rose from 12% to 21% this year.
  • VitaHealth's laundry contract is with a firm owned by the operations director's wife.
  • The health regulator has announced new rules on medical waste disposal from next year.

Task (20 marks + 4 professional skills marks)

As an adviser to the sovereign wealth fund's representative NED, prepare a briefing paper which:
