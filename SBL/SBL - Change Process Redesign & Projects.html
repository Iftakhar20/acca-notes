02

Types of strategic change

H5b · Balogun & Hope Hailey
In simple wordsEvery change has two dimensions. Scope: does it keep the current way of doing things (realignment) or change the organisation's fundamental culture and paradigm (transformation)? Nature: does it happen step by step (incremental) or all at once (big bang)? Cross the two and you get four types.
NATURE OF CHANGE

Adaptation

Incremental + Realignment. Small steps within the existing paradigm. Most common type.

Evolution

Incremental + Transformation. Planned, gradual move to a new paradigm over years.

Reconstruction

Big bang + Realignment. Rapid, often painful change (cost cuts, restructuring) without changing the paradigm.

Revolution

Big bang + Transformation. Fast, fundamental change, often in a crisis or after strategic drift.

← RealignmentSCOPE OF CHANGETransformation →

Top row = incremental, bottom row = big bang.

Real-world examples

Adaptation

A hospital extends outpatient clinic hours to 9 pm and adds a self-check-in kiosk. Same model of care, small steps.

Evolution

A government health authority moves over five years from paper to fully digital patient records, changing how clinicians think and work.

Reconstruction

An FMCG company facing falling margins closes two of six factories in one quarter. Still a mass-market manufacturer, just smaller.

Revolution

A camera film maker that must become a digital imaging company within 18 months to survive. Culture, skills and business model all change fast.

How it appears in the exam"The CEO describes the proposed change as 'just a system upgrade'. Assess the type of change." The examiner wants scepticism: if the case shows new roles, new culture and job losses, it is not adaptation. Classify it, justify with evidence from the exhibits on both axes, then state the implication (e.g. revolution needs strong directive leadership and a clear sense of urgency).
Common mistakes
  • Describing all four types when the task asks you to classify this change. Name one, justify it, and move on.
  • Confusing "big" with "transformational". A huge cost-cutting programme can still be reconstruction if the paradigm is unchanged.
  • Forgetting the time dimension: a change can start as reconstruction and need to become evolution.
Memory tip"A-E-R-R" clockwise from top-left: Adaptation, Evolution, Revolution, Reconstruction. Evolution and revolution both transform (like species do); the "re-" words that build rather than transform are on the left.
03

Contextual features of change

H5g · Balogun & Hope Hailey's change kaleidoscope · High-probability exam model
In simple wordsBefore deciding how to change, check the conditions you are changing in. Eight features tell you whether the change will be easy or hard and what approach suits. Think of it as a health check of the organisation's readiness.

1. Time

How long do we have? A crisis forces fast, directive change.

Is there a deadline (regulator, cash, contract)?

2. Scope

How much changes: one department or the whole organisation? Realignment or transformation?

How many people, sites and processes are affected?

3. Preservation

What must be kept: key staff, knowledge, brand, culture, assets?

Who will we lose if we get this wrong?

4. Diversity

How varied are the groups, cultures and experiences? Diversity makes uniform change harder but brings ideas.

Are there distinct professional or national subcultures?

5. Capability

Do managers and staff have the skills and experience to manage change?

Has the organisation done change like this before?

6. Capacity

Are there enough resources: money, people, time, IT?

Can we fund it while running daily operations?

7. Readiness

Are staff aware of the need and willing to change? Is there resistance?

Do they see the burning platform?

8. Power

Does the change leader have the authority to impose change? Who else holds power (unions, doctors, ministry)?

Can the sponsor actually make this happen?

Worked example: a public hospital moving to a new electronic medical record (EMR)
  • Time: the health ministry requires go-live in 14 months, so there is pressure for a phased but firm timetable.
  • Scope: every clinical department and 3,200 staff. This is transformation of clinical workflows, not a simple IT upgrade.
  • Preservation: 15 years of patient history must migrate accurately; senior consultants must not leave.
  • Diversity: staff from 40+ nationalities, plus distinct medical, nursing and admin subcultures with different priorities.
  • Capability: the IT team has done finance ERP projects but never a clinical system. External expertise is needed.
  • Capacity: AED 45m budget approved, but clinicians cannot be taken off wards for long training sessions.
  • Readiness: nurses welcome less paperwork; some consultants fear typing will slow consultations.
  • Power: the CEO has ministry backing, but department heads (consultants) hold strong informal power and must be won over.

Conclusion: capacity and power are adequate, but capability and readiness are weak. Use an evolutionary, participative approach with clinical champions in each department.

How it appears in the exam & what the examiner expects
  • Usually "Assess the implications of the proposed change using Balogun and Hope Hailey's contextual features" for 8–16 marks.
  • Expect 1 mark per feature applied with case evidence, plus up to 1 more for an implication (what it means for how the change is managed).
  • The examiner rewards a final overall conclusion: is the organisation ready? What is the biggest barrier? This earns evaluation marks.
  • You don't need all 8 if the case gives no evidence for one. Six well-applied features beat eight thin ones.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up capability (skills, experience) with capacity (resources, money, time). Markers check this.
  • Defining each feature without linking it to the scenario. Definitions score almost zero in SBL.
  • No conclusion, so you lose the evaluation professional skills mark.
Mnemonic"TSP DC CRP": Time, Scope, Preservation, Diversity, Capability, Capacity, Readiness, Power. Say it as "The Smart Planner Doesn't Change Course Rashly, Promptly."
Capability = ability (skills). Capacity = city of resources (money, people).
04

Lewin's three-stage model & force field analysis

H5f · Managing change
In simple wordsPeople are like a block of ice. To reshape it you must unfreeze it (make people see why the old way must go), change it (move to the new way with support), then refreeze it (lock the new way in so people don't slide back).

Unfreeze

Create awareness and urgency. Communicate the "burning platform". Use data, show the gap, involve opinion leaders. Weaken restraining forces.

Change (move)

Implement the new process or structure. Train, coach, pilot, give quick wins. Provide support and two-way communication.

Refreeze

Embed the change: new policies, KPIs, rewards, appraisals and procedures aligned to the new behaviour. Celebrate success. Remove old systems.

Example: FMCG sales team moving to a mobile ordering app

Unfreeze: show that competitors process orders in 2 hours vs our 2 days, and that 8% of paper orders contain errors. Change: pilot with the best-performing region, train with ride-along coaches, keep a help desk. Refreeze: commission calculated only from app orders, paper order books withdrawn, app usage added to the monthly scorecard.

Force field analysis

Lewin also said every situation is held in balance by driving forces (pushing for change) and restraining forces (resisting it). Change happens when you strengthen drivers or, often more effectively, weaken the restrainers. Pushing harder on drivers alone tends to increase resistance.

Driving forces →← Restraining forces
Regulator patient-safety target
Medication errors costing AED 3m a year
Nursing staff support
CEO and board sponsorship
Consultants fear slower ward rounds
Limited IT training capacity
Past failed IT project memories
Cost of new devices on wards

Action: weaken the restrainers. Give consultants voice-to-text and tablets, run 20-minute bedside training, publicise lessons learned from the past failure.

How it appears in the exam"Advise the board how Lewin's model could be used to manage the implementation." Structure your answer under the three stage headings, with 2–3 case-specific actions in each. A typical allocation is 2–3 marks per stage. Refreeze is where weaker scripts lose marks, so link it to KPIs, rewards and the culture.
Common mistakes
  • Treating "refreeze" as "do nothing". It means actively embedding through systems, rewards and culture.
  • Skipping unfreeze and jumping to training. Without perceived need, training is wasted.
  • Listing generic actions ("communicate well") instead of specific ones ("CEO town-hall at each of the 3 hospitals showing the error data").
Memory tipIce cube → water → new-shaped ice cube. For force field remember: "remove the brakes before pressing the accelerator."

Resistance to change & change leadership styles

H5f, A2b · Kotter & Schlesinger · Johnson, Scholes & Whittington styles

Why people resist

Self-interest

Fear of losing job, status, pay or power.

Misunderstanding

Lack of trust or information about why change is needed.

Different assessment

Staff genuinely believe the change is wrong (sometimes they are right; listen).

Low tolerance for change

Fear of being unable to cope with new skills or ways of working.

Kotter & Schlesinger: six ways to overcome resistance

ApproachWhen to useDrawbackExample
Education & communicationResistance comes from lack of informationSlow when many people are involvedTown-halls explaining the regulator's new standard
Participation & involvementInitiators lack full information; resisters have powerTime-consuming; may lead to poor compromiseNurses co-design the new discharge checklist
Facilitation & supportResistance comes from anxiety and adjustment problemsCostly and may still failFloor-walkers and a 24/7 help desk at go-live
Negotiation & agreementA powerful group will clearly lose outExpensive; others may demand the sameAgree retraining guarantees with the union
Manipulation & co-optationOther tactics too expensive or won't workBackfires badly if discovered; ethical riskGiving a resisting consultant a token "clinical lead" title
Explicit & implicit coercionSpeed is essential; initiators have powerResentment, loss of key staff"Paper prescriptions will not be accepted after 1 March"

Leadership styles for strategic change

StyleWhat it meansBest fit
Education & delegationExplain the reasons; trust staff to deliverIncremental change, plenty of time, mature workforce
Collaboration / participationInvolve staff in deciding how to changeEvolution or adaptation; knowledge workers such as clinicians
InterventionLeader keeps control but delegates partsMixed situations; moderate time pressure
DirectionTop-down authority decides and announcesReconstruction or revolution; time pressure
CoercionImposed change using powerCrisis only; high resistance cost
Examiner expectationsLink style to the type of change and the contextual features. Example: "Because the change is a revolution and time is short (14 months to regulatory deadline), a directive style is needed at the top, but participation with clinical leads on how the workflow changes will reduce resistance from consultants who hold informal power." A balanced, blended answer shows commercial acumen.
Common mistakesRecommending manipulation or coercion without flagging the ethical risk. SBL is a leadership and ethics paper, so always note the cost to trust and culture.
Memory tipKotter & Schlesinger go from soft to hard: "Every Person Feels Nervous, Manage Carefully": Education, Participation, Facilitation, Negotiation, Manipulation, Coercion.
06

Evaluating current processes

H5a · Process mapping and diagnosis
In simple wordsA process is a set of steps that turns inputs into an output for a customer. To evaluate it, map the "as-is" flow, then hunt for delays, duplication, errors, hand-offs and steps that add no value to the customer.

What a good process looks like (your evaluation criteria)

  • Effective: achieves its objective and customer need
  • Efficient: minimal time, cost and resources
  • Controlled: errors and fraud prevented, segregation of duties
  • Flexible: copes with volume changes and exceptions
  • Simple: few hand-offs and approvals
  • Measured: has KPIs (cycle time, error rate, cost per transaction)
  • Owned: one accountable process owner end-to-end
  • Strategically aligned: supports the CSFs and the generic strategy

Worked example: "as-is" hospital discharge process (swimlane map)

Doctor
Decides patient fit for discharge (ward round 10:00)
Writes discharge summary by hand (often after clinic, 16:00)
Nurse
Re-types doctor's notes into system
Educates patient
Patient leaves (avg 18:30)
Pharmacy
Waits for paper prescription to arrive by porter
Dispenses take-home meds
Billing
Manually checks insurance approval by phone
Patient waits for final bill

Dashed red = non-value-adding or duplicated step. Dashed yellow = waiting.

Diagnosis (what you would write)
  • Long cycle time: 8.5 hours from decision to departure. The bed is blocked, so emergency admissions wait in A&E.
  • Duplication: nurse re-types the doctor's notes, adding transcription error risk (patient safety).
  • Hand-offs and disconnects: four departments, paper moved by porters, no one owns the end-to-end discharge.
  • Sequential steps that could be parallel: pharmacy and billing only start after the summary is written.
  • No KPI: nobody measures "time of discharge decision to bed free".
How it appears in the examAn exhibit describes a process in narrative form (an email from an operations manager, or interview notes). You are asked to "evaluate the effectiveness of the current process". Rewarded points are specific weaknesses + their consequence (cost, customer, risk, strategy). Always quantify where the exhibit gives numbers.
Common mistakesListing weaknesses without their impact. "The nurse re-types notes" is half a point. "…which wastes about 20 minutes per patient and introduces transcription errors that threaten patient safety and the hospital's accreditation" is a full point.
Memory tip: process waste "DOWNTIME"Defects, Overproduction, Waiting, Non-used talent, Transport, Inventory, Motion, Extra processing. A quick checklist for scanning any process exhibit (from lean, not the syllabus, but it helps you spot points fast).
07

POPIT: the four-view model

H3b · People, Organisation, Processes, Information Technology
In simple wordsMost failed change projects fix only one thing, usually the IT. POPIT reminds you that real change needs all four views to move together. Buy a new system without training people, changing the structure or redesigning the process, and it fails.

People

Skills, training, motivation, job roles, culture, recruitment, reward.

Organisation

Structure, reporting lines, management style, policies, location, relationships between departments.

Processes

The steps of work, rules, hand-offs, controls, KPIs.

Information Technology

Systems, hardware, software, data, integration, security.

Example: the discharge process again, with POPIT
  • People: train doctors on electronic discharge summaries; create a "discharge coordinator" role.
  • Organisation: make the ward manager the single process owner; set up a daily 09:00 bed-flow huddle.
  • Processes: plan discharge from the day of admission; run pharmacy and billing in parallel.
  • IT: e-discharge module integrated with pharmacy and insurer pre-approval via API.
Examiner expectationsUse POPIT when a requirement says "analyse opportunities for organisational improvement" or when an IT solution is proposed and you need to show what else must change. Use the four views as headings. The interdependency point (all four must align) is a strong scepticism and evaluation point.
Memory tipPOPIT sounds like "pop it": the change will pop like a balloon if any one of the four is missing.

Harmon's process-strategy matrix

H5c · Setting the scope and focus of process change · High-probability exam model
In simple wordsNot every process deserves the same attention. Harmon asks two questions about each process: how complex and changing is it? and how strategically important is it? The answers tell you whether to automate it, outsource it, or invest heavily in improving it.
STRATEGIC IMPORTANCE

Automate / systematise

High importance, low complexity. Important but stable and rule-based. Automate it (ERP, workflow, RPA) to make it fast, cheap and error-free.

Improve with human expertise

High importance, high complexity. Core competences and sources of competitive advantage. Keep in-house, invest in skilled people, redesign continuously.

Minimal effort / standardise

Low importance, low complexity. Do it cheaply: standard package, automate or outsource. Spend little management time.

Outsource

Low importance, high complexity. Needs expertise but gives no advantage. Let a specialist do it.

Specialist hospitalAppointment bookingAutomatePatient experience matters, but steps are rule-based (online portal)
Specialist hospitalPayrollMinimal effortSimple, stable, no advantage; standard package or shared service
Specialist hospitalMedical equipment maintenance, legal claims handlingOutsourceComplex, needs specialists, but not what patients choose the hospital for

How it appears in the exam"Using Harmon's process-strategy matrix, advise the board on the appropriate approach to the processes described in Exhibit 4." You get 1 mark for correct positioning with justification (both axes) and 1 mark for a sensible recommendation per process. The board has often proposed outsourcing a core process, so the scepticism mark is for saying "this should not be outsourced because…"
Common mistakes
  • Positioning on only one axis ("it's important, so keep it"). Always justify both complexity and importance.
  • Saying a core process should be automated just because it can be. Complex core processes need people.
  • Ignoring risks of outsourcing (loss of control, data security, supplier dependence). Mention them briefly.
Memory tipRead the matrix like a hospital: the surgeon (top-right, human expertise), the booking robot (top-left, automate), the payroll clerk (bottom-left, minimal effort), the outside lawyer (bottom-right, outsource).

Process redesign options

H5d · Harmon's redesign patterns · BPR
In simple wordsOnce you know a process needs to change, choose how much to change it. Small fixes are process improvement; a fresh design of the steps is redesign; starting from a blank sheet is re-engineering. Harmon gives four practical redesign patterns.

The spectrum of change

Process improvementProcess redesignProcess re-engineering (BPR)
DegreeIncrementalModerateRadical, fundamental
Starting pointExisting processExisting process, rethoughtBlank sheet
RiskLowMediumHigh (many BPR projects fail)
Typical gain5–10%20–40%Large step change
Link to change typeAdaptationAdaptation / ReconstructionRevolution / Reconstruction

Harmon's four redesign patterns

1. Re-engineering

Radically rethink the whole process from scratch, often enabled by new IT.

Example: a bank replaces branch-based loan approval (5 days) with an app using automated credit scoring (5 minutes).

2. Simplification

Remove duplication, redundant steps and unnecessary approvals.

Example: purchase requests under AED 5,000 no longer need three signatures, only the budget holder's.

3. Value-added analysis

Classify every step as value-adding, business-necessary (e.g. control or regulatory) or non-value-adding, then eliminate the last group.

Example: patient asked for the same details at reception, triage and billing. Capture once.

4. Gaps & disconnects

Focus on hand-offs between departments, where most delays and errors occur. Fix the interfaces and ownership.

Example: sales promises delivery dates that production can't meet. Create a shared planning system and S&OP meeting.

Hammer's BPR principles (great for "recommend" points)

  1. Organise around outcomes, not tasks (one person or team handles the whole case).
  2. Have those who use the output perform the process (self-service).
  3. Merge information processing into the real work that produces it.
  4. Treat geographically dispersed resources as if centralised (shared services, cloud).
  5. Link parallel activities instead of integrating results at the end.
  6. Put the decision point where the work is done and build control into the process.
  7. Capture information once, at source.
Example: "to-be" discharge processDischarge planned on admission (outcome-focused). Doctor completes an e-summary on a tablet during the ward round (capture once, at source). Pharmacy and insurer pre-approval are triggered automatically and run in parallel (link parallel activities). The ward discharge coordinator owns the whole process (gaps and disconnects). Target: patient leaves by 13:00. Each bed released 5 hours earlier creates extra capacity worth roughly 0.2 bed-days per discharge.
Examiner expectationsRecommendations must be specific, practical and justified: what to change, why it helps (time, cost, quality, risk), and any downside. Consider the controls you might remove; removing an approval could create fraud risk, which is a scepticism point. Link back to strategy: does the redesign support the organisation's CSFs?
Common mistakes
  • Recommending radical BPR for everything. Match the redesign level to the strategic importance and the change context.
  • Saying "use technology" without naming what the technology does in the process.
  • Forgetting staff and job implications (redundancy, reskilling). That is where the change management marks link in.
Memory tipHarmon's four patterns: "R-S-V-G", "Really Simple Value Gains": Re-engineering, Simplification, Value-added analysis, Gaps & disconnects.
10

Process redesign methodology

H5e · Recommending a structured approach
In simple wordsRedesign is done in a sensible order: understand the project, study the current process, design the new one, build what's needed, roll it out, and check it works.

Understand the project

Agree scope, objectives, sponsor, team, timescale, link to strategy. Which process and why?

Analyse the "as-is"

Map the current process (swimlanes), measure baseline KPIs, find root causes, consult staff and customers.

Design the "to-be"

Apply redesign patterns, benchmark best practice, model the new flow, test with users.

Develop resources

Build or buy IT, write procedures, define new roles, train staff, run pilots.

Implement

Roll out (pilot, phased or big bang), manage the change and resistance, communicate.

Review & improve

Measure new KPIs against baseline; continuous improvement; post-implementation review.

Implementation optionProsConsUse when
PilotLow risk, learn before full rolloutSlower; pilot site may be unrepresentativeHigh risk, safety-critical (e.g. one ward first)
PhasedSpreads workload, lessons carry forwardTwo systems run side by side; interfacesLarge multi-site organisations
Parallel runningSafety net if new system failsDouble workload and costFinancial or clinical data where errors are unacceptable
Direct (big bang)Fast, cheap, clean breakHighest risk; no fallbackSimple systems or when old system cannot continue
How it appears in the exam"Recommend a methodology the company should use to redesign its procurement process." Write each stage as a heading with case-specific actions (who, what, how). Finish with a justified recommendation on rollout approach. Example: "pilot at the Deira clinic because it has the highest volume and a supportive manager."
Common mistakesForgetting to measure the baseline in the analysis stage. Without it you cannot prove benefits later, which is a strong scepticism and evaluation point.
Memory tip"U-A-D-D-I-R": "U Are Designing, Developing, Implementing, Reviewing."

What makes a project & its constraints

H6a, H6b · Features, triple constraint, life cycle
In simple wordsA project is a temporary piece of work with a defined start and end that produces a unique output. Running the hospital pharmacy each day is operations; installing a new pharmacy robot is a project.

Distinguishing features

Specific objective

A defined deliverable (e.g. new EMR live in all wards).

Defined start and end

Temporary; disbands on completion.

Unique

Not routine; involves uncertainty and novelty.

Resource constrained

Budget, people and time are limited.

Cross-functional

Draws people from several departments.

Has risk

Uncertain outcome, so needs active risk management.

The triple constraint (iron triangle)

Every project balances scope (and quality), time and cost. Change one and at least one other must move. The sponsor must decide which constraint is fixed and which can flex.

  • Time fixed: regulatory deadline (e.g. new tax e-invoicing go-live). Flex cost or scope.
  • Cost fixed: government grant capped. Flex scope (deliver phase 1 only) or time.
  • Scope/quality fixed: patient-safety system can't cut features. Flex time or cost.

Project life cycle

Initiation

Idea, feasibility, business case, appoint sponsor and PM, PID.

Planning

WBS, schedule, budget, resources, risk register, quality and communications plans.

Execution

Do the work, manage team and suppliers.

Control

Monitor progress vs plan, manage change requests, risks and issues.

Completion

Handover, acceptance, close contracts, post-project review, disband team.

Examiner expectationsWhen the case says "the CEO wants it faster, cheaper and with more features", the examiner wants you to challenge this professionally (scepticism) using the triple constraint, and advise which constraint should be fixed given the organisation's priorities.
Common mistakesCalling routine, ongoing work a "project". Also forgetting scope creep as the main threat to the triangle: uncontrolled additions to scope without matching time or cost changes.
Memory tip"Good, fast, cheap: pick two."

The business case & project initiation document (PID)

H6c · Very commonly examined as a document you must write
Business case: "Should we do it?"Justifies the investment to decision-makers. Produced before approval. Owned by the sponsor. Re-checked at every stage gate.
PID: "How will we do it?"The project's rulebook and contract between sponsor and PM. Produced after approval, before work starts. Owned by the project manager.

Contents of a business case

SectionWhat to includeHospital e-prescribing example
1. Introduction / backgroundContext, the problem or opportunity1,400 medication errors last year; regulator inspection due
2. Strategic fitLink to mission, objectives, CSFsSupports "zero harm" strategy and accreditation
3. Options consideredIncluding "do nothing" and "do minimum"(a) do nothing, (b) improve paper process, (c) e-prescribing module, (d) full EMR replacement
4. CostsOne-off, recurring, intangibleAED 2.4m set-up, AED 0.3m a year support
5. BenefitsClassified (see next section), with ownersWastage savings, fewer errors, pharmacist time
6. Investment appraisalNPV, IRR, payback, sensitivityPayback 4 years; NPV positive at 8%
7. RisksKey risks and mitigationsClinician adoption, data migration, downtime
8. RecommendationPreferred option and whyOption (c): best balance of cost, benefit and risk
9. Implementation outlineHigh-level timeline, resources, governance12 months, pilot in 2 wards first

Contents of a PID

Objectives & scope

What is in and, just as important, what is out of scope.

Deliverables

What will be handed over, with acceptance criteria.

Organisation & roles

Sponsor, PM, board, team, users, suppliers.

Plan

Schedule, milestones, budget, resources.

Constraints & assumptions

Fixed deadlines, budget caps, dependencies.

Risk & quality

Initial risk register; quality standards and testing approach.

Controls & reporting

Progress reports, tolerances, change control, escalation.

Communication plan

Who gets told what, when and how.

Business case

Summary or reference to the approved case.

How it appears in the exam"Prepare the sections of the business case for the proposed project" (15–20 marks with 3–4 professional skills marks for communication). Use the document format: title, To/From/Date, headings for each section, and numbers from the exhibits. The examiner awards communication marks for a document that looks like a real business case, so a well-laid-out answer earns marks in its own right.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up business case and PID. The business case justifies; the PID organises.
  • Leaving out the "do nothing" option, which is the baseline every option is compared to.
  • Accepting the finance director's benefit estimates without question. Challenge optimistic assumptions (scepticism).
Memory tipBusiness case = WHY (sponsor). PID = HOW (project manager). Business case contents: "B-S-O-C-B-I-R-R-I", or just think of it as Problem → Options → Numbers → Risks → Recommendation.

Classifying costs & benefits

H6d · Analyse, assess and classify the costs and benefits of a project investment
In simple wordsNot all benefits are equally certain. Some are cash you can count today; others you can only "see". Classifying them tells the board how much weight to give each one and how it will be proved later.

Benefits, from most to least certain (Ward & Daniel)

ClassMeaningHospital e-prescribing example
FinancialCan be expressed in money with reasonable confidence in advanceDrug wastage falls by AED 0.9m a year (stock data supports this)
QuantifiableCan be estimated in numbers in advance, but not reliably in moneyMedication errors expected to fall by ~50%
MeasurableCan be measured once achieved, but impact can't be estimated in advancePharmacist time freed (tracked after go-live)
ObservableJudged by opinion or observation onlyBetter clinician morale; improved patient confidence

Costs

One-off (capital / set-up)

Licences, hardware, implementation consultants, data migration, initial training, recruitment.

Recurring (ongoing)

Support and maintenance fees, cloud subscription, extra IT staff, refresher training.

Intangible

Disruption during go-live, temporary productivity dip, staff stress, management distraction, risk to reputation.

The evaluation point that earns marks: on cash alone the project is marginal, but the quantifiable benefit (halving medication errors) protects patients and accreditation, and avoided litigation is not yet counted. The board should approve on strategic and safety grounds, but must test the AED 0.9m wastage assumption and assign a benefit owner.

Examiner expectationsClassify each cost and benefit from the exhibit correctly, question the estimates (who prepared them? are they optimistic?), and draw a conclusion on whether the investment is justified. Exhibits often include a deliberate error or optimistic assumption, which earns a scepticism mark when you spot it.
Common mistakes
  • Mixing up quantifiable (estimable before) and measurable (only measurable after).
  • Ignoring intangible costs. Go-live disruption in a hospital can be a patient-safety risk.
  • Rejecting a project purely on negative NPV in the public sector, where public value and the 3Es matter.
Memory tipBenefits ladder from certain to fuzzy: "F-Q-M-O", "Financial Quality Means Outcomes". Quantifiable = before (forecast); Measurable = after (meter it).

Other roles

Project board / steering committee

Example of role failureIn a case, the CFO is sponsor but has "delegated everything to the PM and attends no meetings". The PM, a junior IT analyst, cannot get ward managers to release staff for testing. Your point: the sponsor is failing to champion and remove barriers; the PM lacks authority. The project plan & its key elements

H6f · Importance of planning; WBS, network, Gantt
In simple wordsA plan breaks the project into tasks, puts them in order, gives each a time and cost, and shows who does what. It is the baseline against which progress is measured. Without a plan you cannot know whether you are late.

Key elements

Work breakdown structure (WBS)

Splits the project into deliverables and then into manageable work packages.

Network / critical path analysis

Shows task dependencies and the longest path that sets project duration.

Gantt chart

Bar chart of tasks against time; easy for stakeholders to read progress.

Resource plan & histogram

Who is needed when; reveals overloads.

Budget

Cost per work package and phasing over time; contingency.

Milestones

Key checkpoints (e.g. "data migration signed off").

Risk register

Risks, likelihood, impact, owner, response.

Quality & communication plans

Standards, testing, stakeholder updates.

Monitoring & controlling risks and slippage

H6g · Monitor and control project risks and slippages, recommending improvements

Common project risks

RiskExampleMitigation
Scope creepDepartments keep adding "small" reportsFormal change control; impact assessed before approval
Unrealistic estimatesSupplier quotes 6 months to win the contractIndependent review; contingency; fixed-price contract
Weak sponsorshipSponsor absent, decisions stallSteering committee; escalation route
Key staff unavailableClinicians can't leave wards for testingBackfill budget; schedule around rosters
Supplier failureVendor misses deliveries or goes bustDue diligence, penalties, escrow of source code
Technical / integrationNew system won't talk to lab systemEarly prototype; integration testing
Data migrationPatient allergies lost in transferData cleansing, reconciliation, parallel run
User resistanceStaff keep using paperChange management (Lewin, champions)

Dealing with slippage: corrective options

Add resources ("crashing")

More staff or overtime on critical tasks. Raises cost; adding people late can slow things down.

Fast-tracking

Run tasks in parallel that were planned in sequence. Raises risk of rework.

Reduce scope

Deliver core features now, others in phase 2. Agree with sponsor and users.

Accept the delay

Revise the plan and baseline, and inform stakeholders, if quality and safety matter more.

Improve productivity

Better tools, remove blockers, motivate team, clarify priorities.

Control tools

  • Progress reports against baseline: RAG (red/amber/green) status on time, cost, scope, risk.
  • Milestone and stage-gate reviews: continue, change or cancel? Re-check the business case.
  • Tolerances: e.g. PM can absorb ±5% cost or 2 weeks; beyond that, escalate to the board.
  • Earned value: compares planned value, earned value and actual cost to spot overspend or delay early. Example: 50% of budget spent but only 30% of work done means a problem.
  • Risk and issue logs: reviewed at every steering meeting with owners and due dates.
Exam-style scenarioThe project status report says "all green", yet the exhibit shows 70% of budget spent with testing not started and the go-live date unchanged. Your scepticism point: the reporting is unreliable (optimism bias, or PM reluctant to report bad news). Recommend independent project assurance, earned-value reporting and a re-baselined plan.
Examiner expectationsIdentify the causes of slippage from the exhibits (not generic), assess their impact on the triple constraint and benefits, then recommend realistic actions with trade-offs. Always consider whether the business case is still valid. Recommending cancellation can be the right answer.
Common mistakesRecommending "add more people" as the only fix. Show the trade-off (cost, coordination, learning curve) and consider scope reduction or phasing.
Memory tipSlippage fixes: "MORE, FASTER, LESS, LATER" = add resources, fast-track, reduce scope, accept delay.

Post-implementation review vs post-project review

H6h · Frequently examined as a short requirement
Post-project review (PPR)Post-implementation review (PIR)
FocusThe project process: how well was it managed?The product or outcome: did it deliver the benefits?
TimingSoon after project closureSome months after go-live (e.g. 6–12 months), once benefits can show
Key questionsOn time, on budget, to scope? What went well or badly? Was the plan realistic? How did the team and suppliers perform?Are the business case benefits achieved? Is the system used as intended? Are users satisfied? Any unexpected problems?
Led byProject manager and team, ideally with an independent facilitatorSponsor / benefit owners; often internal audit
OutputLessons-learned report for future projectsBenefits realisation report; corrective actions

Benefits of conducting them

  • Organisational learning: avoid repeating mistakes and improve estimating
  • Accountability: managers know they will be reviewed, so forecasts are more honest (reduces optimism bias in future business cases)
  • Benefit realisation: actions to capture benefits not yet achieved
  • Identifies further improvements to the system or process
  • Recognises team performance; supports appraisals
  • Demonstrates value for money to stakeholders, especially in the public sector (economy, efficiency, effectiveness)
ExampleA government entity's new procurement portal finished 2 months late. The PPR found user requirements were signed off by IT, not procurement officers. The PIR 9 months later found tender cycle time fell from 45 to 28 days (benefit achieved) but supplier registration complaints rose. Action: a supplier help desk and simplified registration form.
Common mistakesUsing the two terms interchangeably. Markers look for the distinction: project process vs benefits of the outcome.
Memory tipPPR = how we Ran it. PIR = what it Returned.

Integrated case & model answers

Practise as the exam does it: exhibits first, then requirements
Scenario: Crescent Bay Health (fictitious)

Crescent Bay Health (CBH) is a government-owned group of 3 hospitals and 12 clinics serving 1.2 million residents. Its strategy is "right care, first time, close to home". The board has approved a Patient Flow Transformation Programme (budget AED 38m, 18 months) to cut emergency waiting times, which the Health Ministry wants below 4 hours for 95% of patients by the end of next year (currently 71%).

Exhibit A, COO's email: "Discharges happen late in the afternoon, so beds are blocked and A&E patients wait for admission. Each department blames another. We should outsource the whole bed-management and discharge process to a private patient-flow company. It's quicker."

Exhibit B, HR director's note: "Nurses are exhausted after the last restructuring two years ago, which was announced with no consultation. Some senior consultants say the programme is 'an IT project dreamed up by managers'. We have never run a programme this large. Our IT team is fully committed to the ERP upgrade until March."

Exhibit C, project update: The CFO is sponsor. The project manager is a seconded finance analyst. The plan shows go-live at all three hospitals on the same day. No testing phase is shown. Reported status: green.

Requirement 1 (12 marks, including 2 professional skills marks for commercial acumen)

Using Balogun and Hope Hailey's contextual features, assess the context of the proposed change at CBH and its implications for managing the programme.

Model answer: Briefing note to the CBH Board12 marks

To: Board of Directors, Crescent Bay Health
From: Strategy Advisor
Subject: Context for the Patient Flow Transformation Programme

Time

The Ministry target of 95% within 4 hours by the end of next year gives CBH about 18 months to improve performance by 24 percentage points. This is a firm external deadline, so CBH cannot rely on slow, purely voluntary change. A clear timetable with interim milestones (e.g. 80% by month 9) is needed. ✓1

Scope

Although the COO frames the problem as discharge, patient flow cuts across A&E, wards, pharmacy, transport and billing at three hospitals. Clinicians will need to change when and how they make decisions, so this is closer to transformation than realignment. Change of this breadth needs strong programme governance across sites. ✓1

Preservation

CBH must preserve the goodwill and expertise of experienced nurses and consultants. Losing them would worsen flow and patient safety. Clinical quality and patient safety must also be protected during the change, so it should be phased rather than introduced on one day. ✓1

Diversity

There are distinct professional subcultures (consultants, nurses, managers) and three hospitals that may work differently. The "each department blames another" comment suggests a silo culture. One standard approach imposed on everyone is likely to be resisted; site-level adaptation within common principles is preferable. ✓1

Capability

HR states CBH has "never run a programme this large", and the project manager is a seconded finance analyst with no evident change or clinical project experience. Capability is a serious weakness. CBH should appoint an experienced programme director and train clinical change champions. ✓1

Capacity

The AED 38m budget appears adequate, but people capacity is not. Nurses are exhausted and IT is fully committed to the ERP upgrade until March. Any IT-dependent elements should be scheduled after March or resourced externally; backfill funding is needed to release clinical staff. ✓1

Readiness

Readiness is low. The previous restructuring was imposed without consultation, which has damaged trust, and consultants see the programme as a managers' IT project. CBH must first unfreeze by sharing the waiting-time data and the patient-safety consequences, and by involving clinicians in redesigning the process. ✓1

Power

The board and CFO sponsor have formal authority and Ministry backing, but senior consultants hold strong informal power over clinical decisions such as discharge timing. Without their support the change will stall. A respected senior consultant should be appointed as clinical co-sponsor. ✓1

Overall conclusion and implications

The context is challenging: time pressure and power support change, but low readiness, weak capability and stretched capacity are major barriers. CBH should not rush into a single-day launch. It should use an evolutionary but firmly time-bound approach: participative with clinicians on how to change, directive on what must be achieved and by when. ✓2 PS: commercial acumen

Requirement 2 (10 marks, including 2 professional skills marks for scepticism)

Evaluate the COO's proposal to outsource bed management and discharge, using Harmon's process-strategy matrix, and recommend an alternative approach to redesigning the process.

Model answer10 marks

Positioning the process

Strategic importance: high. Patient flow directly drives the Ministry's 4-hour target, CBH's public reputation and its strategy of "right care, first time". Delays in discharge create A&E crowding, which is a patient-safety risk. ✓1

Complexity: high. Discharge depends on clinical judgement, varying patient needs, coordination across pharmacy, transport and social care, and three different sites. It is dynamic, not rule-based. ✓1

The process therefore sits in the high-importance, high-complexity quadrant, where Harmon recommends improving the process in-house through skilled people and continuous redesign, not outsourcing. ✓1

Evaluation of the COO's proposal

Outsourcing would hand control of a core public-service process to a private firm. Clinical discharge decisions cannot legally or ethically be made by a third party, so the supplier could only coordinate, and the root cause (late clinical decisions, poor hand-offs) would remain. ✓1 It also creates dependency, data-privacy risk over patient records, and may further damage trust among staff who already feel change is imposed on them. The COO's claim that "it's quicker" is unsupported by any evidence and should be challenged. ✓1 PS: scepticism

Recommended alternative

  • Gaps and disconnects: appoint a single end-to-end process owner per hospital (discharge coordinator) and a daily bed-flow huddle so departments stop working in silos. ✓1
  • Value-added analysis and simplification: plan the expected discharge date on admission; remove re-typing of notes by moving to an electronic discharge summary completed during the ward round. ✓1
  • Parallel activities: trigger pharmacy and transport automatically once the discharge decision is recorded, rather than after the summary is written. ✓1
  • Selected non-core supporting activities (e.g. patient transport) could be outsourced, since they are lower in strategic importance. A specialist firm could also be hired as an adviser on redesign without taking over the process. ✓1 PS: scepticism

Recommendation: reject full outsourcing. Redesign the discharge process in-house, piloting at the hospital with the worst waiting times, with clear KPIs (discharge before 13:00, A&E 4-hour performance) measured against today's baseline.

Requirement 3 (8 marks)

Assess the weaknesses in the current project arrangements shown in Exhibit C and recommend improvements.

Model answer8 marks
  • Inappropriate project manager. A seconded finance analyst is unlikely to have the authority with clinicians or the change-programme experience this needs. Recommend appointing an experienced programme manager, with the analyst supporting on budget and benefits tracking. ✓2
  • Sponsor fit. The CFO can champion value for money but may lack credibility with clinicians on a clinical-flow programme. Recommend the CFO remains sponsor but a senior consultant joins as senior user on a steering committee. ✓2
  • Big-bang go-live at all three hospitals with no testing. This creates unacceptable patient-safety and operational risk and leaves no chance to learn. Recommend a pilot at one hospital, a formal testing and training phase, then phased rollout. ✓2
  • "Green" status is not credible given that the plan lacks testing and IT is unavailable until March. Reporting may suffer from optimism bias. Recommend independent project assurance (PMO or internal audit), RAG reporting against milestones, and a re-baselined plan. ✓2
Why these answers score wellEach point uses a fact from the exhibit, explains the consequence, and gives a specific action. Headings mirror the model, so the marker can tick quickly. There is a clear conclusion, and the advisor challenges management's claims politely but firmly. That is what "thinking like a senior business leader" looks like on paper.

Self-test

Answer in your head, then open each one to check
1. A retailer closes 40% of its stores within 6 months but keeps the same business model. Which type of change is this?

Reconstruction: big bang (fast) and realignment (same paradigm).

2. What is the difference between capability and capacity in Balogun & Hope Hailey?

Capability = skills and experience of managing change. Capacity = resources available (money, people, time, IT).

3. Which Lewin stage is most often neglected, and what does it involve?

Refreeze: embedding the change through new policies, KPIs, rewards, procedures and removing old systems so people don't revert.

4. A process is simple and rule-based but strategically important. What does Harmon recommend?

Automate / systematise it (e.g. ERP, workflow, RPA) to achieve speed, consistency and low cost.

5. Name Harmon's four redesign patterns.

Re-engineering, simplification, value-added analysis, gaps and disconnects.

6. "Staff morale will improve after the new system." Which benefit class is this?

Observable: it can only be judged by opinion or observation.

7. "Average processing time will fall from 5 days to 2 days." Which benefit class?

Quantifiable: it can be estimated in numbers in advance, but not directly in money.

8. Who owns the business case, and who owns the PID?

Business case: project sponsor. PID: project manager (approved by the sponsor/board).

9. Activities: A (4 wks), B (6 wks, after A), C (3 wks, after A), D (2 wks, after B and C). What is the critical path and how much float does C have?

Critical path A–B–D = 12 weeks. C has 3 weeks' float (6 − 3).

10. Distinguish a post-project review from a post-implementation review.

PPR reviews how the project was managed (time, cost, scope, lessons) shortly after closure. PIR reviews whether the outcome delivered the business case benefits, some months after go-live.

11. When is coercion an appropriate way to overcome resistance, and what is its main risk?

When speed is essential and change initiators have considerable power (e.g. a crisis). Risk: resentment, loss of key staff, long-term damage to trust and culture. Flag the ethical dimension.

12. The board wants the project faster, cheaper and with more features. How should you respond as advisor?

Use the triple constraint to explain that all three cannot improve at once. Ask the board to decide which constraint is fixed (e.g. the regulatory deadline), then flex scope (phase 2) or cost. Done politely, this earns scepticism and commercial acumen marks.
