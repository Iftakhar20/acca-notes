The big picture in 2 minutes

You've just done Strategic Choices, so you know how a company picks a direction: Ansoff, BCG, acquisitions, alliances. Risk is the other side of that. Any choice you recommend in the exam carries risks, and the examiner wants you to spot them, rate them and say what to do about them.

Strategy = where we want to go.
Risk management = making sure we get there without crashing.

1D1a

What is risk, and how does it link to strategy?

① Simple explanation

Risk is the chance that what actually happens is different from what you expected. The difference can be bad (downside risk, i.e. a loss) or good (upside risk, i.e. an opportunity).

Risk and reward go together. A company that takes no risk makes no return. So the aim is not to remove all risk. The aim is to take the right risks (ones that fit the strategy) and keep them at a level the board is comfortable with.

The link to strategy works both ways:

  • Strategy creates risk. Every new market, product or acquisition brings new risks with it.
  • Risk shapes strategy. If a risk is too big for the company to handle, that strategy should be rejected or changed.

So the risk management strategy (how much risk we accept and how we handle it) must be aligned with the organisational strategy, and the two should be set together.

② Real business example

Private hospital group expanding into medical tourism. The strategy is to attract patients from overseas. The new risks include currency swings, international legal claims, reputational damage if a foreign patient has a bad outcome and gets publicity online, visa problems, and reliance on travel agents.

If the board decides it cannot tolerate international legal exposure, it might change the strategy. For example, it could partner with a licensed facilitator who takes on part of that risk, rather than go in alone.

Upside example: a food manufacturer sees demand for halal-certified snacks growing in Europe. If it does nothing, the risk is that a competitor grabs the opportunity first. Missing out is a risk too.

③ How it appears in the exam
  • "Discuss how the proposed strategy will change the risk profile of X."
  • "Explain to the board why risk management should be integrated with strategy setting."
  • Often hidden inside a strategy task: "Evaluate the acquisition…". Part of the evaluation marks are for risks.
④ Model answer extract

Why risk management must be linked to the expansion strategy

The board's plan to open three outpatient clinics in neighbouring emirates will significantly change the group's risk profile. At present its risks are concentrated in one regulated market where it has 15 years of experience. The expansion brings new regulatory regimes, recruitment risk for scarce specialist staff, and capital at risk of around AED 40m.

If risk is considered only after the decision is made, the board may commit to a strategy whose risks exceed what the group can bear. If, for example, licensing approval takes 12 months longer than planned, the clinics would carry fixed costs with no revenue, which could breach the bank covenant noted in Exhibit 3.

Recommendation: the board should agree its risk appetite for the expansion before approving it, and require the business case to include a risk assessment of the three most significant risks with costed responses.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Use the case numbers and facts, e.g. "AED 40m", "Exhibit 3 covenant".
  • Show the two-way link: the strategy creates risk, and the risk should shape the strategy.
  • Mention upside risk where relevant. It shows maturity (commercial acumen).
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Treating risk as only "bad things". Upside risk is examinable.
  • Writing "all risks should be eliminated". That is wrong and costs credibility.
⑦ Memory tip "No risk, no return. Wrong risk, no future." The board's job is to choose the right risks, not avoid all of them.
2D1b

Enterprise Risk Management (ERM): the COSO 2017 framework

① Simple explanation

Old approach (silo): each department manages its own risk separately. Finance looks at currency risk, IT looks at cyber risk, HR looks at staff turnover, and nobody sees the whole picture.

ERM (enterprise-wide): risk is managed across the whole organisation, linked to strategy and performance, and owned by the board. It also looks at how risks combine (a cyber attack causes reputational damage, which causes loss of customers).

The COSO ERM framework (2017), "Integrating with Strategy and Performance", has 5 components and 20 principles. You need the 5 components:

1Governance & Culture
2Strategy & Objective-Setting
3Performance
4Review & Revision
5Information, Communication & Reporting
ComponentWhat it means (simple)Hospital example
1. Governance & cultureBoard oversight of risk, the right structures (e.g. risk committee), the right values and "tone from the top", attracting capable people.The board sets up a risk committee chaired by a NED. The CEO says openly that reporting clinical near-misses is welcomed, not punished.
2. Strategy & objective-settingAnalyse the business context, define risk appetite, look at alternative strategies, and set objectives that fit the appetite.Board states: "Zero appetite for patient safety risk; moderate appetite for financial risk in new service lines."
3. PerformanceIdentify risks, assess severity, prioritise them, choose responses, and build a portfolio view (all risks together).Each department identifies risks. They are scored on a 5×5 matrix and top risks get owners and action plans.
4. Review & revisionWatch for big changes in the environment, review risk and performance, improve ERM itself.After a new health regulation, the risk profile is reassessed. ERM effectiveness is reviewed every year.
5. Information, communication & reportingUse information systems, communicate risk information, report on risk, culture and performance.A monthly risk dashboard goes to the executive team and a quarterly report goes to the board.
② Real business example

A government utility (water and electricity) runs separate risk lists in operations, finance and IT. During a heatwave, demand spikes (an operations risk), a cyber attack hits the billing system (an IT risk) and cash collection drops (a finance risk), all in the same week. Nobody had linked these. With ERM, a single portfolio view would have shown that a heatwave scenario makes several risks worse at once, so contingency plans would have been joined up.

③ How it appears in the exam
  • "Using the COSO ERM framework, evaluate X's current approach to risk management." Very common
  • "Advise the board how ERM could improve risk management following the incident in Exhibit 4."
  • You might be a consultant or a newly appointed risk manager writing a briefing note.
④ Model answer extract (evaluating against COSO)

Governance and culture: CityCare's board has no risk committee, and Exhibit 2 shows risk was last discussed at board level 14 months ago. This suggests risk is not seen as a board responsibility. The CEO's comment that "risk is the finance team's job" sets a poor tone from the top. Staff are unlikely to report risks if leaders don't value it.

Strategy and objective-setting: No risk appetite has been defined. As a result, the decision to launch a home-care service was taken without agreeing how much financial or clinical risk was acceptable, which is a significant weakness.

Performance: Risks are identified, but only within departments. The pharmacy's supplier risk and the finance team's cash flow risk are linked (a supplier failure forces expensive emergency buying), but neither register shows the connection.

Review and revision: The risk register has not been updated since the new government funding model was announced, so it no longer reflects reality.

Information, communication and reporting: Risk reports are 40-page spreadsheets that board members admit they do not read. A one-page dashboard showing the top 10 risks and their trends would be more effective.

Overall: CityCare has the basics of risk identification but not an enterprise approach. The most urgent priority is component 1. Without board ownership the other improvements will not stick.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Use the 5 components as headings. It's easy structure and marks.
  • For each component: case evidence → weakness or strength → consequence. Theory alone gets almost nothing.
  • End with an overall judgement / priority. This earns evaluation skills marks.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Using the old 2004 COSO cube (8 components). Use the 2017 five components.
  • Explaining each component in theory for half a page and never mentioning the company.
  • Confusing COSO ERM (risk) with the COSO internal control framework (Control environment, Risk assessment, Control activities, Information & communication, Monitoring), which belongs to Chapter F.
⑦ Memory tip "Good Students Perform Really Intelligently" → Governance & culture · Strategy & objectives · Performance · Review & revision · Information, communication & reporting.
3D1c, D1d

Types of risk: strategic vs operational, and the key risk categories

① Simple explanation

🧭 Strategic risk

Risks from the big decisions and the overall position of the organisation. They are long-term, affect the whole organisation and are managed by the board.
Examples: wrong acquisition, new competitor, technology making the product obsolete, losing a major government contract.

⚙️ Operational risk

Risks from day-to-day running: people, processes, systems, external events. They are shorter-term, usually affect one area, and are managed by operational managers.
Examples: machine breakdown, data entry errors, staff fraud, IT outage, supplier late delivery.

Watch out: an operational risk can become strategic if it is big enough. A single data breach is operational, but a breach of 2 million patient records that destroys public trust becomes strategic.

Key risk categories you can use in any case

🌍 Environmental and climate-related risk (named in the syllabus, so expect it)

Physical risks

Acute: extreme events such as floods, storms, wildfires and heatwaves.
Chronic: long-term shifts such as rising temperatures, sea levels and water scarcity.
Hospital: extreme heat raises patient admissions and cooling costs; flooding could close a ground-floor emergency department.

Transition risks

Risks from the move to a low-carbon economy:
• Policy/legal: carbon taxes, emissions rules
• Technology: old assets become obsolete
• Market: customers switch to greener products
• Reputation: accused of greenwashing
FMCG: plastic packaging tax, retailers demanding recyclable packs.
② Real business example

A tea and beverages company (FMCG) buys most of its tea from two growing regions.

  • Strategic: younger consumers moving to energy drinks; a large competitor launching cheaper own-label tea.
  • Operational: packaging line breakdowns; quality failures in a batch.
  • Climate (physical): drought in the growing region cuts the crop and raises prices.
  • Climate (transition): new rules on plastic sachets.
  • Financial: tea is priced in USD, so currency weakness raises costs.
③ How it appears in the exam
  • "Identify and evaluate the key risks facing X as a result of the proposed strategy."
  • "Distinguish between strategic and operational risks, using examples from the case."
  • "Assess the climate-related risks facing X." Use the physical vs transition split.
④ Model answer extract ("evaluate the key risks")

1. Regulatory risk (strategic, high impact): The new telemedicine service must be licensed in every emirate it serves. Exhibit 5 shows approval has been obtained in only one. If the service treats patients from elsewhere without a licence, the group faces fines and possible suspension of its main hospital licence, which would threaten 70% of revenue. This is the most significant risk because of its severity, even though the likelihood is moderate.

2. Data security risk (operational, could become strategic): Video consultations will transmit sensitive patient data through a third-party app. A breach would bring regulatory penalties and serious reputational damage among patients who value confidentiality.

3. Clinical quality risk: Doctors cannot physically examine patients, so misdiagnosis is more likely. This creates litigation risk and patient harm.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • "Evaluate" = name the risk + explain the cause from the case + the consequence + how significant it is.
  • Prioritise. Saying which risk is most significant and why earns evaluation and commercial acumen marks.
  • Usually 3–5 well-developed risks are better than 12 one-liners.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Listing generic risks ("there is a risk of fraud") without linking them to the case.
  • Calling "reputation" the risk without saying what causes it. Reputation is usually a consequence.
  • Forgetting to classify when the task asks you to distinguish.
⑦ Memory tip Strategic = "Should we?" (board). Operational = "Are we doing it right?" (managers).
Climate risk = "Weather & Rules": physical (weather) and transition (rules, tech, markets).
Writing a risk = C-R-C: Cause (case fact) → Risk event → Consequence (£/reputation/patients).
4D1e

Risk attitude, risk appetite and risk policy

① Simple explanation
TermSimple meaningPersonal analogy
Risk attitudeThe general mindset of the board towards risk: averse, neutral or seeking."I'm a cautious person."
Risk appetiteThe amount and type of risk the organisation is willing to accept to achieve its objectives."I'll put 20% of my savings into shares."
Risk capacityThe maximum risk the organisation is able to bear before it fails."If I lost more than 50%, I couldn't pay my mortgage."
Risk toleranceThe acceptable variation around an objective, often a measurable limit."I accept a fall of up to 10% in a year."
  • Risk-averse: prefers certainty and accepts lower returns (typical of public sector bodies, charities, hospitals on safety matters).
  • Risk-neutral: judges purely on expected value.
  • Risk-seeking: chases high returns and accepts high uncertainty (typical of tech start-ups, venture capital).

Risk policy is the written rules that put the appetite into practice: limits, approval levels, which risks are never accepted.

Rule: appetite must always be below capacity. If a board's appetite exceeds its capacity, it is gambling with the company's survival.

What shapes risk appetite?

  • Stakeholders' expectations: shareholders in a tech growth fund vs taxpayers funding a hospital
  • Industry/sector: aviation and healthcare have very low appetite for safety risk
  • Financial strength: high cash and low gearing allow more risk
  • Personal views of directors: a founder-entrepreneur vs a cautious ex-civil servant
  • Regulation: banks have regulatory capital limits
  • Stage of the business life cycle: start-ups seek risk; mature firms protect what they have
② Real business example

One organisation can have different appetites for different risks. A government health authority might state:

  • Patient safety: zero / very low appetite
  • Regulatory compliance: low
  • Financial: low–moderate (must stay within budget)
  • Innovation (e.g. piloting AI triage): moderate, because it wants to modernise

Compare a fintech start-up: high appetite for market and innovation risk, but it still needs low appetite for regulatory risk, because losing its licence would end the business.

③ How it appears in the exam
  • "Discuss how the board's attitude to risk may affect its decision on the proposed investment."
  • Case clue: the founder says "We didn't get here by playing safe!" while the new NED is cautious. This is a conflict of risk attitudes.
④ Model answer extract

The CEO's comments in Exhibit 1 suggest a risk-seeking attitude, having built the business through bold acquisitions. However, the company is now listed and its shareholders include pension funds who, according to the investor letter, expect steady dividends. This suggests a lower appetite than the CEO's.

The proposed USD 80m acquisition would raise gearing from 35% to 68%, close to the 70% bank covenant. This brings the company near its risk capacity: a single bad year could trigger a covenant breach. The board should therefore formally agree a risk appetite statement, for example a maximum gearing of 50%, and assess the acquisition against it, rather than letting the CEO's personal attitude drive the decision.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Spot whose attitude is driving decisions and whether it matches what stakeholders expect.
  • Use numbers to show capacity (gearing, cash, covenants).
  • Recommend a formal risk appetite statement approved by the board.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Using appetite, attitude and capacity as if they meant the same thing.
  • Saying "the company is risk-averse" without case evidence.
⑦ Memory tip Attitude = personality. Appetite = how much I WANT to eat. Capacity = how much my stomach CAN hold. Tolerance = how far from the plan I'll accept.
5D1f

The dynamic nature of risk: how risk varies

① Simple explanation

Risks don't stay still. New risks appear, old ones disappear, and probability and impact change as the world and the organisation change. That's why a risk register must be a living document, not a once-a-year exercise.

Risk also varies with these features of an organisation:

FactorHow it changes risk
SizeSmall firms depend on a few people, customers and suppliers, so key-person risk is high. Large firms face complexity, slow communication and control risk across many sites.
StructureDecentralised or divisional structures mean local managers may take risks the centre doesn't see. Matrix structures blur accountability.
IndustryPharma: product liability and R&D failure. Banks: credit and liquidity. Tech: obsolescence. Healthcare: clinical risk.
SectorPublic sector: political, budget and accountability risk. Private: market and shareholder risk. Charities: donor and reputational risk.
Development (life cycle)Start-up: survival and cash-burn risk. Growth: overtrading and control lagging behind growth. Mature: strategic drift. Decline: going-concern risk.
② Real business example

A food delivery app over 6 years:

  • Year 1 (start-up): runs out of cash; app doesn't work; founders are the only people who know the code.
  • Year 3 (rapid growth): controls fail to keep up, with fake driver accounts and refund fraud; rider employment law challenges.
  • Year 6 (mature, listed): regulatory scrutiny, competition from bigger platforms, ESG pressure about rider welfare and packaging waste.

The same company faces a completely different risk profile at each stage.

③ How it appears in the exam

"The company has grown from 50 to 600 staff in three years. Discuss how its risk profile has changed and why the existing approach to risk management is no longer adequate."

④ Model answer extract

When the company had 50 staff, the two founders knew every customer and approved every payment personally. This informal control was effective at that size. With 600 staff across four countries, the founders can no longer oversee everything, yet Exhibit 2 shows they still approve all spending over USD 5,000 by email. This causes delays, and expenses below that limit are not reviewed at all, which creates fraud risk.

Expansion abroad has also brought new risks (currency, local labour law, tax compliance) that the current risk register, written three years ago, does not include. Because risk is dynamic, the company should move to formal ERM with quarterly register reviews.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Compare then vs now with case facts.
  • Conclude with what must change in risk management.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Describing life cycle theory without applying it to the case.
⑦ Memory tip "SSISD": Size, Structure, Industry, Sector, Development. Risk is like weather: check it daily, not yearly.
6D1g

Assessing severity and probability

① Simple explanation

Once risks are identified, you must decide which matter most. You score each risk on two things:

  • Probability (likelihood): how likely is it to happen? (e.g. 1 = rare to 5 = almost certain)
  • Severity (impact/hazard): how bad would it be? (e.g. 1 = negligible to 5 = catastrophic)

Risk score = Probability × Impact. A risk scoring 4 × 5 = 20 needs action before a risk scoring 2 × 2 = 4.

Gross (inherent) risk is the risk before any controls. Net (residual) risk is the risk after controls. If net risk is still above appetite, more action is needed.

Quantitative vs qualitative: some risks can be measured in money (expected value, sensitivity, scenario analysis, Value at Risk). Others need judgement (reputation, morale).

② Real business example
Risk (hospital)ProbabilityImpactScorePriority
Ransomware attack on patient records4520🔴 Critical
Nurse shortage in ICU4416🔴 High
Insurer pays late428🟠 Medium
Earthquake damages building155🟡 Low–Med (but plan for it)

Quantified example: a 10% chance of a AED 5m regulatory fine gives an expected loss of AED 0.5m. If a compliance system costs AED 0.2m a year and halves the probability, it saves AED 0.25m in expected loss, so it's worth doing even before you count the reputational benefit.

③ How it appears in the exam
  • "Assess the risks in Exhibit 3 and advise which should be prioritised."
  • You may get a spreadsheet exhibit with probabilities to calculate expected values.
④ Model answer extract

Although an earthquake would be catastrophic, the probability is very low. The board should focus first on the ransomware risk, which is both highly likely (Exhibit 4 notes three attempted attacks on regional hospitals this year) and severe, since all surgeries would stop if patient records were unavailable. This should be rated critical and dealt with immediately. The earthquake risk is better handled by insurance and a business continuity plan than by significant investment.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Justify the rating with case evidence for both likelihood and impact.
  • Be sceptical where you see a reason to: "Management rates this as low, but given…, this appears understated." That earns scepticism skill marks.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Giving scores without reasons.
  • Ignoring low-probability, high-impact risks completely. They still need a plan, usually transfer (insurance) or contingency.
⑦ Memory tip "How Likely? How Ugly?" Likely × Ugly = Priority. Gross = Before, Net = After (think "gross salary before tax, net after").
7D1h

Related and correlated risk factors

① Simple explanation

Related risks are connected, so one risk can trigger or worsen another, like dominoes.

Correlated risks move together statistically:

  • Positive correlation: when risk A rises, risk B rises too. This is dangerous because risks pile up together.
  • Negative correlation: when A rises, B falls. This is useful for hedging or diversifying.

Why it matters: if you assess each risk alone, you underestimate the total risk when several are positively correlated.

② Real business example

Domino chain (related risks) in a hospital:

Nurse shortage → more agency staff → higher costs and agency staff unfamiliar with procedures → more clinical errors → patient complaints → reputational damage → fewer insured patients → lower revenue → less money to recruit nurses → worse nurse shortage 🔁

Positive correlation: an airline's fuel costs and a tourism downturn often rise together during an oil price shock, hitting both cost and revenue at once.

Negative correlation: a company that sells both umbrellas and sunscreen. A rainy summer hurts sunscreen sales but helps umbrellas.

Environmental–social–governance link: pollution from a factory (environmental) leads to community health complaints (social), which leads to regulatory fines and director liability (governance).

③ How it appears in the exam

"Explain how the risks identified are related and why this matters for the board's risk assessment."

④ Model answer extract

The risks in Exhibit 3 should not be assessed in isolation. The supplier's financial difficulty (supply risk) and the plan to stop holding safety stock (operational decision) are positively correlated. If the supplier fails, the lack of buffer stock means production stops within two days instead of four weeks. Taken together, the impact is far greater than the separate ratings suggest. The board should consider these risks as a group and postpone the stock reduction until a second supplier has been approved.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Show you can connect exhibits. This is a sign of a strong candidate (analysis skill).
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Defining correlation in maths terms only, with no business consequence.
⑦ Memory tip Positive correlation = "trouble comes in pairs." Negative correlation = "umbrella and sunscreen."
8D2a

The role of the risk manager (and the risk committee)

① Simple explanation

The risk manager (or Chief Risk Officer, CRO, in large organisations) does not own all the risks. Line managers own their risks. The risk manager builds the system and supports everyone.

Typical duties

  • Provide the overall leadership and framework for ERM: policies, methods, templates
  • Help develop and communicate the risk appetite and policy
  • Build a risk-aware culture through training and awareness
  • Coordinate and maintain the risk register; consolidate risks across departments
  • Prepare risk reports for the board and risk committee
  • Coordinate responses to major incidents and business continuity planning
  • Manage insurance (risk transfer)
  • Ensure compliance with regulation
  • Investigate incidents and share lessons learned

Is the role effective? Assess these factors

  • Seniority and authority: are they listened to by the board?
  • Independence: do they report to the risk committee, not only the CEO?
  • Resources: do they have staff, budget and systems?
  • Skills: technical, communication, industry knowledge
  • Culture: does management see the risk manager as a partner or a "police officer"?

Risk committee (board level)

A board committee, ideally made up mainly of independent NEDs, that oversees risk appetite, the risk framework, the top risks, and the work of the risk manager. In smaller firms the audit committee may cover risk.

② Real business example

A hospital appointed a risk manager two years ago, but they report to the Finance Director, are graded below department heads, and have no team. Surgeons ignore their requests to update the clinical risk register. The role exists on paper but is ineffective because of a lack of authority and independence.

③ How it appears in the exam

"Draft a briefing paper for the board explaining the role of a risk manager and assessing whether the current arrangements are effective."

④ Model answer extract

Assessment of current arrangements: The risk manager role is undermined in three ways. First, reporting to the FD means clinical and operational risks are seen through a financial lens and may be filtered before reaching the board. Second, the role's junior grade means senior clinicians do not engage, which is shown by only 4 of 11 departments submitting risk updates (Exhibit 2). Third, without staff the risk manager spends most of the time compiling spreadsheets rather than advising.

Recommendations: (1) Give the risk manager a direct reporting line to the chair of a new board risk committee. (2) Raise the grade to department head level. (3) Have the CEO formally require all departments to update risks quarterly, setting the right tone from the top.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Don't only list duties. Assess effectiveness using case evidence.
  • Recommendations should be specific and practical.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Saying "the risk manager is responsible for managing all risks". No: risk owners manage risks, and the board is ultimately accountable.
⑦ Memory tip The risk manager is the "coach", not the "player". Players (line managers) own the risks; the coach builds the system; the club owner (board) is accountable.

④ Model answer extract (critiquing a register)

Weaknesses in the current risk register (Exhibit 6):

  • Vague descriptions: "IT risk" gives no cause or consequence, so no one can design a response. It should read: "Unpatched servers → ransomware attack → loss of patient records for several days."
  • No owners: 7 of 10 risks have no named owner, so no one is accountable for action.
  • Out of date: last reviewed in January, before the merger, so merger-related integration risks are missing entirely.
  • Gross only: no net risk is shown, so the board cannot tell whether controls work.
  • Missing risks: climate-related risk is absent despite the coastal location noted in the pre-seen.
  • Questionable ratings: cyber risk is rated "low", yet Exhibit 4 reports two phishing incidents this quarter. This appears understated.

Benefits of a heat map: it would let board members, many without a technical background, see immediately that cyber and staffing risks sit in the red zone. That focuses limited board time on what matters most.

Limitations: heat maps simplify reality. Scores are subjective, they don't show correlations between risks, and they give a snapshot that can quickly go out of date.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • "Evaluate" means strengths and weaknesses, plus limitations of the tool itself.
  • Questioning management's ratings = scepticism marks.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Only describing what a register is.
  • Forgetting the limitations of heat maps.
⑦ Memory tip A good register entry answers "What, Who, How bad, What now, When next?" (description, owner, score, response, review date).
10D2c

Embedding risk in culture and values

① Simple explanation

Embedded risk management means thinking about risk is part of everyone's normal job, not a separate box-ticking exercise done by the risk team once a year. Like safety on an oil rig: every worker thinks about it automatically.

How to embed it

  • Tone from the top: the board and CEO talk about risk and act on it
  • Policies and procedures that include risk steps (e.g. every business case must include a risk section)
  • Training and induction for all staff
  • Clear risk ownership in job descriptions
  • Performance appraisal and rewards that include risk behaviour, not just sales or targets
  • No-blame reporting of near-misses and whistleblowing channels
  • Communication: share lessons from incidents

Benefits vs barriers

✅ Benefits

Early warning of problems; better decisions; fewer incidents; staff who protect the business; regulator and investor confidence.

⛔ Barriers

Cost and time; staff see it as bureaucracy; blame culture stops reporting; targets reward risk-taking; leaders who don't lead by example.

Link to Chapter A: use the cultural web to diagnose why risk isn't embedded (stories, symbols, power structures, controls, rituals).

② Real business example

Aviation-style "just culture" in a hospital: a nurse gives the wrong dose but reports it immediately. In a blame culture she would be disciplined, so next time nobody reports. In a just culture the hospital investigates the system (the two drug labels looked almost identical) and changes the supplier's labelling. The whole organisation learns.

Counter-example: a bank whose sales staff are paid bonuses purely on the number of accounts opened. Risk policies say "treat customers fairly", but the reward system says "sell at any cost". This leads to mis-selling scandals. The culture beats the policy.

③ How it appears in the exam

"Advise how the CEO could embed risk awareness into the culture of the organisation following the incident."

④ Model answer extract

The interviews in Exhibit 3 suggest staff hid the faulty equipment because they feared being blamed. Risk cannot be embedded while this fear exists. I recommend:

  1. The CEO should personally launch a no-blame incident reporting system and publicly thank staff who report issues.
  2. Appraisals should include a risk objective for all managers, so ward managers are judged on safety as well as bed occupancy.
  3. A monthly "lessons learned" bulletin should show staff that reported risks lead to real changes.

These actions cost little but will take 12–18 months to change behaviour, so the board should track progress using the number of near-misses reported (a rise is initially a good sign).

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Link to leadership and tone from the top, which ties in syllabus area A.
  • Give practical, sequenced actions.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Saying "provide training" and stopping there. Training alone doesn't change culture.
⑦ Memory tip "Policy on paper, culture in the canteen." To embed risk, align TRAP: Tone, Rewards, Accountability, Processes.
11D2d

Spreading and diversifying risk

① Simple explanation

"Don't put all your eggs in one basket."

  • Spreading risk: splitting an exposure across several sources so one failure isn't fatal: multiple suppliers, several data centres, a customer base not reliant on one client, staff cross-trained.
  • Diversification: operating in a range of products, markets or investments whose risks are not perfectly positively correlated, so losses in one are offset by gains in another.

When is it appropriate?

  • ✅ When there is high concentration (one supplier, one customer, one market)
  • ✅ When the risks are uncorrelated or negatively correlated
  • ⚠️ Not always: diversifying into areas you don't understand can add risk and destroy value. Shareholders can diversify their own portfolios, so they may prefer focused companies.
  • ⚠️ Spreading has costs: losing volume discounts, managing more relationships

Link to Strategic Choices: Ansoff diversification and BCG portfolios are partly about spreading strategic risk.

② Real business example

Spreading: after a fire at a single glove factory in 2021, many hospitals ran short. A health authority now requires every critical consumable to have at least two approved suppliers from different countries.

Diversification gone wrong: a successful bottled water company diversifies into restaurants. It has no restaurant expertise, management attention is stretched, and both businesses suffer.

Natural diversification: a beverage group selling hot tea (strong in winter) and iced drinks (strong in summer) smooths seasonal cash flows.

③ How it appears in the exam

"Discuss whether diversifying the supplier base / entering a new market would reduce the company's overall risk."

④ Model answer extract

Exhibit 2 shows 85% of the hospital's surgical supplies come from one distributor. This concentration means any disruption at that distributor would stop most surgeries. Adding a second distributor would spread the risk. Although Exhibit 2 notes a 3% volume discount would be lost (around AED 180k a year), this is small compared with the AED 1m+ weekly revenue at risk if surgeries were cancelled. I therefore recommend a dual-sourcing strategy with a 70/30 split, keeping most of the volume discount while removing the single point of failure.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Weigh cost vs benefit using the case numbers.
  • Show balance: diversification isn't automatically good.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Recommending diversification without considering correlation, cost or competence.
⑦ Memory tip Eggs in many baskets, but only baskets you know how to carry.

Risk responses: the TARA model ⭐ (most examined)

① Simple explanation

Once a risk is assessed, choose one (or a mix) of four responses. Which one depends on likelihood and impact:

Low likelihood
High likelihood
IMPACT (severity) → High at top
TRANSFERLow likelihood, high impact.
Pass it to someone else: insurance, outsourcing, contracts, hedging, joint ventures.
AVOIDHigh likelihood, high impact.
Don't do the activity, or stop it. Exit the market, cancel the project.
ACCEPTLow likelihood, low impact.
Live with it and monitor. Cost of control > benefit.
REDUCEHigh likelihood, low impact.
Controls to cut likelihood or impact: training, systems, procedures.
LIKELIHOOD (probability) →
More detail
  • Transfer (share): insurance, outsourcing with penalty clauses, hedging FX with forwards, sharing risk with a JV partner. Note: reputational risk usually cannot be transferred.
  • Avoid (terminate): the risk is outside appetite and can't be reduced cheaply. This often means giving up an opportunity.
  • Reduce (treat/mitigate): the most common response. Use preventive controls (to cut likelihood) and detective/corrective controls (to cut impact).
  • Accept (tolerate/retain): a conscious decision, ideally with monitoring and contingency. "Self-insurance" is a form of acceptance.

Two things to remember: (1) the matrix is a guide, not a rule. Appetite and cost–benefit matter too. (2) Net risk after the response should fall within appetite.

③ How it appears in the exam
  • "Using an appropriate framework, recommend how X should respond to each of the risks identified." Classic
  • Often combined: "Identify the key risks and recommend responses" (e.g. 15–20 marks).
④ Model answer extract

Risk 1: Ransomware attack on patient records (high likelihood, high impact)
In principle a risk this severe would be avoided, but the hospital cannot stop using digital records. So the board should reduce the risk urgently through offline backups tested monthly, multi-factor authentication and phishing training. It should also transfer the remaining financial impact through cyber insurance. Reputational damage cannot be insured, which is why reduction must come first.

Risk 2: Overseas patient legal claims (low likelihood, high impact)
Transfer through international medical malpractice insurance, and require patients to sign contracts specifying UAE jurisdiction.

Risk 3: Expanding into cosmetic surgery in Country Z (Exhibit 4: unregulated market, two clinics closed after scandals)
The likelihood of reputational damage is high and the impact on the group's core reputation for clinical quality would be severe. This is outside the board's stated low appetite for reputational risk. Avoid: I recommend the board does not proceed.

Risk 4: Minor delays in non-clinical supplies
Accept and monitor. The cost of extra controls would exceed the small impact.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Justify each response. Why this one, using likelihood, impact, appetite and cost.
  • Give a specific action (not just "reduce", but how).
  • Combining responses (reduce + transfer) shows depth.
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Writing the label only ("Transfer.") with no action. That's almost zero marks.
  • Recommending "avoid" for a core activity the business can't stop.
  • Claiming reputational risk can be insured.
⑦ Memory tip Draw the 2×2 in your planning. Going clockwise from bottom-left: Accept → Transfer → Avoid → Reduce.
Or: "Small & rare? Accept. Big & rare? Insure. Big & frequent? Run! Small & frequent? Fix it."
13D2f

Accepting some risk: the ALARP principle

① Simple explanation

ALARP = "As Low As Reasonably Practicable." Risk should be reduced until the cost of reducing it further (money, time, effort) becomes grossly disproportionate to the benefit gained. After that point, the remaining risk is accepted.

You can never make anything 100% safe. A hospital with zero infection risk would have to close. ALARP is about being reasonable.

ZoneMeaning
Unacceptable regionRisk is intolerable. Must reduce or stop, whatever the cost.
ALARP / tolerable regionAccept only if further reduction is impracticable or the cost is grossly disproportionate.
Broadly acceptable regionRisk is negligible. Just monitor.

Why accept some risk at all? Taking risk is how businesses earn returns and compete. Over-controlling causes high costs, slow decisions, lost opportunities and less innovation.

② Real business example

Hospital car park: the risk of a slip on a wet surface. Anti-slip paint, signage and lighting cost AED 50k and cut accidents by 80%, so do it. Covering the entire car park with a roof would cost AED 8m to remove the last small risk. That is grossly disproportionate, so the remaining risk is accepted under ALARP.

Tech company: releasing software with zero bugs would take years. It releases when critical bugs are fixed and accepts minor ones, fixing them in updates. This keeps it competitive.

③ How it appears in the exam

"A director argues all risks should be eliminated. Explain why this is neither possible nor desirable, referring to ALARP."

④ Model answer extract

The Operations Director's demand for "zero risk" in the new laboratory is understandable given the serious incident last year, but it is neither achievable nor commercially sensible. Some residual risk exists in any activity. The goal should be to reduce risk to as low as reasonably practicable.

The proposed controls (Exhibit 5) already reduce the contamination risk by 90% for AED 300k. The additional automated sealing system would cost AED 2.5m to remove a further 2%. That cost is grossly disproportionate to the benefit, and would delay the laboratory by 6 months, during which patients would wait longer for test results. I recommend the board accepts the remaining risk, documents the ALARP justification, and monitors it through monthly contamination tests.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Use numbers to show disproportion.
  • Professionally counter-argue the director's view (communication skill: persuade and counter-argue).
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Using ALARP to justify accepting risks in the unacceptable zone (e.g. serious patient safety risks).
⑦ Memory tip ALARP = "Stop spending when the next dirham buys almost no safety."
14D2g

Assurance mapping and the four lines of defence

① Simple explanation

The board needs assurance, meaning confidence, that key risks are really being managed. Different groups give that assurance at different levels of independence:

1st
Operational management: the people who own and manage risk day to day. Controls built into processes (e.g. ward manager checks drug stock; supervisor approves invoices).
2nd
Risk management and compliance functions: set frameworks, monitor the 1st line, challenge it (risk manager, compliance officer, health & safety, information security).
3rd
Internal audit: independent, objective assurance on whether lines 1 and 2 work. Reports to the audit committee.
4th
External assurance: external auditors, regulators (e.g. health authority inspectors), and other independent external reviewers. Fully outside the organisation.
Assurance mapping

An assurance map is a chart showing, for each key risk, which line(s) provide assurance, how strong that assurance is, and where there are gaps (no one checking) or duplications (three teams checking the same thing).

Key risk1st line2nd line3rd line (IA)4th lineOverall
Patient safety✅ Strong✅ Clinical governance✅ Annual audit✅ Health regulator🟢 Possible duplication
Cyber security⚠️ Weak❌ None❌ Not audited in 3 yrs❌ None🔴 Gap
Financial reporting✅✅ Finance control✅✅ External auditor🟢
Supplier fraud⚠️❌⚠️ Partial❌🟠 Gap

Benefits: shows the board where it's relying on hope; avoids duplicated effort and wasted cost; helps internal audit plan its work on the riskiest areas; supports the board's annual statement on risk and control.

Limitations: takes time; relies on honest self-assessment; can become a static document.

② Real business example

A government health department's assurance map reveals that patient-safety reviews are performed by the ward, the quality team, internal audit and the regulator, so there is lots of overlap. Meanwhile no one independently checks the AI triage tool introduced last year. The board redirects some internal audit time from patient safety to a review of the AI tool's accuracy and data controls.

③ How it appears in the exam

"Explain the four lines of defence model and assess how assurance mapping could help the audit committee."

④ Model answer extract

The audit committee currently receives separate reports from compliance, internal audit and the external auditor but has no overall view of which risks are covered. An assurance map would plot each of the top 15 risks against the four lines. Based on Exhibit 5, this would quickly show that cyber risk has no second- or third-line assurance: the IT team marks its own work, and internal audit has not reviewed IT for three years. Given the ransomware threat, this is a serious gap. In contrast, financial controls are reviewed by all four lines, suggesting some internal audit effort could be moved. I recommend internal audit prepares an assurance map before setting next year's audit plan.

⑤ Examiner expectations
  • Identify gaps and overlaps from the case. That's where the marks are.
  • Link to internal audit and the audit committee (ties in Chapter F).
⑥ Common mistakes
  • Mixing up the order (internal audit is the 3rd line, not the 2nd).
  • Forgetting that the 1st line owns the risk.
⑦ Memory tip "Do it → Check it → Audit it → Inspect it." Managers → Risk/Compliance → Internal audit → External auditors/Regulators.
Case study

Integrated practice: Sandhill Health Network

Fictitious government-owned hospital network. Try writing your own answer first (35 minutes), then compare with the model.

Exhibit 1 – Background

Sandhill Health Network (SHN) is owned by a regional government and runs four hospitals and 12 clinics, employing 6,500 staff. Its funding comes 70% from the government budget and 30% from private insurers. The government has set SHN two targets for the next three years: cut waiting times for surgery by 40%, and stay within budget. Any overspend must be explained to the government's Finance Committee.

Exhibit 2 – CEO's proposal (board minutes)

The CEO proposes "Project Swift": a USD 35m programme to (a) introduce an AI-based triage and scheduling system, supplied by a start-up, TriageNow, and (b) outsource all hospital laboratory testing to a private lab company. The CEO said: "We must be bold. Waiting times are a political issue and the minister wants results by next year." A NED asked about risks. The CEO replied: "The finance team will handle that."

Exhibit 3 – Risk register extract (last updated 18 months ago)

Exhibit 4 – Email from Head of IT

Task (30 marks, including 6 professional skills marks)

You are an adviser to the board's audit and risk committee. Prepare a briefing paper that:

(a) Evaluates the key risks of Project Swift. (12 marks)
(b) Recommends responses to these risks using an appropriate framework. (8 marks)
(c) Assesses SHN's current approach to risk management and recommends improvements. (4 marks)

Professional skills: Evaluation (judgement, prioritising) and Scepticism (challenging the CEO's assumptions). (6 marks)

📝 Show the distinction-level model answer
Briefing paper

To: Audit and Risk Committee, SHN · From: Risk Adviser · Re: Risks of Project Swift

Purpose: To evaluate the main risks of Project Swift, recommend responses, and comment on SHN's risk management arrangements.

(a) Key risks

1. Supplier (third-party) risk: TriageNow (strategic, high likelihood, high impact). TriageNow is a three-year-old start-up with only two clients. There is a real chance it fails financially or cannot support a network of SHN's size. SHN would then be locked into a system with no support, and scheduling of patients across four hospitals could fail. This would directly undermine the waiting-time target that the whole project is meant to achieve. I rate this as one of the two most significant risks.

2. Cyber and data security risk (operational, but could become strategic). The system needs full access to patient records, and SHN already faces frequent phishing (four attempts last month). Granting a small external supplier full access widens the attack surface. A breach of patient data would bring regulatory penalties and a loss of public trust, which would be politically damaging for a government-owned body. The register rates "IT risk" as low, but this evidence suggests it is significantly understated.

3. Clinical and ethical risk of AI triage. If the algorithm prioritises patients incorrectly, for example because it was trained on data from a different population, seriously ill patients could wait too long. This creates patient harm and liability, and raises ethical questions about accountability for decisions. SHN's appetite for patient safety risk should be close to zero.

4. Outsourcing concentration risk: lab company. The lab is the only one in the region with enough capacity. Outsourcing all testing creates a single point of failure and weakens SHN's negotiating position at contract renewal, so prices could rise. If the lab suffers an outage, diagnosis across all hospitals stops.

5. Related and correlated risks: these risks are linked. The 40% waiting-time target and the political pressure "for results by next year" create a risk of rushed implementation, which increases the likelihood of risks 1–4. Delay or failure would also cause a budget overspend that must be explained to the Finance Committee. So the risks reinforce each other.

Prioritisation: risks 1 and 2 are the most urgent because they are both likely and severe, and the project cannot succeed without addressing them.

(b) Recommended responses (TARA)

  • TriageNow supplier risk: Reduce and Transfer. Carry out financial and technical due diligence before signing. Require source code escrow so SHN can keep the system running if TriageNow fails. Include penalty clauses and service level agreements. Pilot the system in one hospital for six months before roll-out across the network.
  • Cyber risk: Reduce, then Transfer the residual. Grant TriageNow access only to the minimum data needed, enforce multi-factor authentication, commission an independent penetration test before go-live, and buy cyber insurance. Reputational damage cannot be insured, so reduction is the priority.
  • Clinical AI risk: Reduce. Keep a clinician involved in every triage decision during the pilot, validate the algorithm against local patient data, and set up clinical governance oversight. If the pilot shows unsafe outcomes, avoid by abandoning the AI element.
  • Lab outsourcing risk: Reduce through spreading. Outsource routine tests only and keep an in-house capability for urgent tests, so diagnosis can continue if the lab fails. Negotiate a long-term contract with price caps.

(c) Current approach to risk management

SHN's approach is weak. The CEO's statement that "the finance team will handle" risk shows a poor tone from the top and treats risk as a financial matter only. The register is 18 months out of date, has vague descriptions ("IT risk"), a missing owner, and does not include the risks of Project Swift. The NED's question was dismissed, which suggests board challenge is not encouraged.

Recommendations: (1) The board should require a full risk assessment of Project Swift before approving funding. (2) Appoint a risk manager reporting to this committee. (3) Agree a risk appetite statement with explicitly low appetite for patient safety and data risk. (4) Review the risk register quarterly.

Conclusion

Project Swift could deliver the waiting-time target, but at present its risks are unassessed and in some cases understated. I recommend the board approves only a phased pilot, conditional on the safeguards above. This balances the government's demand for faster results against SHN's duty to protect patients and public funds.

🎯 Why this answer scores highly (marker's view)
  • Format: a briefing paper with To/From/Purpose (communication marks).
  • Every risk uses case facts: start-up with 2 clients, 4 phishing attempts, only lab in the region.
  • Scepticism: challenges the "Low" IT rating and the CEO's dismissal of risk.
  • Evaluation: prioritises risks and concludes with a balanced, conditional recommendation.
  • Commercial acumen: recognises the political pressure and the public sector context (government budget, Finance Committee).
  • Technical: ERM thinking, TARA, correlated risks, spreading, AI ethics (links to Chapter E).
Exam technique

Turning risk knowledge into marks

A 4-step answer template for any risk task

  1. Identify: name the risk, with its cause from the case.
  2. Assess: likelihood and impact, with evidence. Is it strategic or operational? How significant?
  3. Respond: TARA plus the specific action, and who should own it.
  4. Monitor: KRI, review frequency, assurance from which line.

Command verbs

VerbWhat the marker wants
IdentifyName the risk with a brief case link
ExplainMake it clear: what, why, consequence
Assess / EvaluateWeigh significance, pros and cons, reach a judgement
Advise / RecommendClear action, justified, practical, with who and when
DiscussExplore more than one side

Where the professional skills marks are in risk tasks

🔍 Scepticism

Question management's risk ratings, overly optimistic forecasts, a CEO who says "there's no risk", or unverified supplier claims.

⚖️ Evaluation

Prioritise risks, weigh cost vs benefit of controls, and give a clear final recommendation.

💼 Commercial acumen

Use the industry context (patient safety, political pressure, regulators) and realistic solutions.

📣 Communication

Right format (briefing note, email, slides), right tone for the audience, concise headings.

Time guide

About 1.95 minutes per mark (195 min ÷ 100). A 20-mark risk task ≈ 39 minutes: about 8 minutes reading and planning, 31 minutes writing. Aim for roughly one well-developed point (3–4 sentences) per 2 marks.

Top 7 chapter-wide mistakes
  1. Generic risks with no case link
  2. TARA labels with no action
  3. No prioritisation or overall conclusion
  4. Ignoring the role or audience (writing an essay instead of a briefing note)
  5. Using the outdated 8-component COSO cube
  6. Forgetting upside risk and opportunities
  7. Spending too long on one risk and running out of time
