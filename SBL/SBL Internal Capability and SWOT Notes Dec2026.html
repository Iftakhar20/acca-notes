    1Where this chapter fits

    So far you have looked outside the organisation. This chapter turns the camera around and looks inside.

    PESTEL: the wider world›Five Forces: the industry›This chapter: the organisation›SWOT: all three together

    The earlier chapters gave you opportunities and threats. This chapter gives you strengths and weaknesses. SWOT then joins them, and the result feeds your next chapter on strategic choices.

    The story so far: Maison Varenne

    Varenne is our fictional Swiss luxury watchmaker. Chairman Henri dismissed smartwatches as gadgets, young analyst Sara warned about strategic drift, and her mentor Marc taught her to write "so what" after every point. Sara has mapped the outside world. Henri now asks her a harder question:

    "Every watch brand says it has heritage and quality. What do we actually have that nobody else can copy?"

    That question is this whole chapter.

    Two views of competitive advantage

    Positioning view (Porter)

    Advantage comes from choosing an attractive industry and a good position in it. Look outside first.

    Resource-based view

    Advantage comes from what the organisation has and does that others cannot copy. Look inside first.

    A strong exam answer uses both. The market must want what you offer (outside), and you must be able to deliver it better than rivals (inside).

    A note on the models

    The syllabus names threshold resources, unique resources, core competences and SWOT. VRIN, TOWS, SECI and dynamic capabilities are not named in the study guide. They are standard tools for evaluating these outcomes, so use them when they help, but the marks come from your judgement about the case, not from naming a model.

    2Resources and competences

    Syllabus C4a

    Step 1: In simple language

    Resources are what an organisation has. Competences are what it does well with them. Together they make up its strategic capability.

    Strategic capability

    The resources and competences an organisation uses to survive and prosper.

    The kitchen test

    Two restaurants buy the same oven, the same ingredients and the same recipe book. Those are resources. One restaurant serves wonderful food on time every night, and the other is chaos. The difference is competence: how the team plans, cooks and works together. Buying equipment gives you a resource. It does not give you the ability to use it well.

    The four kinds of resource

    Resources can be tangible (you can touch them or count them) or intangible (you cannot). Intangibles are often the hardest to copy.

    TypeMeaningVarenneA hospital
    PhysicalBuildings, machines, stock, locationValley workshop, precision toolsTheatres, scanners, wards
    FinancialCash, access to borrowing, investorsFamily capital, low debtGovernment grant, insurer income
    HumanPeople, skills, experience, relationshipsMaster watchmakersSpecialist surgeons and nurses
    IntellectualPatents, brands, data, know-how, reputation200-year-old brand, archive of movement designsClinical data, accreditation, reputation
    Memory tip

    "Please Feed Hungry Ibex" gives you Physical, Financial, Human, Intellectual. An ibex is a Swiss mountain goat, so it stays with Varenne.

    Nouns are resources. Verbs are competences. "A trained sales team" is a resource. "Winning long-term contracts from large customers" is a competence.

    Competences live between people

    A competence is rarely owned by one person. It usually comes from several activities and teams working together: how a firm shares information, makes decisions and repeats good work. This is why competences are harder to copy than machines.

    Resource (has)Competence (does well)
    Fleet of 400 delivery vansPlanning routes so that 98% of parcels arrive on time
    Customer databaseUsing the data to predict what each customer will buy next
    Cash reserves of $50mChoosing and integrating acquisitions successfully
    Qualified nursesMoving patients from emergency to a ward within 4 hours

    3Threshold or distinctive?

    Syllabus C4a. This is the core idea of the chapter.

    Step 1: In simple language

    Some capabilities are the ticket to play. Others are the reason you win.

    • Threshold capabilities are the minimum needed to compete at all. Without them, customers will not consider you. Having them does not make you special, because every serious rival has them too.
    • Distinctive capabilities are the ones customers value and rivals struggle to match. These create competitive advantage.

    Threshold resources

    What you must have to compete.

    Varenne: a Swiss workshop, "Swiss Made" compliance, quality materials.

    Threshold competences

    What you must do adequately to compete.

    Varenne: assembling reliable watches, after-sales servicing.

    Unique resources

    What you have that rivals cannot easily get.

    Varenne: its movement-design archive and brand history.

    Core competences

    What you do better than rivals, in a way that is hard to copy.

    Varenne: hand-finishing complicated movements, passed from master to apprentice.

    Sara's first draft

    Sara lists "precise movements, Swiss location, good quality control" as Varenne's advantages. Marc crosses them all out.

    "Every brand in this valley has those. They keep you in the game, but they do not win it. Ask what a collector would miss if Varenne vanished tomorrow."

    Sara thinks again. Collectors would miss hand-finished complicated movements and a design archive reaching back two centuries. Those are the distinctive items.

    Three things that change over time

    • Thresholds rise. Yesterday's advantage becomes tomorrow's minimum. Online banking once made a bank special. Today a bank without an app would lose customers.
    • Thresholds differ by segment. Excellent cold storage may be distinctive for a general courier, yet threshold for a medicine courier.
    • Too much threshold can waste money. If you spend heavily on something customers treat as a basic, you add cost without adding value. Meet the threshold, then spend on what makes you different.

    The core competence test (Prahalad and Hamel)

    A real core competence passes three tests:

    1. Customer value: it makes a real difference to what customers care about.
    2. Different from rivals: competitors cannot easily match it.
    3. Extendable: it opens the door to other products or markets.
    Memory tips

    "Ticket to play, reason to win." Threshold is the ticket. Distinctive is the reason.

    Core competence tests: C, D, E. Customer value, Different from rivals, Extendable.

    Step 2: Real business examples
    Honda

    Honda's classic core competence is engine design and manufacture. It is valued by customers, hard to match, and extends across motorcycles, cars, lawnmowers and generators. Notice that the competence sits underneath many products. The motorcycle is not the competence.

    Budget airlines

    Safety, a licence, trained pilots and online booking are threshold capabilities. No traveller chooses an airline because it has a licence. A low-cost airline's distinctive capability is the system it uses to turn aircraft around quickly and fly them for more hours each day.

    Public hospital

    Clinical licences, infection control and qualified staff are threshold capabilities. Patients and funders expect them. A distinctive capability might be a stroke team that treats patients faster than any other in the region, because paramedics, scanners and doctors work as one pathway.

    Step 3: How it may appear in the exam
    • The chief executive claims something is "our competitive advantage". You must test the claim.
    • You are asked to evaluate the organisation's strategic capabilities before a new strategy.
    • An acquisition is proposed, and you must assess whether the target brings a real core competence.
    • Outsourcing is proposed. Is the activity threshold (may be outsourced) or core (should be kept)?
    Step 4: A model exam answer
    Task (8 marks + professional skills)

    Harbourview Hospital is a fictional 240-bed private hospital with four rivals in its city. All five hold the national licence and international accreditation. In 2024 Harbourview spent $6m on the region's only hybrid cardiac theatre, and a rival has ordered the same equipment for next year. Its cardiac team has worked together for 11 years. Its 30-day cardiac readmission rate is 3.1%, against a city average of 5.8%. Nurse turnover is 24%, against 16% in the city. The CEO says: "Our new theatre is our competitive advantage."

    Task: Evaluate Harbourview's strategic capabilities and advise the board on whether the CEO's view is correct.

    Distinction-level answer

    Conclusion. The theatre is a temporary advantage only. Harbourview's real advantage is its cardiac team's clinical outcomes, and high nurse turnover is putting that at risk. [1]

    Threshold capabilities. The licence and accreditation are shared by all five hospitals, so they allow Harbourview to compete but give no reason for a patient or insurer to choose it. They must be protected, not promoted. [1]

    Hybrid cardiac theatre: a unique resource for now. It is the only one in the region today, but it was bought rather than built. A rival has already ordered one, so the advantage may last about a year. Any competitor with $6m can buy the same machine. [2]

    Cardiac team: the core competence. Readmissions are 3.1% against 5.8%, almost half the city rate. This matters directly to patients and to insurers, who pay for repeat admissions. Eleven years of teamwork cannot be bought with the theatre, so rivals would find it hard to copy. It could also extend into other cardiac services. [2]

    Weakness. Nurse turnover of 24% is 8 points above the city average. The competence depends on the whole team, so losing experienced nurses may damage the outcomes that set Harbourview apart. [1]

    Advice. The board should market outcomes rather than equipment, put retention plans in place for the cardiac team, and investigate nurse turnover. Before making public claims, it should confirm that the readmission data is adjusted for how ill patients were, because a healthier patient mix could explain part of the gap. [1]

    Step 5: What the examiner wants
    • A verdict on each capability, not a definition of it.
    • Evidence from the case, ideally a number compared with something.
    • A polite challenge to the CEO's claim, which earns scepticism marks.
    • An explanation of why rivals can or cannot copy it.
    Step 6: Common mistakes
    Avoid these
    • Calling everything positive a core competence. "Good staff" is usually a threshold resource.
    • Treating something expensive as unique. If money can buy it, a rival can buy it too.
    • Naming a product as a competence. The product is the output, and the competence is the ability behind it.
    • Forgetting to say that threshold capabilities still matter. Lose one and you may be out of the market.
    Step 7: Exam shortcut
    Three questions for any capability
    1. Do all serious rivals have it? If yes, it is threshold.
    2. Do customers care? If no, it is not an advantage, however impressive it sounds.
    3. Could a rival get it within a year or two? If yes, it is a temporary advantage.

    4Finding capabilities in a case

    Syllabus C4a. Three practical tools.

    Tool 1: A resource audit

    A resource audit is a structured review of what the organisation has and how well it uses it. Some study texts use the "M" checklist. Use it to scan the exhibits so that you do not miss an area.

    Checklist itemQuestion to ask
    Men (people)How many, how skilled, how old, how loyal?
    MachineryHow old, how reliable, how fully used?
    MaterialsHow secure is supply? What is the quality?
    MoneyHow much cash and debt? How much can it raise?
    MarketsWhich customers, brands and market positions does it hold?
    MethodsHow good are its processes?
    ManagementIs leadership capable and stable?
    Management informationDoes it have reliable data for decisions?
    Make-upDo structure and culture help or hinder?
    Quantity is not capability

    A resource is worth nothing until you ask how well it is used. A hospital with 40 operating theatres used 50% of the time may be weaker than one with 25 theatres used 90% of the time.

    Tool 2: The value chain

    You studied this in the previous chapter. It helps here because it shows where a capability sits. Move along the chain and ask which activities customers really pay for. For Varenne, the distinctive work sits in final assembly and hand-finishing, with support from technology development in the archive. Logistics is merely threshold.

    Linking to outsourcing

    This gives you a simple rule for the value networks topic. Consider outsourcing threshold activities if a partner can do them better or cheaper. Keep core competences in-house, because outsourcing one can hand your advantage to a future rival.

    Tool 3: Benchmarking

    Benchmarking compares performance against a reference point. A strength only exists in comparison with something, so this is the proof behind your S and W.

    Compare againstExampleWatch out for
    Your own pastThis year's margin against last year'sYou may improve while rivals improve faster
    Your industryYour hospital's waiting time against the sector averageThe entire sector may be poor
    Best in classA hospital learns patient handover from an airline's crew briefingNeed to adapt it, not simply copy it
    Limits of benchmarking

    It shows that a gap exists, but not why. It can also encourage copying rivals, which leads to being the same rather than being better. Check that you are comparing like with like.

    5Reading capability from the numbers

    Syllabus C4a and C4d, supported by the analysis professional skill

    Numbers turn "we are good" into proof. In the exam, a few simple calculations can lift an average answer into a strong one.

    The formulas you need

    MeasureFormulaWhat it tells you
    Gross marginGross profit ÷ revenueHow much is kept after direct costs. Shows pricing power and production cost.
    Operating marginOperating profit ÷ revenueHow much is kept after all running costs.
    Return on capital employed (ROCE)Operating profit ÷ capital employedHow well all the money invested is being used.
    Inventory daysInventory ÷ cost of sales × 365How long stock sits before it is sold.

    Worked example: Varenne against the sector

    Varenne has revenue of $60m and capital employed of $80m.

    MeasureVarenneSectorSara's first reaction
    Revenue growth a year2%7%Weak: losing market share
    Gross margin62%55%Strong: customers pay for the craft
    Operating margin12%17%Weak: something is eating the profit
    ROCE9%14%Weak: capital is working poorly
    Inventory days310220Weak: too much cash in stock
    Online share of sales6%24%Weak: a capability gap
    Master watchmaker turnover3%9%Strong: people stay
    Average age of master watchmakers5746Hidden danger: retirement

    Finding the story

    Varenne has a higher gross margin but a lower operating margin. So the problem is not the workshop. It lies in the costs between those two lines.

    CalculationVarenneAt sector rate
    Overheads as % of revenue (gross margin less operating margin)50%38%
    Overheads on $60m revenue$30.0m$22.8m
    Possible saving$7.2m a year

    Operating profit today is 12% × $60m = $7.2m. So closing the overhead gap could, in theory, double operating profit.

    Now look at inventory. Cost of sales is 38% × $60m = $22.8m.

    CalculationInventory
    Today: $22.8m × 310 ÷ 365$19.4m
    At sector level: $22.8m × 220 ÷ 365$13.7m
    Cash that could be released$5.6m
    How Sara writes it up

    "Varenne's 62% gross margin, 7 points above the sector, shows that customers pay a premium for its craftsmanship. This is a real strength. However, overheads take 50% of revenue against 38% for the sector, about $7.2m a year, so the advantage does not reach the bottom line. We should find out where this excess sits before committing to any expensive new strategy."

    Now apply scepticism

    Marc asks Sara to question her own numbers:

    • Is "the sector" a fair comparison? If it includes large volume groups, their scale will flatter their overheads.
    • Is some extra inventory deliberate? Complicated movements take years to build, and aged stock may hold its value.
    • Could low ROCE reflect property valued at today's prices, rather than poor management?
    • The 3% turnover looks good, but is it loyalty, or people staying until retirement at 57 and above?

    These questions earn scepticism marks. Do not simply accept a figure because it appears in an exhibit.

    Memory tip

    Use Number, Compare, Meaning, Action: state the figure, compare it, explain what it means, and say what to do. "Revenue is $60m" scores nothing on its own.

    6VRIN and lasting advantage

    Syllabus C4b

    Step 1: In simple language

    An advantage only lasts if rivals cannot copy it or work around it. VRIN is a four-question test of how long an advantage will last.

    1. V: Valuable? Does it help win customers or reduce cost?
      If no: a competitive disadvantage or a waste of money.
    2. R: Rare? Do only a few organisations have it?
      If no: competitive parity. You are level with rivals.
    3. I: Inimitable? Is it hard to copy?
      If no: a temporary advantage until rivals catch up.
    4. N: Non-substitutable? Is there no different way to deliver the same benefit?
      If no: at risk from alternatives. If yes: a sustained advantage.
    VRIN or VRIO?

    Some textbooks swap N for O: Organisational support. This asks whether the organisation is set up to use the capability. You can raise this point in an answer whichever version you use: a great asset is wasted if culture, systems or structure get in its way.

    Why some things are hard to copy

    BarrierPlain meaningExample
    ComplexityMany activities link together, so copying one part achieves littleA rival can buy IKEA's furniture designs, but not its whole connected system
    Causal ambiguityNobody can tell exactly what causes the success, sometimes even the firm itselfToyota opened its factories to visitors, yet rivals struggled to match its results
    Culture and historyIt was built over many years and cannot be bought or rushedA 200-year reputation, or a team that has worked together for a decade
    Memory tips

    "Very Rare Items Never" get copied gives you V, R, I, N.

    The barriers are the 3 Cs: Complexity, Causal ambiguity, Culture and history.

    Sara tests Varenne's capabilities

    CapabilityVRINVerdict
    Precision machinesYesNo––Parity (threshold)
    Patented date mechanism, expiring in 3 yearsYesYesNo, soon–Temporary advantage
    Brand and archiveYesYesYesMostlySustained among collectors, but young buyers may pick other status symbols
    Hand-finishing skillYesYesYesYesSustained, if the skill survives retirement

    Try it: run the VRIN test

    Pick any capability, perhaps one from your last practice case, and answer honestly.

    Valuable to customers or cost?

    Rare among rivals?

    Hard to copy?

    Hard to substitute?

    Answer the questions in order to see the verdict.

    Step 2: Real business examples
    Apple

    Rivals can match a phone's screen or camera, so hardware alone gives a short-lived lead. Apple's advantage comes from the way its devices, software, services and brand connect. Customers who have invested in the ecosystem face a cost if they switch. The connections are harder to copy than any single product.

    Rolex

    Rolex is known for doing much of its work in-house, including producing its own gold alloys. Together with a very strong brand, this control over quality is difficult to imitate. Many watch brands can buy good components, but few can match the combination.

    Narayana Health

    This Indian hospital group is known for delivering heart surgery in high volumes at much lower cost than many hospitals, by applying standard processes and specialisation. A rival can buy the same equipment, but matching the whole system of volumes, routines and culture is much harder.

    Step 3: How it may appear in the exam
    • "Assess whether the company's competitive advantage is sustainable."
    • A rival is copying the company's main product, or a patent is about to expire.
    • A new technology threatens to substitute for a skill the organisation relies on.
    • The company is about to invest in a capability. Will it last long enough to pay back?
    Step 4: A model exam answer
    Task (8 marks + professional skills)

    Lumio is a fictional cloud payroll provider with 14,000 small business customers. The founder tells investors that its "AI payroll engine" gives it an unbeatable lead. The engine is built on publicly available AI models. Lumio also holds 9 years of anonymised payroll error data, which no rival has at this scale. Moving to another provider takes a customer about 4 months. Annual customer loss is 5%, against a sector average of 14%. Most of its 6 senior developers are approached regularly by rivals.

    Task: Evaluate whether Lumio's competitive advantage is sustainable, as a note to the investor committee.

    Distinction-level answer

    Overall view. Lumio has a sustainable advantage, but not for the reason its founder gives. The lasting sources are its data and customer lock-in, not its AI engine. [1]

    AI engine: temporary at best. It is valuable, but it is built on public models that any rival can access. It is neither rare nor hard to copy, so investors should not treat it as a lasting barrier. The word "unbeatable" is not supported by this evidence. [2]

    Payroll error data: the strongest asset. Nine years of data are valuable because they improve accuracy, and rare because no rival has this scale. Time is the barrier: a new entrant cannot buy history. Its value may fall if rivals gain similar data through partnerships, so this should be monitored. [2]

    Customer lock-in: strong but needs care. A four-month move is a real switching cost, and 5% customer loss against 14% suggests that customers stay. However, we should find out whether they stay because they are satisfied or because leaving is painful. If it is the latter, a rival offering a free migration could expose the weakness. [2]

    Key risk and recommendation. Six senior developers are a small base and could be lost to rivals. Before investing, the committee should ask Lumio to show retention plans, confirm its rights to use the data, and measure satisfaction separately from retention. [1]

    Step 5: What the examiner wants
    • Apply the test to specific capabilities. Do not write about VRIN in general.
    • Give a clear rating, such as temporary or sustained, with your reasons.
    • Say what could erode the advantage and what the organisation should do.
    Step 6: Common mistakes
    Avoid these
    • Explaining all four letters without applying them. The marks are for application.
    • Forgetting substitutes. A rival need not copy you if it can offer the same benefit another way.
    • Assuming any patent gives a lasting advantage. Always check how long it has left.
    • Saying an advantage is sustained for ever. "For now, unless…" is better judgement.
    Step 7: Exam shortcut
    Use one sentence per capability

    "[Capability] gives a [parity / temporary / sustained] advantage because [evidence]. It could be eroded by [threat], so we should [action]."

    7Staying ahead

    Syllabus C4b

    Even a strong capability ages. Two ideas explain why some organisations renew themselves and others do not.

    Core rigidities: when a strength becomes a trap

    The very capability that made a firm successful can stop it from changing. Staff, systems and culture all become built around it. This is the internal side of the strategic drift you already know.

    Kodak and Varenne

    Kodak was outstanding at chemical film, and an engineer there built an early digital camera in 1975. But its profits, skills and culture were tied to film, so it failed to lead the digital shift.

    Varenne has the same risk. Its mechanical skill is its pride, which is why Henri dismissed quartz in the 1970s and smartwatches today.

    Dynamic capabilities: the ability to change capabilities

    Dynamic capability

    An organisation's ability to renew and recreate its strategic capabilities as its environment changes.

    StageQuestionVarenne could
    SenseWhat is changing?Track resale prices and younger buyers' behaviour, rather than rely on the chairman's opinion
    SeizeHow do we act on it?Run a small trial of certified second-hand sales
    ReconfigureHow do we rebuild ourselves?Move money and people from wholesale towards online and direct sales
    Netflix

    Netflix moved from posting DVDs to streaming, then into making its own shows. Each move changed what the company needed to be good at. Its lasting strength has been this ability to shift, rather than any one service.

    Sustaining a cost advantage

    Not all advantages come from uniqueness. Low cost can also be a strategic capability. It usually comes from:

    • Economies of scale: high fixed costs spread across high volumes.
    • Supply costs: a better location, or buying power.
    • Product and process design: simpler products and better processes.
    • Experience: costs fall as people repeat tasks and learn.

    A cost advantage must be lower than rivals' costs for a reason they cannot easily copy. Cheap labour alone rarely lasts, because anyone can move to it.

    A simple plan for sustaining advantage

    1. Protect it: patents, contracts, retention of key people.
    2. Deepen it: keep investing so that rivals chase a moving target.
    3. Connect it: link activities so that copying one part is not enough.
    4. Renew it: use dynamic capabilities before the market forces you to.
    Memory tip

    "Sense, Seize, Reconfigure" for change. "Protect, Deepen, Connect, Renew" for your recommendation section.

    8Organisational knowledge

    Syllabus C4c

    Step 1: In simple language

    Organisational knowledge is what the organisation collectively knows from experience. It is often its hardest-to-copy asset, and it can walk out of the door.

    Data: raw facts›Information: facts with meaning›Knowledge: understanding how to act
    The difference

    Data: 37.9. Information: the patient's temperature is 37.9°C, up from 36.8°C at noon. Knowledge: an experienced nurse knows that this pattern after this operation suggests infection, and calls the doctor early.

    Explicit and tacit knowledge

    Explicit knowledge

    Can be written down and shared: manuals, procedures, databases, patents.

    Easy to store, but also easy for rivals to copy or steal.

    Tacit knowledge

    Lives in people's heads and hands: judgement, feel, know-how, gained from experience.

    Hard to share, which makes it hard to copy and a source of advantage.

    Riding a bicycle

    You can read a manual about riding a bicycle (explicit), but you learn balance only by doing it (tacit). Varenne's masters can feel when a tiny spring is correctly tensioned. No drawing fully captures that.

    How knowledge moves: Nonaka's SECI model

    Socialisation (tacit to tacit)

    Learning by working beside someone. An apprentice sits next to a master.

    Externalisation (tacit to explicit)

    Putting know-how into words or video. A master explains and films each step.

    Combination (explicit to explicit)

    Joining documents to create something new. Merging repair records with design files.

    Internalisation (explicit to tacit)

    Learning by doing until the guide is no longer needed. A new watchmaker practises until it is second nature.

    Memory tips

    "Some Experts Can Inform" gives you Socialisation, Externalisation, Combination, Internalisation.

    Tacit is the iceberg under the water. Manuals show only the tip.

    Why knowledge matters to strategy

    • Advantage: tacit knowledge is hard to copy, so it supports core competences.
    • Efficiency: people stop repeating mistakes and reinventing solutions.
    • Innovation: new ideas appear when knowledge from different groups combines.
    • Continuity: the organisation survives when individuals leave.

    Practical ways to manage knowledge

    MethodHelps withLimitation
    Mentoring and apprenticeshipsTacit knowledgeSlow and expensive
    Communities of practiceSharing tacit knowledge across departmentsNeed management time and support
    Knowledge systems and shared databasesExplicit knowledgeBecome out of date if nobody owns them
    Video, exit interviewsCapturing some tacit knowledgeCapture only part of it
    AI assistants trained on internal documentsFinding explicit knowledge quicklyQuality depends on the data, and confidential data must be protected
    Rewards for sharingFixing a culture of "knowledge is power"Needs a change of culture, not just a bonus

    Worked example: Varenne's retirement cliff

    Sara finds that 14 of Varenne's 30 master watchmakers will retire within 5 years. These 30 produce the complicated watch line, with $18m of annual revenue. Training a master takes 7 years, and an apprentice costs $45,000 a year.

    CalculationResult
    Share of masters leaving: 14 ÷ 3047%
    Revenue at risk: 47% × $18m$8.4m a year
    14 apprentices at $45,000 a year$0.63m a year
    Total training cost: $0.63m × 7 years$4.41m
    Timing gap: 7-year training less 5 years to retirement2 years

    Sara spots something a list of ideas would miss. Even if apprentices start today, they will not be ready when the masters leave. So Varenne must also keep some masters on part-time as teachers for around two more years. The money is small compared with the risk, while the timing is the real problem.

    Step 2: Real business examples
    The bread machine (Nonaka's famous case)

    Matsushita's engineers could not make a home bread machine work. A software developer trained with the head baker at a top Osaka hotel, and noticed that he twisted the dough as he stretched it. She turned this feel into a design instruction, and the machine worked. That is tacit knowledge becoming explicit, then becoming a product.

    Stradivarius violins

    Three centuries on, people still debate how Stradivari's workshop produced its violins. Much of what his workshop knew was carried in people, not written down. Knowledge that is not passed on can be lost for good.

    Step 3: How it may appear in the exam
    • Experienced staff retire, or a key person leaves.
    • Two merging companies must combine their expertise.
    • Departments work in silos, and teams keep repeating one another's mistakes.
    • A company proposes to replace experts with AI or outsource their work.
    • Staff refuse to share because "my knowledge is my job security".
    Step 4: A model exam answer
    Task (8 marks + professional skills)

    Northbank Water Authority is a fictional public body supplying a city of 1.2 million people. Of its 60 senior network engineers, 25 will retire within 4 years. Records of the older pipe network are incomplete, so engineers rely on memory to locate problems. Average repair time is 5 hours, against a 3-hour regulatory target that is met only by the senior engineers. The director of operations suggests "writing everything in a manual before they go". Budgets are tight and taxpayers expect value for money.

    Task: Write a briefing note to the board that explains why this knowledge matters and recommends how to protect it.

    Distinction-level answer

    Key message. Northbank is about to lose 42% of its senior engineers (25 of 60), along with knowledge that exists nowhere else. A manual is needed, but it will not solve the problem alone. [1]

    Why it matters. Knowing where old pipes run and how they fail is tacit knowledge built up over decades. It lets senior staff meet the 3-hour target, so losing it may lengthen repairs, breach regulatory targets and leave homes without water. Since Northbank has no competitors, the concern is its public duty and value for money rather than market advantage. [2]

    Assessment of the manual. It would capture some explicit facts at low cost, but an engineer's judgement cannot be fully written down. We should use it as one part of a wider plan. [1]

    Recommendations.

    1. Pair each retiring engineer with a successor on live repairs, starting with the most critical areas. This transfers tacit knowledge, and it uses existing staff time. [1]
    2. Use site visits with tablets to map old pipes as engineers walk them, turning memory into a digital asset that lasts. [1]
    3. Offer some retirees a part-time return to mentor during peak periods. This buys time at modest cost. [1]
    4. Report progress to the board using the share of the network mapped and the repair times of newer engineers. [1]
    Step 5: What the examiner wants
    • Use tacit and explicit correctly, tied to the people in the case.
    • Link knowledge to the organisation's objectives, which may be public duty rather than profit.
    • Recommend practical steps that fit the budget, the people and the deadline.
    • Recognise that culture often blocks sharing, not technology.
    Step 6: Common mistakes
    Avoid these
    • Recommending only an IT system. Technology stores explicit knowledge, but tacit knowledge needs people.
    • Confusing information with knowledge.
    • Assuming all knowledge should be documented. Writing down the crown jewels can make them easier for a leaver to take.
    • Ignoring why people may not share, such as fear of losing their jobs.
    Step 7: Exam shortcut
    Three questions for any knowledge issue
    1. Who holds it, and when might it leave?
    2. What type is it: tacit or explicit?
    3. How do we transfer it: people for tacit, systems for explicit?

    9SWOT and TOWS

    Syllabus C4d

    Step 1: In simple language

    SWOT brings the outside and inside analysis together on one page, so that leaders can see what matters most.

    Strengths (internal, helpful)

    What we do better than rivals, with proof.

    Weaknesses (internal, harmful)

    What holds us back, or where rivals do better.

    Opportunities (external, helpful)

    Changes in the outside world that we could benefit from.

    Threats (external, harmful)

    Changes in the outside world that could hurt us.

    The control test

    Ask: "Could the organisation change this directly?" If yes, it is internal, so it is an S or a W. If it would still exist had the organisation never been created, it is external, so it is an O or a T.

    The biggest SWOT error

    An opportunity is a change in the world, not something you plan to do. "Open a boutique in Singapore" is an action. The opportunity is "growing demand for luxury goods among Singapore's wealthy buyers". If your opportunity starts with a verb, rewrite it.

    A good SWOT point has five qualities

    1. Specific: not "good brand", but "brand ranked first by 70% of collectors".
    2. Evidenced: drawn from the exhibits, preferably with a number.
    3. Relative: better or worse than rivals. A strength only counts by comparison.
    4. Prioritised: the most important first, with 3 or 4 per box.
    5. Interpreted: it says why this matters and what follows.

    Sara's SWOT for Varenne

    Strengths

    • Gross margin 62% against 55%: customers pay for craft
    • Hand-finishing skill is rare and hard to copy
    • Design archive and 200-year brand

    Weaknesses

    • 47% of masters retire within 5 years
    • Overheads 50% of revenue against 38%
    • Online sales 6% against 24%
    • Half of sales rely on a few retail groups

    Opportunities

    • Growing resale market for luxury watches
    • Younger buyers want proof of authenticity online
    • Demand for traceable, ethical gold

    Threats

    • Smartwatches reach tomorrow's buyers first
    • Key movement part relies on one supplier
    • Tariffs in the US and China
    • Strong franc makes exports more expensive
    A point can sit in two boxes

    The brand strength depends on who you ask. A two-century heritage is a strength for collectors, but it may feel old-fashioned to younger buyers. Noting this shows real judgement.

    Moving from SWOT to action: TOWS

    A SWOT is a description. On its own, it does not tell leaders what to do. TOWS crosses the boxes to create possible strategies.

    SO: use strengths to seize opportunities

    Launch a certified second-hand service, using the archive to prove authenticity.

    WO: fix weaknesses to reach opportunities

    Build online capability to reach younger buyers and reduce dependence on retailers.

    ST: use strengths to reduce threats

    Use craft skill to make the key movement part in-house.

    WT: reduce weaknesses and avoid threats

    Start apprenticeships and cut overheads to protect the firm in a downturn.

    Memory tips

    S and W are "we". O and T are "world".

    For TOWS, remember use, seize, fix, defend.

    Try it: sort these ten statements

    Choose where each one belongs. Watch for items that are not SWOT points at all.

    Step 2: Real business example
    A public library service

    SWOT also works outside business. A city library service has a trusted brand and buildings in every district (S), but ageing systems and falling visitor numbers (W). Government plans for digital skills training (O) sit alongside budget cuts and online alternatives (T). A TOWS action: use trusted local buildings to become the place residents go for digital skills help, funded by that programme. Here SWOT supports public value rather than profit.

    Step 3: How it may appear in the exam
    • "Prepare a SWOT analysis for the board." Often part of a briefing paper or a presentation slide.
    • You are given several exhibits and must pull out only what matters.
    • You may be asked to use the SWOT to recommend or rank a strategic option.
    • In a slide task, keep it short. Each box needs a few clear bullets with speaker notes that explain them.
    Step 4: A model exam answer
    Task (8 marks + professional skills)

    Sunfield Juices is a fictional drinks company. Revenue is $200m, up 1% on last year, with no-added-sugar lines growing 18%. Its brand is the market leader, with 34% share against 21% for the nearest rival. One supermarket group accounts for 41% of sales. Its bottling plant is 26 years old and has 11% downtime against 4% for the sector. A sugar tax takes effect in 18 months, and would cover 60% of current products. Younger consumers are moving towards functional drinks.

    Task: Prepare a prioritised SWOT for the board and state the single most urgent issue.

    Distinction-level answer
    BoxPrioritised points, with the reason each one matters
    Strengths1. Market share of 34% against 21%: gives leverage with retailers and scale for marketing.
    2. No-added-sugar lines growing 18%: an existing platform for the shift in demand, not a start from zero.
    Weaknesses1. 60% of the range would be taxed, and most of the portfolio is in the wrong place.
    2. One customer is 41% of sales: it can squeeze margins, and losing it would remove $82m.
    3. Plant downtime of 11% against 4%: raises unit costs and may limit reformulated production.
    Opportunities1. Consumers moving to no-added-sugar and functional drinks.
    2. Tax may weaken smaller rivals who cannot afford to reformulate.
    Threats1. Sugar tax in 18 months: a fixed deadline.
    2. Supermarket own-brand or rival launches may target health-conscious shoppers.

    [6] for evidenced, correctly classified, prioritised points.

    Most urgent issue. The sugar tax is the priority. It affects 60% of products and has a fixed 18-month deadline. Overall revenue growth of only 1% hides a core range that is likely to be declining, and the board should ask to see sales split by product line. We should use the brand strength to speed up reformulation, and assess whether the old plant can handle the changes in time. [2]

    Step 5: What the examiner wants
    • Correct classification: internal against external.
    • Only points that matter, each drawn from the case.
    • Visible prioritisation, plus a conclusion on what the SWOT means overall.
    • Links between points. For example, the tax (T) hits a weakness (W) that makes it worse.
    Step 6: Common mistakes
    Avoid these
    • Writing a long list, with no ranking and no "so what".
    • Putting actions in the Opportunities box.
    • Putting external trends in Strengths, such as "the market is growing".
    • Claiming a strength with no comparison. "We have 100 stores" says nothing alone.
    • Stopping at the four boxes, with no overall conclusion.
    Step 7: Exam shortcut
    The 3-3-1 rule

    Aim for about 3 strong points per box, each in about 3 parts (fact, comparison, so what), then finish with 1 overall conclusion.

    Open after your attempt: distinction-level answer
    Briefing paper to the board of Northway Couriers

    Key message. Northway's advantage lies in Northway Cold, not in its routing software. Cold earns 48% of group profit from 22% of revenue, in a market growing 9% a year. That position has about 14 months to be secured, because a key contract expires and a global rival is seeking a licence.

    (a) Strategic capabilities

    Parcels: threshold only. Vans, drivers and on-time delivery are the basics of the industry. Parcels' 94.2% on-time rate is level with the sector's 94.0%, so it provides parity and no advantage. Its 4.2% margin is well below the 7.0% sector average. With market growth of 2%, it should be run for efficiency rather than treated as a source of advantage. [2]

    Medicine licence: unique for now. Only three firms hold one, so it is valuable and rare. But a licence is an entry requirement rather than a permanent wall. The global courier's application shows it can be obtained, so this advantage is shrinking. [2]

    Cold chain delivery: the core competence. On-time delivery of 99.2% is 5 points above the sector, and it matters greatly when late medicines can spoil or put patients at risk. A 13.6% margin suggests that customers pay for this reliability. It rests on licensed processes, trained people and relationships, which together are harder to copy than any one part, so it is the strongest candidate for lasting advantage. [2]

    Routing software: challenge to the chief executive's view. The evidence does not support "nobody can copy it". Parcels uses the same software, I assume, yet performs only at sector level. If so, the software cannot on its own explain why Northway wins. A rival has also bought an alternative off the shelf, which provides a substitute. The software may be valuable, but it fails the non-substitutability test. It is also a weakness in one respect: the know-how sits with two people and is poorly documented, so it is a key-person risk. We should confirm how much the software actually contributes, and that Northway legally owns the code. [2]

    Overall. The advantage is real but only partly sustainable. Driver turnover of 31% against 24% may threaten the reliability on which Cold depends. [1]

    (c) Recommendations for the next 12 months

    Priority 1: secure Northway Cold. Open early renewal talks with PharmaLink now, aiming for a multi-year contract before the global courier has a licence. Add new customers from the growing home-delivery market to reduce reliance on one contract. [1]

    Vans. The $2.4m replacement is needed, but it can be phased towards 2028. Start with vans used by Cold, and explore leasing, so that 60% of cash is not used at once while contract talks are underway.

    • Scepticism: testing the chief executive's claim, and stating the assumption about Parcels using the software.
    • Commercial acumen: seeing the 14-month window, the competitor's timing and the need to protect cash.

    12One-page cheat sheet

    Vocabulary

    • Resources: what we have
    • Competences: what we do well
    • Physical, Financial, Human, Intellectual

    Threshold or distinctive

    • Threshold: ticket to play
    • Unique resources and core competences: reason to win
    • Tests: Customer value, Different, Extendable

    Lasting advantage

    • VRIN: Valuable, Rare, Inimitable, Non-substitutable
    • 3 Cs: Complexity, Causal ambiguity, Culture and history
    • Risk: core rigidity
    • Renew: Sense, Seize, Reconfigure

    Knowledge

    • Data, information, knowledge
    • Explicit: can be written down
    • Tacit: in heads and hands
    • SECI: Socialisation, Externalisation, Combination, Internalisation

    Diagnosing

    • Resource audit: the M checklist
    • Value chain: where is it?
    • Benchmarking: past, industry, best in class
    • Number, Compare, Meaning, Action

    SWOT and TOWS

    • S and W are "we"; O and T are "world"
    • Opportunities are not actions
    • 3-3-1 rule
    • TOWS: SO, WO, ST, WT
    The sentence that earns marks

    "[Capability] is [threshold / temporary / sustained] because [evidence], so we should [action]."
